Writing Investment Goals and Constraints

Define the goal, amount, horizon, liquidity and risk capacity before discussing asset allocation; a goal statement is not a return promise.

Problem and output

“I want my money to grow” is not an executable investment goal. It has no amount, date, purpose, priority or risk boundary, so it cannot show whether a portfolio is on track. The output of this method is a checkable goals-and-constraints statement, a short investment policy: why the money is invested, how much is needed, when it will be used, how much loss can be borne, which limits must not be violated, and when the plan will be reviewed. It is not a product recommendation or a return guarantee.

Inputs and data requirements

  • Goal and purpose: education, retirement, a home, emergency reserves; record them separately.
  • Amount and date: target amount, target date, current assets, planned contributions.
  • Priority: essential, important, optional, and how conflicts are resolved.
  • Liquidity: when cash is needed and whether the need can be postponed.
  • Risk capacity: how much loss the financial situation can absorb.
  • Risk willingness: how much volatility the investor is prepared to tolerate.
  • Constraints: tax, legal, employer plan, concentrated holdings, liquidity, ethical or other preferences.
  • Review: when and why the plan will be reassessed.

The SEC publication "Beginners' Guide to Asset Allocation, Diversification, and Rebalancing" states the frame outright: "The asset allocation that works best for you at any given point in your life will depend largely on your time horizon and your ability to tolerate risk." It defines the horizon as "Your time horizon is the expected number of months, years, or decades you will be investing to achieve a particular financial goal." and rebalancing as "Rebalancing is bringing your portfolio back to your original asset allocation mix." The eight-item input list below weaves these together with goals, amounts, priority, liquidity, constraints and review; that arrangement is this article's own[1].

Assumptions

The feasibility calculation below rests on a deliberately simple set of assumptions; change any one of them and the answer has to be recomputed:

  • amounts are in Chinese yuan (CNY), and target and contributions use the same currency with no exchange-rate conversion;
  • contributions are made at the end of each year, compounded annually, with the compounding period matching the contribution period;
  • the annual contribution stays fixed at a nominal CNY 15,000 and is not indexed to inflation or income growth;
  • trading costs, management fees, taxes and tax deferral are ignored;
  • the nominal return is constant inside each scenario — a scenario runs one assumption through to the end and is not a market forecast.

Steps

  1. Write the goal, not the product. Start with “how much is needed, by when, for what,” not “buy this asset.”
  2. Separate nominal from real. State whether the target is in today’s purchasing power or future nominal money, and record the inflation assumption.
  3. Confirm horizon and liquidity. Money needed soon should not carry long-horizon volatility; keep emergency reserves separate.
  4. Assess capacity and willingness separately. The SEC guide defines risk tolerance as "Risk tolerance is your ability and willingness to lose some or all of your original investment in exchange for greater potential returns." - capacity and willingness inside one phrase - and adds that "An investor with a longer time horizon may feel more comfortable taking on a riskier, or more volatile, investment because he or she can wait out slow economic cycles and the inevitable ups and downs of our markets." Splitting them into two separate judgements is this article's refinement. Capacity reflects stable income, debt, reserves and loss absorption; willingness reflects sleep, experience and temperament. Where they conflict, adopt the more conservative constraint and say why[1].
  5. Run feasibility scenarios. Calculate gaps under different returns, savings and inflation assumptions; treat returns as scenarios, not forecasts.
  6. List constraints. Tax, legal, account type, concentration limits, prohibited products or sustainability preferences; make each constraint executable and checkable.
  7. Write the policy. Goal, allocation ranges, rebalancing rules, prohibited actions, review triggers and decision responsibility.
  8. Stress-test. Would the plan remain executable if returns are lower, a large expense occurs, or income falls?

Example

Suppose the goal is CNY 300,000 in today’s purchasing power in 10 years for education; current assets are CNY 100,000; contributions of CNY 15,000 are made at each year end; and inflation is assumed at 2.5%. The nominal target is approximately:

300,000 × 1.025¹⁰ ≈ 384,025 CNY.

Under a nominal annual return r, the value after 10 years is the compounded value of the opening balance plus the future value of an ordinary annuity:

FV = 100,000 × (1 + r)¹⁰ + 15,000 × ((1 + r)¹⁰ − 1) / r
Nominal annual return r Future value (CNY) Gap to 384,025 (CNY)
4% 328,116 55,909
5% 351,558 32,468
6% 376,797 7,229

Future values and gaps are computed unrounded and then rounded to whole yuan, so subtracting the two rounded figures can differ by CNY 1 (the exact gap in the 5% scenario is 32,467.51). These returns are scenario assumptions, not promises; actual results depend on returns, fees, taxes and inflation.

The adjustable variables are higher contributions, a lower target, a longer horizon, a greater tolerance for volatility (which does not guarantee higher returns), or re-prioritising the goals. The choice depends on constraints and risk capacity, not the gap number alone.

FINRA's “For Investors” page frames getting started the same way: “Investing is a vital step toward achieving your financial goals, whether you’re saving for college, planning for retirement or building long-term wealth.” It adds that “Understanding the basics—like balancing risk and return and knowing how fees impact your investments—can set you up for success.” and points to “differences between account and product types to finding and working with an investment professional.” The eight-item input list below follows those items; the division and ordering are this article's own[2].

Interpreting the result

The value of the statement is that it makes trade-offs explicit: higher expected returns usually require more volatility or longer lock-ups, while more certainty usually requires more saving, a lower target or a longer horizon. It cannot remove uncertainty or guarantee a return.

Failure conditions

  • The goal is too vague to judge success.
  • Expected return is treated as a promise, ignoring fees, taxes and inflation.
  • Risk willingness is confused with risk capacity, or vice versa.
  • Short-term liquidity, emergency reserves and debt are ignored.
  • Goals conflict without a priority order.
  • The portfolio is never reviewed after drifting, or the long-term plan is changed on every short-term swing.
  • Leverage or concentration is used to close a gap, magnifying losses the investor cannot bear.

Verification and reproduction

  1. Calculate gaps under at least three return scenarios and two savings scenarios.
  2. Include fees, taxes and inflation, then check how the result changes.
  3. Stress-test income loss, a market decline and a large expense.
  4. Record assumptions, sources and review dates so another person can reproduce the result.
  5. Update the written statement when goals or constraints change, not only the holdings.

Knowledge-point review

return objective

Definition: A return objective is the nominal or real return a portfolio aims for, consistent with risk, horizon and spending needs.

Mechanism: It can be absolute, benchmark-relative or liability-covering; expected return comes from allocation and risk premia, not a guarantee.

Example: For example, a retirement portfolio withdrawing 4 percent a year must cover spending and inflation, not just chase the highest past return.

Boundary: A return target is not a wish list; too high a target forces risk-taking, and fees, taxes and inflation erode returns that cannot be extrapolated.

risk tolerance and capacity

Definition: Risk tolerance is the psychological ability to accept volatility and losses; risk capacity is the financial ability to absorb them without derailing goals.

Mechanism: Capacity depends on cash flow, liabilities, horizon, liquidity and insurance, while willingness reflects personality, experience and markets; the lower binds.

Example: For example, a young household with stable income but a large mortgage may tolerate volatility psychologically yet have limited capacity.

Boundary: Risk profiling is not a one-off questionnaire; capacity changes with unemployment, debt and health, willingness falls in drawdowns, and appetite is not capacity.

investment horizon

Definition: An investment horizon is the time before funds are needed, shaping tolerable volatility, liquidity needs and compounding room.

Mechanism: Long-horizon money can bear more equity and illiquidity, short-horizon money must match spending, and mismatch forces sales at lows.

Example: For example, a down payment needed in three years should not be all in volatile stocks, while a 30-year retirement pool can hold more equity.

Boundary: Horizon is not just a calendar number; spending dates, liabilities and cash-flow uncertainty shorten effective horizon, and job loss can force early withdrawals.

investment constraints

Definition: Investment constraints are tax, legal, liquidity, ethical, concentration and liability limits that narrow the feasible portfolio.

Mechanism: They operate through account types, policy statements, exclusion lists and cash buffers; tighter constraints limit achievable return and diversification.

Example: For example, a pension faces liquidity and concentration limits, while a charity may exclude tobacco, alcohol or gambling.

Boundary: More constraints are not always safer; over-restriction reduces diversification, raises costs or forces foreign risk, and tax constraints can lock in poor assets.

investment policy and decision records

Definition: An investment policy statement states objectives, risk, allocation, rebalancing, constraints and governance; decision records capture the reasons and evidence for each trade.

Mechanism: Policy separates long-term principles from short-term emotion, while records support review, accountability and consistency; rebalancing and deviation rules limit discretionary trading.

Example: For example, a policy sets 60 percent equity and rebalances beyond a 5-point deviation, with records explaining the trigger.

Boundary: Policy is not a liability shield; stale assumptions, vague mandates and missing records defeat it, and discipline cannot replace ongoing fundamental review.

Data and institutional check: investment goals

Before using "investment goals" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns risk capacity.

Third, the institutional boundary. investment constraints is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: investment goals

Learning "investment goals" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "risk capacity": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "investment constraints" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of investment goals still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind risk capacity reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "investment goals" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "risk capacity" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Exercises

  1. Goal elements. The goal is 500,000 in today’s purchasing power in 15 years, with 150,000 current assets and 10,000 annual contributions. What else is needed to judge feasibility?
  2. Capacity versus willingness. One person has a stable job and ample reserves but is anxious about losses; another has unstable income but is eager to take risk. Who has more risk capacity, and why can’t a questionnaire alone decide?
  3. Return assumption. Why is “assume 8% annual return” not an investment plan by itself?
Suggested answers
  1. Inflation, fees and taxes, cash-flow needs, risk capacity, liquidity requirements, goal priority and a range of return and savings scenarios.
  2. Capacity depends on financial buffers and loss absorption; willingness is psychological and behavioral. Stable income and reserves raise capacity, while anxiety lowers willingness; unstable income can lower capacity even when willingness is high. Assess both and use the more conservative constraint.
  3. 8% is an assumption, not a guarantee; actual results depend on fees, taxes, inflation, timing and volatility. A plan also needs goals, horizon, savings, risk limits and review rules.
  1. In 100 words, state the core metric in "INV-001", including its denominator and unit.
  2. Write "investment goals" and "risk capacity" as observable variables and name the time gap between them.
  3. Find one rule change in "investment constraints" and explain how it changes sample coverage.
  4. Run a counterfactual with this article's method: change one variable and hold the rest fixed.
  5. If you switched to an independent source, which order of magnitude would move most, and why?
  6. Write one falsifiable claim with an observable signal, a waiting period and a review metric.
