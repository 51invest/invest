Performance Attribution: Where the Return Came From

Performance attribution splits portfolio-minus-benchmark returns into allocation, selection and interaction: right sectors, right stocks, and their combination. Without attribution, performance is a story; with it, you can judge whether it is repeatable.

Problem

A portfolio beats its benchmark by 2.5 percentage points: is it overweight in a strong sector, stock selection, or both? Performance attribution splits the total gap; otherwise “the manager is skilled” is an unfalsifiable story. Attribution also explains underperformance: wrong allocation or weak selection[1].

Inputs

Portfolio and benchmark sector weights, stock weights, sector and stock returns, cash and fees, period length. Factor attribution also needs factor returns and exposures. Data must be consistent: sector classification, return calculation (time-weighted or money-weighted) and fee treatment[2].

Assumptions

First, the benchmark represents the portfolio’s normal exposure; second, sector and stock returns add up; third, weights are stable or rebuilt daily/monthly; fourth, fees and cash drag are separated; fifth, residual is not skill but the unexplained model part[3].

Steps

Step 1, total excess: Rp−RbR_p - R_bRp​−Rb​. Step 2, allocation: (wp−wb)×Rb(w_p - w_b) \times R_b(wp​−wb​)×Rb​, summed across sectors. Step 3, selection: wb×(Rp,i−Rb,i)w_b \times (R_{p,i} - R_{b,i})wb​×(Rp,i​−Rb,i​). Step 4, interaction: (wp−wb)×(Rp,i−Rb,i)(w_p - w_b) \times (R_{p,i} - R_{b,i})(wp​−wb​)×(Rp,i​−Rb,i​). Step 5, sum and reconcile residual. Step 6, list fees, cash and FX separately.

Interpreting results

Suppose total excess is 2.5%, allocation 1.2%, selection 0.9%, interaction 0.4%. The manager overweighted strong sectors and picked better stocks within them. If allocation is negative and selection positive, stock picking is strong but sector bets are wrong. Attribution explains, it does not forecast.

Failure conditions

First, benchmark and portfolio styles differ too much; second, sector classification changes; third, weights change frequently without rebuilding; fourth, fees and cash are not separated; fifth, the sample is too short; sixth, residual is treated as alpha. Under these conditions, attribution can mislead.

Reproduction

Recompute the three effects with the same weights and returns, and check that they sum to total excess; test robustness with another benchmark and sector classification; reconcile fees, cash and FX item by item. Record data versions and calculation frequency.

Deeper note: multi-period linking, interaction and the limits of attribution

Single-period attribution adds up, but multi-period attribution cannot simply sum each period's effects. If the portfolio rises 20% then falls 10%, arithmetic addition gives 10% while geometric return is 1.2×0.9−1=8%; allocation, selection and interaction effects also distort as the compounding base changes. Multi-period attribution uses geometric linking or smoothing algorithms that reweight each period along the portfolio and benchmark compounding paths.

Interaction is not a residual. Allocation asks which sector was overweight, selection asks whether stocks were picked well within sectors, and interaction asks whether the overweight sectors happened to be the ones with strong selection. Interaction can be positive or negative: overweighting a strong sector with strong selection is positive; overweighting a strong sector with weak selection is negative. Assigning all interaction to 'stock-picking skill' overstates the manager; assigning it all to 'allocation luck' understates the manager.

Brinson attribution has three conventions: BHB reports interaction separately; Brinson-Fachler adjusts allocation by the benchmark sector return minus total benchmark return; Brinson 1986 folds interaction into selection. The numbers for allocation and selection change with the convention; state which one is used and never compare across conventions.

Attribution cannot prove causation. It explains where returns came from, not that the manager intended it. A post-hoc claim of 'I always liked that sector' cannot be tested by attribution; use holdings changes, trade records and prior memos.

Boundary: attribution depends on the benchmark and classification. A wrong benchmark, changing sector definitions or unreconstructed weights create fake allocation or selection; combine attribution with risk models, fee analysis and qualitative review, not as a standalone appraisal[1][2][3].

Attribution frequency must match the appraisal horizon. Daily attribution is dominated by single-day noise and suits trading review; monthly or quarterly suits appraisal but has fewer observations and weaker significance. Judging a manager on 12 quarters may leave standard errors so wide that skill and luck cannot be separated. Report frequency, sample length and confidence intervals.

Benchmark choice drives the conclusion. A broad index, a style index or the peer average gives different allocation and selection effects. If the benchmark holds assets the portfolio cannot buy (small caps, overseas markets), attribution creates fake 'allocation skill'. Match the benchmark to the portfolio's mandate, style and risk exposure, and fix it ex ante rather than choosing it after the fact.

Attribution should end in actionable questions. If allocation comes mainly from overweighting one sector, ask whether that was a research conclusion or accidental drift; if selection is negative, ask whether the stock logic failed or trading costs dragged. The value of attribution is the next question, not a manager score[2][3].

Attribution is not the only evidence. Cross-check it against holdings, trade logs, risk exposures and prior research; one attribution table cannot separate skill, style and luck. Compare several periods for the same manager; a single period cannot establish stable skill.

Working paper: a four-column check for "Performance Attribution: Where the Return Came From"

The subject of this article is "Performance attribution: where benefits come from". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "Allocation, selection and interaction". Column two states the transmission channel of "Brinson and factor attribution". Column three states the institutional boundary of "Benchmark, fees and residual". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Brinson and factor attribution" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. Benchmark, fees and residual is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.
