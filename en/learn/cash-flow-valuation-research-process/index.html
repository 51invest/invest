Cash-Flow Valuation: A Research Process

Estimate a value range from future free cash flow, a discount rate and a terminal value: rebuild base cash flow, discount each year, then sensitize; all example numbers are hypothetical.

By the end of this article, you can discount a company's forecast free cash flow into today's value and say how the terminal value, discount rate, and perpetual growth each move the estimate.

Problem and output

Cash-flow valuation uses the present-value result of discounted cash flows (DCF) to price the free cash a business can distribute in the future; the definitions and basic formulas for present value and discounting belong to the present value and discounted cash flows article (FIN-011) and are not repeated here — this article covers the applied process. It answers two questions: what does the current price imply, and what would I pay? The output is not a target price but a value range, a list of key assumptions, a sensitivity table and falsification conditions. It does not forecast short-term price moves, and it does not replace an understanding of the business model.

Inputs and data requirements

5 to 10 years of statements and notes; operating cash flow, capex, working capital, tax, debt, cash and diluted shares; documented sources for the discount rate and terminal growth; at least one conservative case. The discount rate should show its parts: risk-free rate, risk premium, cost of debt and weights. Use one currency and unit, align the price and statements to the same date, and use the latest diluted share count. Keep data gaps visible; do not fill them forward without a stated basis.

Assumptions

The example company is hypothetical, with all amounts in one hypothetical currency unit (no specific currency is intended): base free cash flow (FCF) of 100, five-year growth of 10%, 10%, 8%, 6% and 4%, terminal growth of 2.5%, and a discount rate of 9%; net debt is 200 (debt 400 less cash 200), with 100 shares. Cash flows arrive at year-end; capex is already deducted from operating cash flow; the discount rate and growth rates are nominal. The example has no mid-period switch or loss-making years. If FCF is negative, financing and going-concern issues must be resolved first.

Steps

  1. Rebuild base FCF as operating cash flow - capex; remove one-off working-capital swings. Cash flow is related to but not the same as net income, and the statements must be read together [1].
  2. Forecast five years of FCF: 110, 121, 130.7, 138.5 and 144.1.
  3. Discount each year: PV = Σ FCF_t/(1+r)^t; unequal multi-period flows must be discounted period by period rather than through an annuity shortcut [2]. The five-year total is about 495.
  4. Terminal value is TV = FCF5 × 1.025/(0.09 - 0.025) ≈ 2,272, discounted at the required return together with the yearly flows [3]; its present value is about 1,477.
  5. Enterprise value is about 1,972. Subtract net debt of 200 to get equity of about 1,772; divide by 100 shares for about 17.7 per share.
  6. Re-run at r = 8% and 10%, which gives about 21.4 and 15.0 per share. Record the evidence for every assumption.

Scope note: this article takes free cash flow to be "operating cash flow minus capital expenditure". That is the working definition used on this site and a textbook convention; the supports of the registered sources do not state the formula word for word, so it is not a conclusion quoted from them. The terminal value uses the perpetual-growth model FCF5 × (1 + g)/(r - g), also the textbook convention adopted here; the sources provide a DCF framework, not that line verbatim. Compare both with the definitions used by whichever source you follow, and if that source defines them differently, use the source definition and adjust the numbers.

Interpreting the result

The terminal value is about 75% of enterprise value, so the estimate is mostly a bet on perpetuity assumptions. A 1 percentage point change in the discount rate moves value from 17.7 to 15.0 or 21.4. Lowering terminal growth to 2.0% gives about 16.6; raising it to 3.0% gives about 19.0, so g is another key switch. The 17.7 is not "true value"; it is the price equivalent of this assumption set. Break growth into volume, price, margin and reinvestment, and check that these drivers are mutually consistent.

Failure conditions

  • base cash flow is a cyclical peak or contains one-off items
  • accounting profit is used as cash flow, or capex and working capital are omitted
  • the discount rate is below or close to terminal growth
  • terminal growth exceeds long-run nominal economic growth for a sustained period
  • undiluted shares are used, or debt and cash are double-counted
  • the forecast period is too short, pushing an immature business straight into terminal value
  • a single point is reported without a range or sensitivity

Verification

Rebuild FCF from five years of statements and tie it to the filings; reverse enterprise value from market cap plus net debt; test an exit multiple or a different forecast horizon; sensitize r, g and the growth path; finally, use reverse valuation to ask whether the price implies a credible growth rate (see the reverse valuation and expectations article, INV-011).

Reproduction

The table records year, FCF, discount factor, present value, terminal value, enterprise value, net debt, equity value and value per share. Formulas: PV = Σ FCF_t/(1+r)^t + TV/(1+r)^5; TV = FCF5 × (1+g)/(r-g). Keep the source, date and unit for every input; the calculation should be reproducible in a spreadsheet.

Knowledge-point review

discounted cash flow

Definition: DCF forecasts free cash flow and discounts it at the cost of capital to value the firm or equity.

Mechanism: Key assumptions are growth, margins, reinvestment, tax and discount rate; terminal value often dominates and needs drivers and sensitivity.

Example: For example, a stable consumer firm can use 3 percent perpetual growth, while a cyclical firm should use normalised earnings.

Boundary: DCF is not a precise answer; assumptions are sensitive, terminal value dominates and cycle peaks overvalue, and a wrong discount rate biases everything.

dividend discount model

Definition: The dividend discount model discounts future dividends at the cost of equity, suited to stable dividend payers.

Mechanism: It has Gordon growth and multi-stage forms; payout, growth and discount rate drive value, and retained earnings must earn returns.

Example: For example, a stable utility is valued on 4 percent dividend growth, and mature banks often use it.

Boundary: The model is not universal; non-payers or unstable dividends do not fit, growth needs reinvestment, and it is highly sensitive to growth and discount rate.

residual income

Definition: The residual income model adds the present value of future excess returns to book equity, where excess return is profit above the cost of equity.

Mechanism: It suits financial and capital-intensive firms; ROE, capital cost and book value drive value, and clean surplus matters.

Example: For example, banks are valued on residual income, and persistent ROE above capital cost yields value above book.

Boundary: Residual income is not book plus a plug; accounting conservatism, capital cost and ROE durability matter, and perpetual excess returns are easily overestimated.

relative valuation

Definition: Relative valuation prices assets using peer or historical multiples such as P/E, EV/EBITDA, P/B and sales.

Mechanism: Multiples must match growth, risk, capital structure and accounting; peer selection, cycle and liquidity affect conclusions.

Example: For example, mature retail uses EV/EBITDA, high-growth software EV/sales, and banks P/B.

Boundary: Relative valuation is not simple cheapness; negative denominators, cycle peaks and accounting mislead, and a whole sector can be overvalued.

sum of the parts

Definition: Sum-of-the-parts values each business with its appropriate method, then adjusts for headquarters costs, net debt and holding discounts.

Mechanism: Segments differ in growth, risk and capital needs and deserve different multiples or DCFs; synergies and tax need separate treatment.

Example: For example, a conglomerate with banking, property and tech units is valued separately, then head-office costs and net debt are deducted.

Boundary: SOTP is not simple addition; intercompany deals, capital allocation and holdco discounts matter, synergies can be overstated, and disclosure may be insufficient.

reverse valuation

Definition: Reverse valuation backs out implied growth, margins, duration or discount rate from the current price to test expectations.

Mechanism: It turns valuation into an expectations question: what assumptions justify the price, and which are most sensitive or likely wrong.

Example: For example, a price implying 15 percent growth for a decade may be too high if the industry caps out.

Boundary: Reverse valuation is not a forecast; assumptions and terminal value dominate, expectations can include irrationality or undisclosed information, and triangulation is needed.

scenarios and sensitivity

Definition: Scenario analysis sets bull, base and bear assumption sets, and sensitivity tests how single variables or key drivers change value.

Mechanism: Scenarios need probabilities, triggers and capital-cost shifts; sensitivity shows dependence on growth, margins, discount rate and terminal value.

Example: For example, rates up 100 basis points and growth down 2 points can cut valuation by 30 percent.

Boundary: Scenarios are not forecasts; probabilities are subjective, variables correlate and tails deviate, and stacking cases can create false precision, so key assumptions must be stated.

Data and institutional check: free cash flow

Before using "free cash flow" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns discount rate.

Third, the institutional boundary. terminal value is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: free cash flow

Learning "free cash flow" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "discount rate": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "terminal value" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of free cash flow still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind discount rate reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "free cash flow" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "discount rate" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Review questions: free cash flow

  1. State the numerator, denominator and unit of "free cash flow" in one sentence, then explain why it is not the same metric as "discount rate".
  2. Give an example where a change in "terminal value" reverses the conclusion, and name the rule that changed.
  3. Find a time series and show how seasonal adjustment, base period or revision changes its direction.
  4. Design a transfer test: from firm to household, or bank to fund; which parameters must be re-estimated?
  5. Write a falsifiable claim: which observable signal would make you abandon this article's conclusion?

Short answers are enough, but each must leave a number, date or source location. If you cannot answer, the module is still at the noun level; if you can, you have the minimum condition for transferring the concept to new data.
