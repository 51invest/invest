Building an Investment Research Process

Turn an opinion into a traceable, falsifiable research file: state the question and falsification conditions, then check the 10-K, 8-K and EDGAR source documents; all example numbers are hypothetical.

Problem and output

With limited time, research must turn scattered facts into a record another person can reproduce and you can overturn yourself. Inputs are objectives and constraints, public filings and a time budget. The output is a research file holding the question, assumptions, evidence references, falsification conditions, update triggers and a separated conclusion. It does not guarantee a correct call, only a traceable one.

Inputs and data requirements

  • objectives, horizon and constraints (see INV-001), which set depth
  • filings: Form 10-K annual report, Form 10-Q quarterly report, Form 8-K current report, and “/A” amendments
  • audited financial statements and Management's Discussion and Analysis (MD&A)
  • industry and macro data with definitions and release dates
  • valuation inputs and their sources
  • time budget and review date

Assumptions

The example assumes 8 hours a month for one company, free public filings only, a 12-month horizon, scenario returns of +20%, +3% and -35% as teaching assumptions, and thresholds (revenue growth 5%, net debt/EBITDA 3.0 times) that illustrate rather than set an industry standard.

Steps

  1. Write the question and the decision. State what must be answered and which decision changes.
  2. Build the annual-report baseline. Follow the 10-K structure: Business, Risk Factors for the significant risks disclosed (the page reads "includes information about significant risks that the company faces, generally listed in order of importance"), Selected Financial Data ("provides certain financial information about the company for the last five years") and MD&A for trends and management's explanation, then the audited financial statements [1].
  3. Set event monitoring. Most 8-K items are due within four business days of the triggering event, catching material changes before the next periodic report - the page states "Companies must file 8-Ks promptly, rather than waiting until their next periodic report" and that companies must make most 8-K disclosures "within four business days of the triggering event and in some cases even earlier" [2].
  4. Go back to source documents. Search EDGAR by form type ("EDGAR presents search results in chronological order and identifies the filings by form types"), download filings, and separate 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K and "/A" amendments ("Filings with “/A” appended to the form type code indicate an amendment") instead of relying on summaries [3].
  5. Write assumptions and falsification conditions. Give each conclusion an observable condition that would overturn it, such as revenue growth below 5% for two quarters or negative operating cash flow for two quarters.
  6. Run scenarios and valuation. Use at least three scenarios and label the source and retrieval date of every key input.
  7. Separate the conclusion. Record facts, inferences and opinions in separate sections, each pointing to a page or link.
  8. Review quarterly. Compare forecasts with outcomes, append records, and never rewrite old ones.

Reading and review

A research process turns an opinion into a reviewable record. Build a fact base from 10-K and 8-K filings, separate management narrative from verifiable numbers, write the key assumptions, disconfirming conditions and valuation range, and log dates, sources and changes at each update. The process does not guarantee a correct call, but it prevents rewriting the story after the fact. A reproducible record lets another person rebuild the conclusion and its error boundary from the same materials. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Figure

Example

Suppose a file records a 12-month horizon, scenario returns of +20%, +3% and -35%, and a falsification condition of “revenue growth below 5% or operating cash flow negative for two quarters.” Three months later an 8-K on an auditor change triggers a review, which only appends records and marks the conclusion “unverified.” Without a pre-written condition, the change would be dismissed as noise.

Interpreting the result

The process buys traceability and falsifiability, not accuracy. In this article's view, the order of the 10-K can serve as a reading method: business and risks first, then numbers, then management's explanation [1]. Periodic reports lag, and the 8-K adds timely disclosure of material events [2]; the EDGAR form type and “/A” marker determine whether the version in hand is current [3]. Separating facts, inferences and opinions shows which layer failed at review.

Failure conditions

  • reading only press releases or commentary instead of the filing
  • missing an “/A” amendment, or treating a superseded figure as final
  • taking the MD&A narrative as fact rather than management's explanation
  • no falsification condition, so the conclusion drifts with price and story
  • covering too many names for the time budget and leaving files unfinished
  • rewriting past records at review, which hides the error
  • using one document to support opposite conclusions

Verification

  1. Ask a second person to recompute the key figures from the file alone.
  2. Compare the prior conclusion with the outcome and record the difference.
  3. Sample three conclusions and confirm each points to a filing and section.
  4. Check data versions and retrieval dates.

Reproduction

The template records the subject and date, the question, the linked decision, assumptions, an evidence list (form type, filing date, link), falsification conditions and thresholds, scenarios, the separated conclusion and the next review date. Another person should be able to rebuild the reasoning from that list.

Working paper: a four-column check for "Building an Investment Research Process"

The subject of this article is "Building an Investment Research Process". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "investment research process". Column two states the transmission channel of "annual report on Form 10-K". Column three states the institutional boundary of "current report on Form 8-K". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "annual report on Form 10-K" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. current report on Form 8-K is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Boundary case: Can a 10-K risk factor overturn a research hypothesis?

Suppose the hypothesis is “rising demand lifts gross margin”. The 10-K discloses that one customer is 40% of revenue and one supplier is 60% of purchases. If the customer cuts orders by 20%, revenue falls 8% and fixed costs cut gross margin by two points, weakening the hypothesis. If an 8-K after the quarter reports that the customer renewed, revenue falls only 2% and the hypothesis remains under review. Verifying with headlines instead of original filings can turn “renewal” into “weak demand” or the reverse.

The conclusion reverses when risk disclosure is a boilerplate list: 10-K risk factors are listed by importance but are not guaranteed to be quantified; the analyst must return to statements, customer concentration and cash flow. The boundary is that a research process turns a view into a falsifiable file. One item cannot replace the hypothesis, the disconfirming condition and the update log.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not company disclosure.
