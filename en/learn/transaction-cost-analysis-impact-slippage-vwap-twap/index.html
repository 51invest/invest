Transaction Cost Analysis: Impact, Slippage and VWAP/TWAP

Trading cost is not just a commission; it is the gap between the decision price and execution price, made of delay, spread, impact and opportunity cost.

Definition

Implementation shortfall is the difference between the paper portfolio return at decision time and the actual portfolio return; Perold separates it into explicit commissions, the gap between execution and decision price, and the opportunity cost of unfilled quantity [3]. Slippage is an adverse move from a benchmark price; market impact is the price concession caused by the order consuming liquidity. VWAP is a volume-weighted average price and TWAP a time-sliced average; both are execution benchmarks, not investment decision benchmarks.

Intuition

Transaction cost is like buying an airline ticket: beyond the fare there are seat selection, baggage, change fees and airport transport. An order has a decision price, arrival price, execution price and close, and each step can leak return. VWAP is the volume-weighted average market price; TWAP is the time-weighted average. They are two benchmark fares. Evaluating a large order against VWAP asks 'did I buy better than the market average?'; evaluating against arrival asks 'how much did I pay from decision to execution?' Different benchmarks can produce opposite conclusions.

Mechanism

Larger orders, thinner liquidity and greater urgency increase impact. The spread is the cost of immediate execution; delay is exposure to price drift while waiting; opportunity cost is the move missed when a limit order does not fill. VWAP compares executions with market volume and is useful for 'did we track the market', but a large order is part of that volume and can become self-referential; TWAP controls time but ignores volume distribution. POV slices by participation rate, limiting impact but risking incomplete execution when liquidity disappears. Benchmark choice changes the definition of cost, so the objective must come first [1][2].

Worked example

Suppose the decision price is CNY 50.00, the order is 100,000 shares, arrival price is CNY 50.20, average execution is CNY 50.50, the close is CNY 51.00, and commissions and taxes total CNY 2,000. Paper cost is CNY 5.00 million and actual cost is CNY 5.05 million, a shortfall of CNY 50,000. Delay cost is (50.20 − 50.00) × 100,000 = CNY 20,000; impact cost is (50.50 − 50.20) × 100,000 = CNY 30,000; explicit fees are CNY 2,000; total CNY 52,000. If only 80,000 shares fill, the 20,000-share opportunity cost at the close is (51.00 − 50.00) × 20,000 = CNY 20,000. Shortfall is often in basis points: 52,000 / 5,000,000 ≈ 104 bp. These are teaching assumptions.

Second worked example: decomposing implementation shortfall

Suppose you decide to buy 10,000 shares at a decision price of CNY 100.00. At arrival the market is CNY 100.20, the period VWAP is CNY 100.50 and your average execution is CNY 100.80. Implementation shortfall = (100.80 − 100.00) × 10,000 = CNY 8,000, or 80 basis points of the decision value. Delay cost = (100.20 − 100.00) × 10,000 = CNY 2,000; impact versus arrival = (100.80 − 100.20) × 10,000 = CNY 6,000; gap versus VWAP = (100.80 − 100.50) × 10,000 = CNY 3,000. Against VWAP the cost is 30bp; against the decision price it is 80bp. Both are correct but answer different questions. These are teaching assumptions and exclude commissions, tax and opportunity cost.

Boundary and common mistakes

First, a low VWAP does not prove good execution: if the order itself lifts volume, VWAP is influenced by the trade. Second, TWAP ignores intraday liquidity and can increase impact at the open or close. Third, low commissions do not mean low total cost; impact and opportunity cost can dominate. Fourth, short-term drawdown and trading cost must be separated; not every market fall is slippage. Fifth, set the benchmark and urgency ex ante, or the ex-post analysis can choose a convenient ruler.

Failure modes

The first failure is the wrong benchmark: using VWAP for an urgent build-up understates delay cost; using the decision price for a passive allocation turns a market-wide rally into an execution loss. The second is too small a sample: one day or one order is noisy, and a single 10,000-share order cannot establish annual skill. The third is gaming the benchmark: a trader can push executions toward the close to flatter VWAP while raising real impact and opportunity cost. The fourth is ignoring opportunity cost: an unfilled order shows no price loss but may miss the whole move, and that cost is not in the average execution price. The fifth is looking only at explicit commissions, not bid-ask spreads, market impact, taxes and borrow cost.

Three checks

First, what is the benchmark: decision price, arrival, VWAP or close, and what question does each answer? Second, how is cost decomposed: delay, impact, opportunity and explicit fees, and how much does each contribute? Third, is the sample sufficient and adjusted for style and market conditions? These three determine whether you are seeing execution skill or a gift from the market.

Adjacent concepts and a counterexample

VWAP, TWAP and implementation shortfall are easily mixed. VWAP is volume-weighted and suits orders that participate by volume; TWAP is time-weighted and suits uniform execution; implementation shortfall starts at the decision price and captures the whole loss from decision to execution. The benchmarks differ and are not directly comparable. A counterexample is 'a good VWAP means good execution': a trader can concentrate orders in high-volume periods to track VWAP while delay and opportunity cost rise; if the order is not fully filled, VWAP only measures the filled part and hides the unfilled opportunity cost. Another is 'low commission means low cost': commission is only the explicit fee; spread, impact, tax and borrow cost can be larger. Compare executions with one benchmark, decomposed cost and out-of-sample tests.

Scope and limits

This transaction-cost framework applies to orders with an identifiable decision price, recorded fills and estimable market impact. If the order is split across accounts, the decision price is unrecorded, or fills occur in a liquidity break, VWAP/TWAP and implementation shortfall are distorted. It does not apply to treating commission as the whole cost, nor to judging a trader by one execution. Align benchmark and cost definitions, then compare by order size, volatility and depth; small-order conclusions do not extrapolate to large orders.

Finally, if one order spans several days or includes dark-pool fills, the link between decision price and fills weakens; separate overnight drift as delay cost and compare across volatility regimes, since a one-day sample cannot support a long-run execution policy.

If only broker confirmations are available without exchange timestamps, delay cost is a lower bound; cross-market execution must align time zones and FX, and fills from different liquidity periods should not be averaged directly, or sample structure magnifies a single-order conclusion.

Also keep reproducible intermediate records and scripts.

Research problem

Transaction-cost analysis does not ask “was the fill price good?” but “how much more did execution cost relative to a reproducible benchmark?” The benchmark can be arrival price, VWAP, TWAP or close; different benchmarks are not directly comparable.

Inputs

Order arrival time, side, quantity, fills, book snapshots, benchmark prices, commissions, taxes, borrow fees and opportunity cost. Without book or fill data, only an approximation is possible, not a full implementation shortfall.

Assumptions

Order side, benchmark timing and fee conventions are consistent; later prices are not treated as information available at execution; unfilled quantity is not treated as zero cost. A benchmark containing future information systematically biases the result.

Steps

Fix the benchmark and fee convention, decompose the total difference into delay, impact, timing and fees, group by order, size, volatility and liquidity, and recompute with the same convention.

Interpreting results

Express cost in basis points or per-share cost, alongside absolute amount and share of ADV. Low cost is not automatically good execution: slowing down to reduce impact can raise opportunity cost.

Failure conditions

Too few samples, irreproducible benchmark, mixed order sides, ignored unfilled quantity, commission-only cost, or comparing different dates all invalidate the conclusion.

Reproduction and verification

Keep order timestamps, fill data, benchmark script and fee table; rerun on another sample and check whether ranking and grouping conclusions hold.
