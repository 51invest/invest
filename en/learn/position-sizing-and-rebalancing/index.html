Position Sizing and Rebalancing

Problem and output

Position sizing decides how much of a portfolio a single asset or strategy occupies; rebalancing decides when and how much to trade back to target after a drift. The outputs are target weights, risk-budgeted position sizes, trigger rules, a trade list and a cost estimate, not a one-off market forecast.

Portfolio weights determine portfolio return and risk, and portfolio volatility depends on the asset volatilities, the weights and the correlations — the OpenStax framing used here [3].

Inputs and data requirements

Inputs include portfolio value, target weights, asset volatilities and correlations, risk budgets, trading costs, taxes, minimum trade sizes and cash flows; risk budgets must fit within total portfolio risk. The example is a hypothetical CNY 1 million portfolio with a 60/40 stock/bond target, stock volatility of 16%, bond volatility of 6% and correlation of 0.20.

The SEC guide describes rebalancing as bringing the portfolio back to its target allocation — selling the overweight, buying the underweight, or directing new contributions — and warns that trading costs and tax consequences have to be considered [1].

Assumptions

Long-only and unlevered; costs are 0.10% of traded value on each side; taxes and minimum trade sizes are ignored; the rebalancing threshold is 5 percentage points; all numbers are teaching assumptions.

Steps

  1. Risk-based size: a satellite asset has 30% volatility and a 1.5 percentage point portfolio risk budget. Assuming low correlation, its weight is about 1.5%/30% = 5%, or CNY 50,000.
  2. Start with CNY 600,000 in stocks and CNY 400,000 in bonds.
  3. Stocks rise 40% and bonds are unchanged: stocks reach CNY 840,000, bonds CNY 400,000, total CNY 1,240,000; the stock weight is 84/124 = 67.74%, a drift of 7.74 percentage points.
  4. The 5 percentage point threshold is breached, so restore 60/40: stocks target CNY 744,000 and bonds CNY 496,000.
  5. Sell CNY 96,000 of stocks and buy CNY 96,000 of bonds; at 0.10% on each side, cost is 96+96 = CNY 192, leaving CNY 1,239,808.
  6. With new contributions, buy the underweight asset first to reduce sales, taxes and slippage.
  7. Record the trigger date, execution prices, slippage and deviation from target.

Why drift is checked before thresholds: Vanguard’s illustration shows a 60/40 portfolio becoming roughly 65/35 after stocks rise 10% and bonds fall 10%, taking more risk than the target, with a five-percentage-point band used to return to the allocation [2].

Interpreting the result

The 5 percentage point threshold pulls a 7.74 percentage point drift back to target at a cost of CNY 192; frequent trading would accumulate costs. Position size should follow the risk budget and volatility, not confidence or recent performance. Rebalancing is not a return-enhancement guarantee: it controls exposure and discipline and may lag buy-and-hold in a one-way trend. Calendar, threshold and combined rules are the three common choices; the choice belongs in the investment policy and should fit portfolio size, cash flows and taxes.

Failure conditions

  • a threshold so narrow that trading and costs explode
  • ignoring taxes, minimum trade sizes and liquidity
  • using leverage without a margin buffer
  • being unable to trade, or facing wider slippage, in extreme volatility
  • treating rebalancing as market timing
  • changing the rule after the fact instead of writing it into policy
  • target weights that no longer fit the goal or horizon

Verification

Compare thresholds and frequencies; include costs, taxes and slippage; observe drift, turnover and risk; run liquidity stress tests; check whether post-trade weights are close to target.

Reproduction

drift = actual weight - target weight; trade value = target value - actual value; cost = trade value × fee rate × 2. Example: 67.74%-60% = 7.74%; 840,000 - 744,000 = CNY 96,000; 96,000×0.10%×2 = CNY 192.

Reading checklist

A final check is the liquidity of the rebalancing trade: the assets used to adjust may be illiquid precisely when risk is high. Set a liquidity buffer and a maximum trade size; a rule that works only in calm markets is not a risk control.

Working paper: four columns for a checkable judgement

The subject of this article is "INV-019". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "position sizing". Column two states the transmission channel of "rebalancing". Column three states the institutional boundary of "risk budget". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "rebalancing" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. risk budget is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Review sentence

Write one sentence only: "Under the ____ convention, a change in ____ affects ____ through ____; if ____ appears, the claim fails." The four blanks are the unit, the variable, the channel and the falsifier. If the sentence cannot be written, at least one of the four columns is still empty; if it can be written but cannot be observed, the falsifier is wrong. On every data update, reread this sentence before reading the charts.

Boundary case: After a 40% stock rally, 60/40 becomes 67.7/32.3

A portfolio starts with 600,000 of stocks and 400,000 of bonds, worth 1,000,000. Stocks rise 40% to 840,000 while bonds stay at 400,000, so the total is 1,240,000 and the stock weight is 840,000 ÷ 1,240,000 = 67.74%, a 7.74-point drift. With a five-point threshold, restore 60/40: stock target 744,000, bond target 496,000, sell 96,000 of stocks and buy 96,000 of bonds. At 0.10% each way, cost is 192 yuan and the total becomes 1,239,808.

With a ten-point threshold, the current 7.74-point drift does not trigger and the portfolio keeps drifting; another 10% stock gain lifts the weight to about 70.7% and more risk. The conclusion reverses when new money is available: buying bonds with contributions avoids selling stocks and cuts taxes and slippage. The boundary is that rebalancing is a risk-budget tool, not a forecast. A wider threshold means fewer trades but more risk drift.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not portfolio records.

Rebalancing must also consider taxes, minimum trade sizes and cash-flow timing. Selling in a retirement account may trigger tax, and a taxable account may realise capital gains; using contributions or dividend reinvestment can lower trading costs. Threshold rules should be designed with account type, tax and cash-flow schedule.

Reading and review

Position sizing and rebalancing are not calendar trading; they turn a risk budget into weights. Set a maximum single-asset weight, a portfolio drawdown limit and a liquidity floor, then allocate by risk contribution. When a rally pushes weights past a threshold, rebalancing trims the winner and buys the laggard, controlling concentration and buying low. Trading costs eat the benefit, so the band cannot be too narrow; rebalancing inside tax-advantaged accounts is cheaper. Leverage must include financing cost and margin-call risk, not target volatility alone. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.
