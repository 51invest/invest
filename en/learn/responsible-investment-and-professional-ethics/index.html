Responsible Investment and Professional Ethics: Writing Preferences into Portfolios

Responsible investment is not “doing good earns more”; it turns environmental, social and governance preferences into executable screening, engagement and disclosure rules. Professional ethics put client interests, conflicts and suitability before sales.

Definition

Responsible investment is the umbrella for methods that include environmental, social and governance factors in analysis and portfolio construction[1]. It has at least 4 approaches: negative screening (excluding tobacco, weapons and so on), positive screening (choosing high ESG scorers), thematic investing (clean energy, health care and so on), and ESG integration (bringing ESG risk into valuation). Professional ethics requires advisers to put client interests before their own commissions, disclose conflicts and ensure suitability.

Intuition

Think of responsible investment as ordering food: some people avoid spicy food, some eat only organic, some pay more for sustainable ingredients. A portfolio is similar: exclusions narrow the investable universe, thematic investing concentrates exposure, and ESG integration swaps in better inputs for the same dish. The key is to state “why exclude, how much, at what cost”, not to attach a green label.

A stricter approach uses a factor model or historical tracking error. The larger and more concentrated the exclusion, the higher the tracking error; the cost of the constraint must be compared with the agreed risk budget[2].

When it applies

First, ESG data is verifiable; second, exclusion rules match client preferences; third, tracking error is within the risk budget; fourth, disclosure is not exaggerated; fifth, fees and liquidity are bearable; sixth, professional-ethics and suitability processes are in place. Without one, responsible investment can become marketing language.

Example

Suppose the portfolio excludes 10% of market cap and the excluded segment has 20% volatility; rough tracking error rises by about 2 percentage points. If the client’s risk budget is 4 points, the constraint is acceptable; if it is 1 point, a narrower exclusion or broader substitutes are needed. Numbers are teaching assumptions; use historical data in practice.

Common mistakes

First, “ESG always raises returns”: costs, exclusions and style exposure can drag. Second, “a green label means compliance”: look at disclosure and underlying assets. Third, “responsible investment means no energy”: engagement and transition can be active improvement after passive holding. Fourth, “ethics is only personal character”: conflicts and suitability have explicit process requirements[3].

Reading and review

Responsible investment is not pasting “good company” labels onto a portfolio; it converts preferences into executable rules and measures the cost. Excluding 10% of a sector creates tracking error; thematic concentration raises sector exposure; engagement and voting change corporate behaviour but cost time and work slowly. Suitability starts with the client's goals and constraints before products; conflicts require disclosing who pays and who benefits. Judge an ESG programme by screening scope, benchmark, tracking error, engagement resources and exit rules, not by the marketing label. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Reading checklist

Responsible investment also needs a counterfactual: what would the portfolio have held without the screen, and how different is the risk? Without that comparison, a lower carbon score may simply reflect a different sector mix. Report the active share, tracking error and intended exit rules so preferences are auditable rather than narrative.

Run one counterexample and one stress case before extending the conclusion to another market, period or population.

Boundary case: Excluding 10% of market value adds about two points of tracking error

A benchmark holds 100 stocks and the portfolio excludes 10% of market value. If the excluded sleeve has 20% volatility, a rough estimate adds about two points of tracking error. With a client risk budget of four points, the exclusion is acceptable; with a one-point budget, the exclusion must shrink to 5% or use a more diversified substitute, adding about one point. If the remaining portfolio is concentrated in 30 stocks, specific risk rises and actual tracking error may exceed two points.

The conclusion reverses when exclusion improves long-term risk or reflects an explicit client preference: if the client does not want exposure to an industry, tracking error is an authorised cost, not mismanagement; if the exclusion is only a label and holdings do not change, it is greenwashing. The boundary is that responsible investment turns preferences into screening, engagement and disclosure rules, while professional ethics puts client interests, conflicts and suitability before sales.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not a risk budget.

Responsible investment must also state who defines the preference. Clients, beneficiaries and regulators may hold different values; a manager cannot package its own preferences as a client mandate. Screening, engagement and disclosure need authorisation records, and performance attribution should separate constraint costs from active choices.
