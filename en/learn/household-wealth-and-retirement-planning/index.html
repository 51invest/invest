Household Wealth and Retirement Planning

Use a hypothetical 40-year-old couple's balance sheet to convert a 70% replacement rate, 2.5% inflation and 25-year retirement into CNY 4.64 million of required assets, compare it with CNY 5.53 million projected, and show why a 119% funding ratio is not secure under return and longevity stress.

Problem and output

Household wealth planning links goals, the balance sheet and cash flows to answer how much to save now, how to allocate assets and how much can be spent in retirement. The outputs are a saving rate, projected retirement assets, sustainable spending, funding ratio and gap, plus stress-test results, not a specific product recommendation.

Inputs and data requirements

Inputs include household assets and liabilities, income and expenses, an emergency fund, insurance, pensions and retirement accounts, tax rates, investment horizon, expected returns, inflation and longevity. The example is a hypothetical couple: age 40, retirement at 65 (25 years of accumulation), 25 years in retirement; CNY 800,000 of investable assets and CNY 120,000 of annual saving (adjusted for inflation), a saving rate of 120,000/300,000 = 40% of pre-retirement income; pre-retirement income CNY 300,000, a 70% replacement target, or CNY 210,000 a year in today's money; inflation 2.5%; nominal returns 5.0% before retirement and 3.5% after. Amounts are in CNY as a teaching unit and do not correspond to any jurisdiction's tax rules, social insurance or retirement-account rules; the example contains no real statistics.

The CFPB frames retirement readiness as a balance of debt, income and assets, and flags the risks of products such as pension advances and reverse mortgages [1].

Assumptions

Saving is adjusted for inflation, so real saving is constant; returns are constant annual rates before fees; taxes, public pensions and bequests are excluded; the primary residence is excluded from investable assets; all numbers are teaching assumptions.

The composition of the household balance sheet here is calibrated against the Federal Reserve survey, which accounts for household net worth by assets and liabilities and provides pension wealth and income data [3].

Steps

  1. Target spending is CNY 210,000 today; at age 65 the nominal value is about 210,000×1.025^25 ≈ CNY 389,000 a year.
  2. Real returns: before retirement (1.05/1.025)-1 ≈ 2.44%; after retirement (1.035/1.025)-1 ≈ 0.98%; the Fisher relation and the definition of a real return are covered in the Real Returns and Purchasing Power article (INV-003) and are not repeated here.
  3. Projected real assets at 65: 800,000×1.0244^25 + 120,000×[(1.0244^25-1)/0.0244] ≈ CNY 5.53 million.
  4. Required assets: 210,000×[1-1.0098^-25]/0.0098 ≈ CNY 4.64 million.
  5. Funding ratio CNY 5.53 million/CNY 4.64 million ≈ 119% (both expressed at age 65 in today's money), a surplus of about CNY 890,000.
  6. Reverse check on sustainable spending: CNY 5.53 million/22.08 ≈ CNY 250,000 a year in today's money, where 22.08 is the [1-1.0098^-25]/0.0098 factor from step 4, above the CNY 210,000 target.
  7. Stress test one: if the pre-retirement return falls to 4.0%, assets are about CNY 4.74 million and the funding ratio is 102%.
  8. Stress test two: if retirement lasts 30 years, required assets are about CNY 5.44 million and the funding ratio is 102%.

Per the SEC investor guide, investment objectives and the time horizon determine risk tolerance and asset allocation, diversification reduces the risk of any single asset, and rebalancing brings the portfolio back to the target allocation [2].

Interpreting the result

A 119% funding ratio is only 19% above target, and either stress test almost exhausts the buffer; if lower returns and longer life occur together, the plan may become underfunded. Retirement planning turns on real returns, saving, fees and longevity, not a single return assumption. Allocation should shift with the horizon, reducing volatility near retirement; an emergency fund and insurance come before investing; the home counts only if downsizing is a firm plan. The plan should be recomputed at least annually, adjusting the saving rate and allocation when income, costs or rates change. For the record: "reduce volatility near retirement", "an emergency fund and insurance come before investing" and "the home counts only if downsizing is a firm plan" are this article's normative rules of thumb (value judgements), not empirical findings from the three registered sources; the 70% replacement rate is only an input target of this example, and the article does not claim it as a universal rule of thumb - realised replacement depends on after-tax income structure, public pensions and spending.

Failure conditions

  • treating average returns as a certain outcome and ignoring the sequence of returns
  • ignoring inflation, taxes, fees and health costs
  • double-counting the home or an unrealised pension
  • an emergency fund too small, forcing sales at a low point
  • omitting longevity, disability and care risks
  • never updating the plan after income or spending changes
  • a liquidity mismatch that funds short-term goals with long-term assets
  • over-concentration in employer stock or a single asset

Verification

Build a three-way sensitivity table over returns, inflation and longevity; use historical ranges or Monte Carlo simulation; reconcile actual assets with the plan each year; stress-test a 20% fall two years before retirement; recompute after fees and taxes; confirm pension eligibility rules and the replacement-rate definition.

Reproduction

real return=(1+nominal)/(1+inflation)-1; terminal assets=A₀(1+r)^n+S[((1+r)^n-1)/r]; required assets=spending×[1-(1+r)^-n]/r. Example: 2.44%; CNY 5.53 million; CNY 4.64 million; 119%.

Knowledge-point review

personal balance sheet

Definition: A personal balance sheet lists assets, liabilities and net worth, reflecting financial health and liquidity.

Mechanism: Assets are classified by realisable value, liabilities by rate, maturity and payment; net worth is assets minus liabilities but illiquid assets cannot fund emergencies.

Example: For example, a home may be valuable but slow to sell, while card and consumer debt are costly, leaving positive net worth but tight cash flow.

Boundary: Net worth is not security; valuations, debt costs and liquidity change, a primary home is not disposable investment, and hidden liabilities count.

emergency fund

Definition: An emergency fund is liquid savings for job loss, medical or repair shocks, usually three to twelve months of essential spending.

Mechanism: Size depends on income stability, debt, dependants and insurance; it belongs in low-risk, quickly accessible accounts.

Example: For example, a dual-income debt-free household may need three months, while a single earner with a mortgage may need twelve.

Boundary: More emergency cash is not always better; excess drags long-term returns, too little forces distressed sales or high-cost borrowing, and inflation erodes cash.

insurance protection

Definition: Insurance transfers low-frequency, high-severity risks such as death, illness, disability, property and liability for a premium, focused on protection not investment.

Mechanism: Cover should address income loss, debt, medical costs and family duties; deductibles, waiting periods, exclusions and insurer credit determine payouts.

Example: For example, term life covers a mortgage and children's education, and medical insurance covers large hospital bills.

Boundary: More insurance is not better; over-insurance wastes premiums, under-insurance bankrupts on a shock, and investment-linked policies can be costly with weak cover.

housing and education

Definition: Housing is shelter and an asset, and education is human-capital investment; both are large, long-term commitments affecting cash flow.

Mechanism: Housing involves down payment, mortgage, rates, liquidity and opportunity cost; education involves fees, time, returns and risk, and both should fit the life cycle.

Example: For example, buying a home adds leverage and stability, while education raises earning capacity with uncertain returns.

Boundary: Housing is not a guaranteed investment; leverage, upkeep, policy and illiquidity cause losses, education does not ensure high returns, and overspending squeezes retirement saving.

life cycle planning

Definition: Life-cycle planning incorporates changing income, spending, risk and responsibilities across youth, family formation, midlife and retirement.

Mechanism: Human capital, debt, dependants and capacity differ by stage; saving, insurance, investing and withdrawals should adapt.

Example: For example, youth with strong human capital can bear more equity, while retirement needs stable cash flow and medical cover.

Boundary: The life cycle is not a fixed template; health, career, family and legacy needs differ, transition timing and policy change, and age alone is not a plan.

withdrawal and inheritance basics

Definition: Withdrawal funds retirement and living costs from assets, and inheritance transfers assets according to wishes and tax efficiency.

Mechanism: Withdrawal rate, order, inflation and longevity determine asset life; inheritance involves wills, trusts, gifts, taxes and family communication.

Example: For example, a retiree withdraws 4 percent with a cash buffer, and a trust reduces disputes and tax.

Boundary: Withdrawal is not simple selling; return order, taxes and longevity create pressure, and inheritance is more than a will because law, tax and family conflict can defeat it.
