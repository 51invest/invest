Investment Strategies

An investment strategy is not a style label but a reproducible set of return sources, risk exposures, capacity limits and trading costs.

ModuleINV-M09 · 投资策略
LevelFoundation
Read6 min · 12 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

Definition

An investment strategy is a reproducible set of rules: what return source it harvests, what risk it bears, when it works, how much capacity it has and what it costs. Common types include holding and dollar-cost averaging, index tracking, active styles (value/size/profitability/investment), trend and mean reversion, carry and arbitrage, relative value and event-driven[1][2][3][4]; hedge funds commonly use short selling, leverage and derivatives, with higher fees and greater liquidity limits[5]. The name is a label; the substance is return source and risk exposure.

Intuition

Two people both “invest”: one buys and holds an index, the other trades a spread between two related assets. The first earns market beta, the second earns spread convergence; their risk, capacity and costs differ completely. Comparing historical returns alone does not tell whether they are the same kind of strategy.

Formal expression

Holding and dollar-cost averaging

Buy-and-hold and dollar-cost averaging use time diversification to reduce timing risk. Averaging does not guarantee higher returns; it changes entry timing and cash-flow rhythm, and in a persistently rising market a lump sum may have higher expected return[2].

Index

Index investing tracks a rules-based portfolio, earning market beta and rebalancing effects. Evaluation should examine tracking error, fees, taxes and liquidity, not the index name[1].

Active styles

Value, size, profitability and investment styles express selection rules as testable factor exposures; growth and quality are common labels, but must first be tied to a specific variable (such as revenue growth, ROE or earnings stability) before they can be tested. Style returns may persist or fail for long periods; treating a style label as a return guarantee is a common error[3].

Trend, mean reversion, carry and arbitrage

Trend bets on continuation, mean reversion on return to average; carry earns yield differentials, arbitrage earns price gaps for the same asset across markets or contracts. Each has a different return source, crowding and tail risk[4][5].

Relative value and events

Relative value compares spreads between related assets; event-driven bets on mergers, restructurings or regulatory outcomes. They depend on event probability, timetable and legal risk; backtests should guard against survivorship and look-ahead bias — a general research caution, not a finding of [5].

Four elements

Every strategy should state return source, risk exposure, capacity and cost. Missing one makes it impossible to replicate or evaluate.

Conditions and limits

  1. Strategy evaluation must state sample period, benchmark, fees and taxes; gross returns alone cannot show net effect.
  2. Backtests must handle survivorship bias, look-ahead bias, multiple testing and trading costs; more parameters raise overfitting risk.
  3. Capacity depends on market depth, turnover and impact cost; a strategy that works for small capital may fail at scale.
  4. Style and factor premia change with institutions, participants and valuations; history is not a guarantee.

Worked example

Suppose strategy A earns 8% annually with 12% volatility; strategy B earns 8% with 18% volatility; the risk-free rate is 2%. These are teaching assumptions.

Strategy Annual return Volatility Sharpe ratio ((return-2)/volatility)
A 8% 12% 0.50
B 8% 18% 0.33

For the same 8% return, A has better risk-adjusted performance. But Sharpe is not the only criterion: if B has lower tail risk, more capacity or better matches the client's liabilities, it may still fit. Evaluate return, risk, capacity, cost and purpose together.

Common mistakes

A good backtest means live trading will work. Backtests often contain survivorship bias, look-ahead bias and omitted costs; slippage, capacity and execution change results materially.

The strategy name explains the return. “Value”, “trend” and “arbitrage” are labels; return source, risk exposure, capacity and cost must be written down to judge replicability.

Knowledge-point review

buy-and-hold and dollar-cost averaging

Definition: Buy-and-hold stays invested long term with infrequent trading, and dollar-cost averaging invests a fixed amount at regular intervals to smooth entry cost.

Mechanism: Holding cuts trading costs and timing errors, while DCA averages through volatility; both rely on long-term returns and discipline.

Example: For example, monthly investment in a broad index buys more shares in declines and fewer in rallies.

Boundary: Holding is not never selling; deterioration, changing goals and extreme valuations warrant changes, and DCA cannot guarantee profit in a long decline.

index investing

Definition: Index investing replicates a market or rules-based portfolio, seeking benchmark return rather than stock-selection alpha.

Mechanism: Low cost, broad diversification and transparency are advantages; tracking error, concentration and cap weighting are risks.

Example: For example, a CSI 300 index fund gives large-cap exposure at a much lower fee than active funds.

Boundary: Indexing is not risk-free; concentration, bubbles and tracking error affect results, and index rules can be stale or biased to past winners.

active and style investing

Definition: Active investing seeks excess return through selection, timing and allocation, while style investing concentrates on value, growth or quality traits.

Mechanism: Active relies on research, information and execution, style on factor premia; both need control of fees, concentration and drift.

Example: For example, an active manager overweights undervalued sectors, while a style fund holds growth stocks by rule.

Boundary: Active does not guarantee outperformance; competition, size and fees erode it, styles lag in cycles, and labels can hide actual exposure.

trend following and mean reversion

Definition: Trend following trades with price direction, while mean reversion bets prices return to a long-run average or relationship.

Mechanism: Trend earns continuation and reversion earns overreaction; volatility, costs and regime shifts determine which works.

Example: For example, breakout strategies profit in one-way markets, and pairs trading profits when a spread converges.

Boundary: Both can fail; trends whipsaw in ranges, reversion buys into structural breaks, and leverage magnifies losses.

carry and arbitrage

Definition: Carry buys higher-yielding assets and sells lower-yielding ones for the spread, while arbitrage exploits price differences between the same or related assets.

Mechanism: Carry bears FX, rate and credit risk; arbitrage bears execution, liquidity and model risk; leverage is often used to magnify small spreads.

Example: For example, borrowing in a low-rate currency to buy a high-yield one earns carry, or the same stock is arbitraged across markets.

Boundary: Carry is not risk-free; FX crashes can wipe out the spread, arbitrage is scarce and crowded, and leverage and counterparty risk cause blowups.

relative value and event-driven strategies

Definition: Relative value compares price relationships among related assets, and event-driven strategies trade mergers, restructurings, earnings or policy events.

Mechanism: Relative value earns convergence, event strategies earn completion or expectation revisions; law, timing and deal terms determine risk.

Example: For example, merger arbitrage buys the target and shorts the acquirer to earn the spread on completion.

Boundary: Neither is low-risk; spreads can widen, deals can fail or delay, and liquidity and leverage amplify losses.

Exercises

  1. Concept check: distinguish trend from mean reversion, stating the return source and main risk of each.
  2. Calculation: if the risk-free rate rises to 3% in the example, what are the Sharpe ratios of A and B?
  3. Conditions: explain why a strategy may work for small capital but fail at scale.
Suggested answers
  1. Trend earns continuation; its main risks are reversal and crowding. Mean reversion earns convergence to an average; its main risk is that the average moves or the spread stays dislocated.
  2. (8−3)/12≈0.42; (8−3)/18≈0.28.
  3. Market depth, turnover and impact costs rise with size, or arbitrageurs crowd the strategy; small-capital efficacy may come from liquidity and niche opportunities.
