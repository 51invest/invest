The Investment Policy Statement: Writing Objectives, Constraints and Rebalancing into Rules

An investment policy statement is not compliance decoration; it writes down why you invest, how much risk you can bear and when to adjust, in executable rules. Without one, portfolios drift with emotion, hype and short-term performance.

Definition and intuition

An investment policy statement (IPS) is a rulebook between the investor and the portfolio manager, covering objectives, constraints, allocation, rebalancing, benchmarks and review. It does not forecast markets; it specifies what to do under different markets.

Six core elements: objectives (return, risk, horizon), constraints (liquidity, tax, legal, ethical), allocation (strategic and tactical ranges), rebalancing (thresholds and frequency), benchmark (evaluation standard), and governance (who decides, reviews and amends).

The value of an IPS is pre-commitment: when markets fall, the rules say whether to add; when markets euphoric, the rules limit chasing. Without one, decisions follow recent performance and narratives[1][2].

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose the target is 6% annual return, maximum drawdown 15% and liquidity needs of 5% a year. Strategic allocation is 60% equities, 30% bonds and 10% cash; equity range 50%-70%, bonds 25%-35%.

Rebalancing rule: any asset deviating 5 points from target, or a quarterly check. If equities rise to 68%, just below the 70% cap, the rule triggers a sale of 8 points into bonds and cash.

If equities fall 20%, the portfolio drawdown is about 12%, within the 15% tolerance; if they fall 30%, the drawdown is about 18%, beyond tolerance, and the IPS requires reviewing the risk budget rather than blindly buying more. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Three rules

Rule 1: objectives must be measurable. 'High returns' is not an objective; '6% annual return, volatility below 12%, maximum drawdown under 15%' is.

Rule 2: rebalancing needs thresholds. Calendar rebalancing trades too often; pure threshold rebalancing may never trigger; combining both is steadier.

Rule 3: exceptions must be recorded. Deviations are allowed but need a reason, approver and review date; exceptions cannot become the norm.

The IPS also specifies benchmarks and evaluation horizons: short-term underperformance is not strategy failure; long-term deviation requires review[3][4].

Conditions, boundaries and common mistakes

Boundary 1: an IPS is not a return guarantee. It manages process and risk, not market returns; objectives need reasonable assumptions.

Boundary 2: the IPS updates with life stages. Retirement, job loss, marriage and tax changes alter constraints; review annually and after major changes.

Boundary 3: institutions and individuals differ. Pensions have fiduciary duties and actuarial assumptions; individuals care more about liquidity and tax; do not copy institutional templates.

Mistake 1: longer is more professional. Clear, executable rules matter; long templates go unread.

Mistake 2: rebalancing always raises returns. It controls risk, not return; in trending markets it may reduce short-term gains.

Deeper note: IPS governance, risk budgeting and rebalancing

An IPS is not a list of return targets but a governance contract. It should state who decides, who bears fiduciary duty, who monitors and how conflicts are resolved. Without governance clauses, the IPS is bypassed under stress: targets change after losses, risk is chased after gains, constraints loosen in election years.

Separate 'needs' from 'wants'. Needs are spending obligations and solvency requirements; wants are return rankings or excess return. Writing wants as hard targets invites excessive risk; calculating needs sets a reasonable return and risk budget.

Risk budgeting allocates total risk across asset classes, strategies and active management. A common approach sets total volatility or maximum drawdown, then allocates by marginal risk contribution; when correlations rise, a nominally diversified portfolio concentrates in risk terms and needs dynamic review.

Constraints must be executable. Liquidity, leverage, concentration, derivatives, currency and ESG limits need metrics, caps and exception processes; 'moderate' or 'prudent' is not a constraint. Vague constraints produce arbitrary execution.

Rebalancing is the IPS execution layer. Calendar rebalancing is simple but can miss drift; threshold rebalancing is flexible but trades more; combining them is common. Rebalancing must account for transaction costs, taxes and liquidity, not run mechanically daily.

Strategic and tactical must be separated. Strategic asset allocation sets the long-run centre; tactical allocation deviates within limits. Without a separate risk budget and stop rules, tactical becomes momentum chasing.

Stress tests should cover equities and bonds falling together, a rate spike, liquidity freeze, above-target inflation and currency depreciation. Results should feed back into limits and rebalancing rules, not just sit in a report.

Common failures: objectives mismatched to spending, unmeasurable constraints, emotion-driven rebalancing, governance rewriting rules under stress, and backtests treated as evidence. The value of an IPS is that it is followed under stress.

Finally, a good IPS decides 'what to do' in calm times so that stress leaves only execution. It does not guarantee returns, but it reduces behavioural and governance risk.

IPS review checklist

To review an investment policy statement, test whether it constrains real decisions, not whether it reads well:

  1. Are objectives measurable? 'Long-term growth' cannot be assessed; specify nominal or real return, horizon, inflation benchmark and risk tolerance.
  2. Are constraints enforceable? Liquidity needs, legal limits, tax and concentration caps must be auditable; vague constraints get bypassed in stress.
  3. Are rebalancing rules pre-agreed? Thresholds, frequency, trading cost and drift tolerance should be set in calm markets; crisis-time discretion invites chasing.
  4. How is risk budgeted? Total, factor and active risk should be separate; asset weights alone miss leverage and correlation shifts.
  5. Are governance roles clear? Who proposes, approves, executes and reviews; trustees, advisers and the investment committee must not blur.
  6. How are behavioural biases constrained? Chasing, home bias and overtrading need institutional offsets; checklists and cooling-off periods beat willpower.
  7. Does performance evaluation match objectives? Benchmarks, attribution and risk-adjusted return must align with the IPS; a wrong benchmark rewards the wrong behaviour.
  8. When is the IPS reviewed? Objectives, constraints and market assumptions should trigger review; an annual calendar alone misses structural change.

An IPS is valuable in execution, not binding; every clause must map to authority, process and records.

Finally, an IPS is not a decorative document for regulators or clients. The real test is whether it gives an executable next step when markets fall and someone wants to change strategy; if the answer is still 'we will meet and decide', constraints and authority are not yet clear.

Minimum action: put a one-page IPS summary at the top of every investment-committee minute; each deviation records its rationale, horizon and reviewer.

What would change the conclusion

Whether an IPS constrains behaviour depends on objectives and governance:

  1. Objectives conflict. If return, liquidity and risk budget have no priority, stress creates an 'everything matters' stalemate.
  2. Liquidity needs are uncertain. If spending and capital calls cannot be forecast, static rebalancing sells at the worst point.
  3. Governance is captured. If the investment committee is dominated by a founder or CEO, written constraints get bypassed.
  4. Trading costs are high. If rebalancing cost, tax and impact are large, thresholds should widen; frequent adjustment hurts returns.

Practical implication: IPS quality is whether it constrains behaviour under stress; executable clauses on objectives, constraints, rebalancing and governance are worth more than a vision statement.

Data note: risk budgeting must cover total, factor and active risk; adding asset weights alone misses leverage and correlation.

Scope note: separate policy objectives from implementation; objectives can be principled, but process must specify authority, records and review. Otherwise the IPS becomes a vision statement in stress.

Final note: authority and records make the IPS a real constraint; a document without an execution trail is a wish list.

Exercises

  1. List the six core elements of an IPS.

  2. Target 6%, drawdown limit 15%: if equities fall 30%, what is the portfolio drawdown?

  3. In a 60/30/10 portfolio, equities rise to 68%: what is the deviation?

  4. What are the pros and cons of threshold and calendar rebalancing?

  5. What role does the benchmark play in an IPS?

  6. When should the IPS be amended?

  7. True or false: an IPS guarantees returns. Explain.

  8. Why should an IPS start with governance clauses rather than return targets?

  9. Why must rebalancing rules account for trading cost, taxes and liquidity?

Suggested answers

  1. Objectives, constraints, governance, strategic allocation, rebalancing and review. They must be consistent: objectives follow spending, constraints follow risk tolerance, governance decides who can change what.
  2. A 60% stock weight falling 30% cuts the portfolio about 18%, breaching a 15% limit; bonds and alternatives rising would reduce the drawdown.
  3. Stocks rising from 60% to 68% is 8 percentage points absolute, about 13.3% relative, above a common 5% threshold.
  4. Calendar rebalancing is simple and trades less but may act only after large drift; threshold rebalancing is timely but trades more and costs more.
  5. The benchmark defines the investment universe, risk sources and evaluation basis; without it, active value added cannot be judged.
  6. When spending, liabilities, risk tolerance, regulation or organisational goals materially change; not because of short-term underperformance or market fashion.
  7. No. An IPS reduces behavioural and governance risk, not guarantee returns; returns come from markets, costs and execution.
  8. Governance clauses decide who may change rules under stress; without them, objectives and constraints are bypassed by emotion or politics.
  9. Rebalancing has costs: fees, taxes, spreads and market impact; ignoring them overstates the benefit.

Working paper: a four-column check for "The Investment Policy Statement: Writing Objectives, Constraints and Rebalancing into Rules"

The subject of this article is "The Investment Policy Statement: Writing Objectives, Constraints and Rebalancing into Rules". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "Investment policy statement". Column two states the transmission channel of "Rebalancing". Column three states the institutional boundary of "the institutional boundary". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Rebalancing" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. the institutional boundary is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.
