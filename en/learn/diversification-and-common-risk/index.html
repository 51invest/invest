Diversification and Common Risk

Diversification reduces idiosyncratic risk but cannot remove the market and recession risks shared by all assets.

ModuleINV-M11 · 组合与资产配置
LevelFoundation
Read9 min · 13 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

Problem and output

If money is spread across several stocks, bonds or other assets, is risk eliminated? The answer depends on the type of risk and the correlations among assets. Diversification can reduce company-, industry- or region-specific risk, but it cannot remove macro risks shared by all assets. This article presents the two-asset risk formula, shows how correlation, weights and volatility affect a portfolio, and lists the limits of diversification. The SEC guide defines diversification as spreading investments across different assets to reduce risk [1]; what it mainly reduces is idiosyncratic risk, while common risk does not disappear simply because more assets are held — a standard result of portfolio theory.

Inputs and data requirements

  • asset weights that sum to one when leverage and short sales are excluded;
  • assumed volatility or standard deviation for each asset;
  • correlations or covariances among assets;
  • the same return frequency and sample period;
  • fees, taxes, liquidity, trading constraints and horizon.

Correlations must be estimated from a common sample at the same frequency. Splicing different periods or frequencies produces risk figures that are not comparable.

Assumptions and steps

The simplified two-asset portfolio variance is:

σ_p² = w₁²σ₁² + w₂²σ₂² + 2w₁w₂ρσ₁σ₂

where w is a weight, σ a standard deviation and ρ the correlation. Portfolio volatility is σ_p = √σ_p². The formula assumes stable weights and volatilities over the period, an estimable correlation, and a mainly linear covariance relationship.

Worked example

Suppose two assets each have an assumed volatility of 20% — a teaching assumption, not a forecast about real assets — and equal 50% weights. Changing only the correlation gives:

Correlation ρ Portfolio volatility
1.0 20.00%
0.5 17.32%
0.0 14.14%
-1.0 0.00% (theoretical limit)

For ρ = 0.5:

σ_p² = 0.5² × 0.2² + 0.5² × 0.2² + 2 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.2 × 0.2 = 0.03, so σ_p ≈ 17.32%.

With perfect positive correlation, portfolio volatility is the weighted average and diversification offers no risk reduction. Lower correlation usually lowers portfolio volatility. Perfect negative correlation can theoretically produce zero volatility, but correlations change in practice and a historical estimate is not a guarantee.

The SEC guide frames asset allocation as dividing assets according to goals, horizon and risk tolerance, diversification as spreading investments across asset classes to reduce risk, and rebalancing as restoring the target mix [1].

Interpreting the result

  • Diversification is not a one-off decision: once one asset class has risen substantially the portfolio drifts from its intended mix. The SEC guide defines rebalancing as bringing a portfolio back to the original asset allocation mix, so the rule used here is that when the drift exceeds a stated threshold the class that grew is trimmed and the lagging class is bought back to restore the mix[1].

  • Adding low-correlation assets usually lowers total portfolio volatility, and the part removed is mainly risk that does not occur at the same time across assets; how much is removed depends on weights and correlations.

  • When all assets are exposed to the same macro shock, the portfolio can still lose value; diversification does not remove common risk, also called systematic or market risk.

  • Mechanically, if correlations rise during stress, the diversification benefit weakens — this is a standard concern when using historical correlations, and this article does not test it with data; an average historical correlation is not a crisis correlation.

  • The number of holdings is not the only measure: overlapping positions, industry concentration and factor exposures (sensitivity to a shared source of risk, such as interest rates or the overall stock market) can make many products carry essentially the same risk.

The correlation coefficient measures the direction and strength of the linear relationship between two variables and is a key input to portfolio risk (OpenStax) [2].

Failure conditions

  • Correlation estimation error, sample changes and structural breaks (the relationship itself shifting within the sample, for example an inflation shock affecting stocks and bonds differently after a given date) can make realized risk higher than expected.
  • In extreme markets assets can fall together, and a correlation estimated in normal times describes that joint-tail behaviour only partially (the dependence of extreme moves on each other is called tail dependence); a linear correlation does not fully capture joint crashes.
  • Trading costs, taxes, minimum investment sizes, liquidity limits and short-sale constraints restrict diversification.
  • Over-diversification can add management costs and tracking error, and “false diversification” can arise when different products hold the same underlying assets.
  • Diversification does not guarantee positive returns or principal protection; it changes the sources and concentration of risk.

Verification and reproduction

  1. Specify weights, frequency, currency and the common sample.
  2. Compute the correlation matrix; inspect missing values and outliers rather than relying on a single pair.
  3. Compute portfolio risk with the formula or scenarios, then stress-test it.
  4. Examine look-through holdings and industry, region and factor concentration, plus changes in correlation.
  5. Report fees, constraints and estimation error; describe the result as a risk profile, not a return guarantee.

Knowledge-point review

diversification

Definition: Diversification spreads capital across assets, sectors, regions and factors to reduce the impact of any single risk.

Mechanism: Its effect depends on correlation and risk contribution; low-correlation assets cut volatility, but correlations rise in crises.

Example: For example, adding bonds, overseas markets and style exposures reduces single-market shocks.

Boundary: Diversification is not many holdings; homogeneous positions, hidden factors and liquidity concentrate risk, and over-diversification dilutes edge and raises cost.

efficient frontier

Definition: The efficient frontier is the set of portfolios with the highest expected return for a given risk, or lowest risk for a given return.

Mechanism: It depends on expected returns, volatility and correlation; constraints, costs and taxes distort it, and estimation error makes it unreliable.

Example: For example, stock-bond mixes trace a risk-return curve, and the best point depends on goals.

Boundary: The frontier is not a practical optimum; input errors, tail risk and liquidity worsen out-of-sample results, and constraints limit the feasible set.

risk budgeting

Definition: Risk budgeting allocates total portfolio risk across assets, strategies or teams by risk contribution rather than capital.

Mechanism: It can use volatility, VaR or stress loss; correlation, leverage and capacity determine actual contribution.

Example: For example, a risk budget might assign 60 percent to equity, 30 to bonds and 10 to alternatives, not capital weights.

Boundary: Risk budgeting is not precise control; correlation shifts, tails and model error deviate actual risk, and leverage magnifies budget overruns.

strategic and tactical allocation

Definition: Strategic allocation sets long-term target weights, and tactical allocation deviates short term to capture valuation, cycle or event opportunities.

Mechanism: Strategy reflects goals, risk and liabilities; tactics reflect short views and catalysts; deviations need ranges and exit rules.

Example: For example, a 60/40 strategy may tactically underweight equity by 5 points at extreme valuations.

Boundary: Strategy is not fixed forever and tactics are not frequent trading; blurred boundaries cause drift, and short-term timing has low hit rates and high costs.

rebalancing and liability matching

Definition: Rebalancing restores target weights, and liability matching aligns asset cash flows and maturities with future obligations.

Mechanism: Rebalancing controls risk and buys low, liability matching reduces surplus volatility; cash flow, duration and inflation matter.

Example: For example, a pension matches retirement liabilities with long bonds and rebalances quarterly.

Boundary: Rebalancing is not automatic alpha and liability matching is not risk-free; trends make it sell winners, and liability assumptions and inflation can open gaps.

Exercises

  1. Perfect positive correlation. Two assets have 20% volatility, equal weights and correlation 1. What is portfolio volatility? Does diversification reduce risk?
  2. Negative-correlation limit. If correlation is -1, what is theoretical portfolio volatility? Why is it not a practical guarantee?
  3. Common risk. Why does holding more different companies not remove recession risk?
Suggested answers
  1. Portfolio volatility is 20%, the weighted average; perfect positive correlation offers no diversification benefit.
  2. The simplified formula gives 0%, but this requires exact weights, stable perfect negative correlation and executable trades. Correlations change, estimates have error, and costs and constraints exist.
  3. A recession is a common shock that affects the earnings and valuations of most companies. Diversification reduces company-specific risk, not the market-wide exposure.
