The Household Balance Sheet: Human Capital, Housing and Pensions

A household is not just “how much money it has”; it also has future wages, housing, pensions and liabilities. Human capital resembles a bond, housing a local asset, pensions a long-horizon fund; putting them on one sheet shows whether risk is concentrated.

Problem

Is a household with 1 million in financial assets “wealthy”? If 800k is owner-occupied housing, 200k is stock, and the future pension gap is 300k, the answer is not simple. The household balance sheet puts future income, housing, pensions, liabilities and liquidity together to see where risk is concentrated[1].

Inputs

Household members’ ages, income stability, occupation, home value and mortgage, financial assets, pension entitlements, expected retirement spending, debt rates and maturities. Data can come from account statements, pension plan documents, social-security records and household surveys[2].

Assumptions

First, human capital can be estimated as the present value of future income; second, home value correlates with the local economy; third, pension entitlements follow plan rules; fourth, liabilities are split by fixed or floating rates; fifth, the household goal is intertemporal consumption and security, not return maximisation alone[3].

Steps

Step 1, list assets: financial assets, home equity, pension present value, human capital. Step 2, list liabilities: mortgage, consumer loans, other obligations. Step 3, compute net worth. Step 4, compute the pension funded ratio: Assets/PV(Liabilities)Assets / PV(Liabilities)Assets/PV(Liabilities). Step 5, check concentration: are housing, stocks and human capital correlated? Step 6, stress test: job loss, rate rises, home-price falls.

Interpreting results

With pension assets 800 and present value of future spending 1000, the funded ratio is 80% and the gap 200. If the household holds high equity and works in the same industry, human capital and financial assets may move together. When housing is 80% of net worth, the household is more sensitive to local prices and rates.

Failure conditions

First, income assumptions are too optimistic; second, pension rules change; third, housing is illiquid; fourth, debt rates float; fifth, household structure changes; sixth, medical and long-term-care costs are ignored.

Reproduction

Recompute net worth and funded ratio with the same assumptions, reporting financial assets, housing, pensions and liabilities separately; run sensitivity on income, home prices and rates; check whether the result matches actual household cash flow.

Reading and review

The household balance sheet most often omits future cash flows. A 35-year-old household with 800k of financial assets, a 2m home and a 300k mortgage looks like 2.5m of net worth; if the present value of future wages is 6m and the pension gap is 1m, the true risk budget is different. Wages behave like a long-duration bond, but job loss and industry downturns can make them co-move with equities; housing is a local, inflation-sensitive and illiquid asset; pensions are long-term claims with rule risk. To judge concentration, split the three by industry, region and rate sensitivity, then stress job loss, a 20% home-price fall and a two-point rate rise together rather than reading the account balance alone. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Putting the model to work

This section does not repeat the definition. It turns "household balance sheet：human capital、Housing and elderly care" into a judgement sheet a colleague can audit. First, the household balance sheet is divided into three parts: human capital, housing and financial assets, and liabilities and pension obligations. The liquidity and duration of the three blocks are completely different.

  1. Fix the object and the convention. First, the household balance sheet is divided into three parts: human capital, housing and financial assets, and liabilities and pension obligations. The liquidity and duration of the three blocks are completely different. Write the entity, period, unit and control group at the top; every later number must trace back to those four fields.
  2. Keep the middle of the chain. Retesting income shocks: Unemployment, illness, and longevity all change the present value of human capital, and current wages alone cannot reflect risks. Do not keep only the final value; list each multiplier, haircut and timing separately and cite [5][6] here. List each multiplier, haircut and timing separately so the sensitive step is visible.
  3. Stress the assumptions. Finally, do a liquidity stress test: put the necessary expenses, debt maturities and emergency cash in a table for the next 12 months and calculate the shortfall. Change at least one price, one time window and one institutional assumption, then record whether the conclusion flips.

Data and institutional check

Before comparing "Human capital and income risk" with "Housing, liabilities and liquidity", check four things: whether units and denominators match; whether nominal and real, adjusted and unadjusted series have been reconciled; whether the sample is bound by one legal and accounting regime; and whether an independent source can reproduce the order of magnitude.

Three counterexamples

  1. Counterexample 1. Think of housing market value as readily liquidable savings: transaction costs, lending constraints and regional price shocks can discount paper wealth in times of need. Its value is not to reject the whole article but to show where the conclusion starts to need extra evidence.
  2. Counterexample 2. Only pension account balances are counted, not benefit commitments and funded ratios; it is determined that the gap in benefit-based plans may not be on personal bills. Its value is not to reject the whole article but to show where the conclusion starts to need extra evidence.
  3. Counterexample 3. Use average lifespan for retirement planning and ignore longevity risks, the tail of medical expenses and the erosion of fixed pensions by inflation. Its value is not to reject the whole article but to show where the conclusion starts to need extra evidence.

All three point to the same thing: the model is a conditional statement, not an unconditional forecast. State the conditions and the reader knows when to use it and when to stop.

Reading checklist

A household balance sheet also needs a risk-bearer map: the correlation of spousal incomes, the industry of the home town, pension rules and care obligations all change true risk. Layer assets by controllability (liquid financial assets, illiquid home equity, non-tradable pension claims), then stress job loss, illness and long-term care together. Net worth alone is not a decision input.

Further check

Before applying “The Household Balance Sheet: Human Capital, Housing and Pensions”, test the weakest link between “Human capital and income risk” and “Housing, liabilities and liquidity”. Write down the observable proxy, the direction of the causal link and the condition under which the link reverses. Then ask whether the data are measured at the same frequency and definition as the decision, and whether a cost or lag sits between the signal and the outcome. If the chain cannot be stated without hand-waving, the result is a description, not a usable claim. Run one counterexample and one stress case before extending the conclusion to another market, period or population.

Evidence grades: who can check this claim

For "Human capital and income risk", the source can usually support what it is, who publishes it and how it is measured. It cannot automatically support "the same happens in another market". Pull out the third-layer sentences and the reader can see where the risk sits.

For replication, give one person the raw numbers and assumptions and ask them to reach the same order of magnitude. If they cannot, the article is missing a step, not the reader.

Orders of magnitude: rule out arithmetic error first

Before the mechanism, run three checks. First, convert ratios into absolute amounts: 5% of 1,000 and 5% of a trillion are different objects. Second, convert annualised figures into period values: multiplying a quarterly number by four amplifies seasonality and one-offs. Third, convert nominal into real: high inflation can hide a real contraction behind nominal growth.

None of these needs a complex model, only paper and a calculator. They block the common failure of a plausible story with a wrong order of magnitude. Teaching numbers in the article are labelled assumptions; formal citation follows the date, unit and footnote of the source.

Compress the conclusion into three lines

Line one is the conclusion: one sentence with the entity, direction and condition. Line two is the condition: which assumption must hold. Line three is the counterevidence: what data would make you abandon it.

If the three lines cannot be written, the article is still wrapped in background, significance and implications rather than a testable judgement. Hand it to a reader who disagrees and ask them to attack only lines two and three; that is more useful than asking them to repeat the whole text.

This is the house style: checkable facts first, conditional judgement second, and the unknown left on the table rather than covered by a longer explanation.

Exercises

  1. Gap question: pension assets 800, present value of future spending 1000. What is the funded ratio and the gap?
  2. Concentration question: 80% of net worth is in the home and the job is in local real estate. Where is the risk?
  3. Conditions question: list three failure conditions for household allocation and explain how each affects the conclusion.
  4. Explain the causal chain between “Human capital and income risk” and “Housing, liabilities and liquidity” with the article's framework, and name one observable indicator.
  5. Change the base-case number to another value, recompute once, and explain the direction and size of the result.
  6. List at least three boundary conditions and say which one is most often ignored.
  7. Construct a counterexample in which the article's conclusion fails; state the conditions it needs.
  8. If you could keep only one check, which would it be, and how does it prevent the worst error?
  1. Convention stress test. Replace "Human capital and income risk" with another source's definition, list two differences and say whether the conclusion still holds.
  2. Counterfactual. If Housing, liabilities and liquidity moved in the opposite direction, which intermediate quantity changes first and what happens to the conclusion?
  3. Data check. Take one number, find its denominator and base period; if the source omits them, say how you would handle the gap.
  4. Writing exercise. Explain the conclusion to a non-specialist in three sentences, with one condition and one counterexample.
