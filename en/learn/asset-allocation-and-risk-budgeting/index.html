Asset Allocation and Risk Budgeting

Use a two-asset hypothetical example to distinguish capital weights from risk contributions, compute volatility, marginal risk contribution and risk budgets, and show the limits imposed by leverage, correlation and estimation error.

ModuleINV-M11 · 组合与资产配置
LevelSystematic

Problem and output

Asset allocation decides how capital is divided among stocks, bonds, cash and other assets; risk budgeting decides how total risk is distributed across them. The outputs are target weights, risk contributions, constraints and monitoring rules, not the highest-return portfolio. Risk budgeting answers who is using the risk, not who looks largest by capital.

Correlations are a required input because OpenStax explains that diversification reduces firm-specific risk but not systematic risk, and that portfolio volatility is not a simple average of asset volatilities but depends on the volatilities and the correlations, with lower correlation giving greater diversification [2].

Inputs and data requirements

Inputs include volatilities, correlations and the covariance matrix, the investment horizon, liquidity, leverage and concentration limits. Expected returns are optional; risk budgeting does not centre on them. State the frequency, window, currency and missing-data treatment, and do not hide gaps with forward filling. The example uses hypothetical stocks and bonds: volatility 16% and 6%, correlation 0.20, and starting weights 60/40.

Spreading a portfolio across asset classes such as stocks, bonds and cash, sized by investment horizon and risk tolerance, is the SEC investor guide’s definition of asset allocation; the same guide notes that rebalancing returns the portfolio to that allocation and must take trading costs and tax consequences into account [1].

Assumptions

Second moments are stable within the sample; there are no trading costs; rebalancing follows a rule; long-only means no leverage, and any leverage is stated separately; risk is measured by volatility. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Steps

  1. Portfolio variance: σ² = 0.6²×0.16² + 0.4²×0.06² + 2×0.6×0.4×0.20×0.16×0.06 = 0.010714, so σ = 10.35%.
  2. Marginal risk contribution: MRC_i = (Σw)_i/σ. Stocks 0.1558, bonds 0.0250.
  3. Risk contribution: RC_i = w_i×MRC_i. Stocks 9.35 percentage points, bonds 1.00, totalling 10.35.
  4. Risk shares: stocks 90.3%, bonds 9.7%. Capital of 60% carries about 90% of the risk.
  5. For a 70/30 risk budget, the long-only solution is about 38.5% stocks and 61.5% bonds, with volatility falling to 7.78%.
  6. To restore volatility of 10.35%, leverage of about 1.33 times is needed, giving 51.1%/81.9% and a total of 133.0%.
  7. Record the constraints, rebalancing threshold and the monitoring convention for risk contributions.

The formal definition of risk budgeting comes from the arXiv paper: a portfolio strategy in which each asset is to contribute a prespecified amount to the aggregate risk of the portfolio, with risk budgeting portfolios estimated from simulated returns [3].

Interpreting the result

60/40 is a capital ratio, not a risk ratio. A low-volatility, low-correlation asset needs a larger capital weight to contribute the same risk; pure risk budgeting does not take expected returns as an input, so it cannot promise a return or beat 60/40. Covariances change with regime and sample, so risk contributions are estimates; leverage adds financing costs and margin calls. The risk-budget split is itself a governance choice: write down why it was chosen, who approved it and when it will be reviewed.

Failure conditions

  • extrapolating historical covariances
  • correlations often rise in stress and diversification can weaken sharply. This conditional caution is the article's own: none of the three registered sources (SEC, OpenStax 15.2, the arXiv abstract) states that empirical result, and the article does not assert that correlations converge to one.
  • volatility windows that are too short or mix frequencies
  • ignoring tails, non-normality and liquidity
  • treating risk budgets as return forecasts
  • constraints that rule out the theoretical weights
  • budget shares disconnected from goals or liability horizons
  • leverage magnifying losses and forced liquidation

Verification

Use rolling windows and different frequencies; recompute risk contributions under stress; compare with equal weight, 60/40 and target-date funds; check out-of-sample risk shares; report estimation error, turnover and constraint effects; test whether the conclusion depends on the volatility measure.

Reproduction

σ = √(wᵀΣw); MRC_i = (Σw)_i/σ; RC_i = w_i×MRC_i; ΣRC_i = σ. Example: 0.0935 + 0.0100 = 0.1035.

Exercises

  1. Calculation. What is the bond risk share of the 60/40 portfolio?
  2. Reasoning. Why is the stock weight below 50% under a 70/30 risk budget?
  3. Conditions. What happens to total volatility and the bond risk share if correlation rises to 0.80?
Suggested answers
  1. About 9.7%.
  2. Bonds are less volatile, so contributing 30% of risk requires a larger capital weight; stocks are more volatile and contribute most of the risk with less capital.
  3. Total volatility rises from 10.35% to 11.61%, and the bond risk share rises from 9.7% to about 17.9% (stocks fall to about 82.1%).
