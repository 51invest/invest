The Macro Scenario Tree: Probabilities for Growth, Inflation and Policy Paths

A macro forecast is not a point but a scenario tree: growth, inflation and policy each have paths, and probabilities determine expected value and tail risk. Writing scenarios as a tree shows where a forecast can go wrong.

Definition and intuition

A scenario tree decomposes macro outcomes into branches. The first layer is growth (high, base, low), the second inflation (high, low), the third policy (tight, easy), each with a probability and asset response.

A scenario tree does not forecast one future; it says 'if A, then B; if C, then D'. Its value is conditioning: policy response, asset correlations and tail losses sit in one framework.

Steps: identify key variables, set branches, assign probabilities, map asset returns and check consistency. Probabilities need not be precise but must be rankable and weightable[1][2].

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose three scenarios: soft landing (3% growth, 2.5% inflation, neutral policy) with 50% probability and 8% portfolio return; stagflation (1% growth, 4% inflation, tight policy) with 30% and −6%; recession (−1% growth, 1% inflation, easy policy) with 20% and −12%.

Expected return is 0.5×8% + 0.3×(−6%) + 0.2×(−12%) = −0.2%. The expected value is near zero but the tail loss is 12%; with leverage, the tail is larger.

If probabilities shift to 70/20/10, expected return is 2.4%, but stagflation and recession can still cause large drawdowns. Read both expected value and the worst branch, not only the weighted average. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Three checks

Check 1: are tails covered? Covering only soft landing and recession misses stagflation and underprices the policy dilemma; use at least growth, inflation and policy dimensions.

Check 2: do probabilities sum to 100%? If they exceed or fall short, scenarios overlap or leak.

Check 3: is asset mapping reasonable? Stock-bond-commodity correlations change by scenario; do not assume historical correlations hold.

Update the tree regularly: after data, policy and market pricing change, reassess probabilities and branches, or the tree becomes a stale narrative[3][4].

Conditions, boundaries and common mistakes

Boundary 1: probabilities are not exact science. Macro probabilities come from judgement and models and differ across institutions; disclose assumptions and sensitivities.

Boundary 2: scenarios are not forecasts. A tree describes possibilities, not outcomes; the base case is not destiny.

Boundary 3: policy responses are non-linear. Central banks may tighten more than expected when inflation is high and ease more in recession; include 'policy surprise' branches.

Mistake 1: more scenarios are better. Too many branches dilute probability and add complexity; focus on key variables and non-linear risk.

Mistake 2: a positive expected return makes tails irrelevant. Survival depends on the worst scenario; leverage and liquidity amplify tail losses.

Deeper note: scenario trees, conditional probabilities and asset mapping

A scenario tree is not a forecast but structured uncertainty. Choose two or three key variables (growth, inflation, policy), give each three states, and combine them into scenarios; each scenario needs triggers, a time window and observable indicators, not just 'bull/base/bear' labels.

Probabilities must be calibrated. Subjective probabilities are overconfident; anchor on historical frequencies, option-implied distributions, analyst dispersion and macro models, then adjust for current information. Precision is not required, but comparability and updatability are.

Conditional probabilities are more useful than unconditional ones. The probability of higher inflation given higher growth, or lower growth given tighter policy, is what asset mapping needs; treating variables as independent understates tail combinations.

Asset mapping must spell out the transmission chain: growth to earnings, valuation, rates, FX and credit spreads. Which assets gain or lose in each scenario, and which are non-linear; in stagflation, equities and bonds may fall together while commodities and inflation-linked bonds do relatively better.

Update mechanism: adjust probabilities monthly or quarterly with data, and record why; do not change probabilities because prices moved, or change data because the narrative did. Bayesian updating requires priors, likelihoods and posteriors to be written down.

Hedging and positioning: scenario probabilities inform tilts and tail hedges, not all-or-nothing bets. If a scenario's probability rises but is already priced, do not adjust much; if probability is low but loss is large, tail hedges still pay.

Common misuses: treating scenarios as forecasts, making up probabilities, assuming independence, ignoring transmission lags, and rewriting scenarios with hindsight. The value is thinking ahead, not calculating the future.

Finally, connect the tree to the IPS risk budget: each scenario's loss must stay within tolerance; if not, adjust positions or hedges before debating the view.

Scenario-tree review checklist

Review a macro scenario tree for coherent probabilities and mappings, not for elegant branches:

  1. Are branches exhaustive and mutually exclusive? Growth, inflation and policy combinations should cover the main outcomes; omitting 'low growth plus high inflation' understates the tail.
  2. Do probabilities sum to one? Conditional probabilities multiply along the tree; adding branch probabilities directly is a common error.
  3. Where do probabilities come from? Historical frequency, market pricing, expert judgement or a model; calibration differs by source.
  4. Is asset mapping conditional? The same asset earns different returns in different scenarios; specify rates, credit spreads, FX and commodities.
  5. Do correlations shift with the scenario? In crises stocks and bonds can fall together, commodities and the dollar can co-move; calm-period matrices understate losses.
  6. Is policy endogenous? Fiscal and central-bank responses switch between growth and inflation; a fixed policy branch misses feedback.
  7. Are there falsifiable triggers? Each branch needs indicators and review dates; otherwise the tree becomes storytelling.
  8. How is the weighted result used? Report expected return, tail loss and hedge ratio separately; a mean alone hides the path.

Quality lies in calibration, conditional mapping and updatability, not the number of branches.

The review should also record what happened since the last update: which branch occurred, whether probabilities were calibrated and whether asset mappings diverged. Only after scoring does a scenario tree become an improvable tool rather than a forecast story.

Minimum action: give each branch one observable indicator and a review date, update probabilities quarterly, and delete branches without triggers.

Record: keep the prior version and actual outcome at each update so probabilities can be calibrated.

In one line: probabilities must be scorable after the fact.

Note: archive prior versions or calibration is impossible.

What would change the conclusion

A scenario tree works only if probabilities and mappings hold:

  1. Branch probabilities are correlated. If growth and inflation branches are not independent, simple multiplication understates joint probability.
  2. Asset responses are non-linear. If options, credit and liquidity behave non-linearly in extremes, linear mapping mis-sizes hedges.
  3. The policy regime changes. If central-bank or fiscal rules shift, historical conditional probabilities fail.
  4. The tail is missing. If the tree lacks 'low growth plus high inflation plus a credit event', expected return is overstated.

Practical implication: a scenario tree is for preparation, not prediction; every branch needs triggers, asset mapping and a review date, or it is only storytelling.

Data note: label probability sources and update dates; map assets with total return and a common currency to avoid FX and dividend confusion.

Scope note: version the scenario tree; keep the old probabilities, branches and mappings at each update or posterior calibration is impossible.

Final note: version control is part of calibration; without old probabilities, there is no way to tell whether forecasting improved.

Exercises

  1. Build a three-layer growth-inflation-policy scenario tree.

  2. 50% probability 8% return, 30% at −6%, 20% at −12%: what is expected return?

  3. If probabilities become 70/20/10, what is expected return?

  4. Why should the tree cover stagflation?

  5. What does a probability sum other than 100% indicate?

  6. How do policy surprises enter the tree?

  7. True or false: positive expected return makes tails irrelevant. Explain.

  8. How should scenario probabilities be calibrated, and why not rely on subjective judgement alone?

  9. Why can equities and bonds fall together in stagflation?

Suggested answers

  1. Growth (high/mid/low) × inflation (high/mid/low) × policy (loose/mid/tight) gives 27 states; merge into 6-8 economically meaningful scenarios, each with trigger indicators.
  2. Expected return = 0.5×8% + 0.3×(−6%) + 0.2×(−12%) = 4% − 1.8% − 2.4% = −0.2%.
  3. 0.7×8% + 0.2×(−6%) + 0.1×(−12%) = 5.6% − 1.2% − 1.2% = 3.2%.
  4. Stagflation is falling growth with rising inflation, so stocks and bonds can fall together; omitting it understates tail correlation.
  5. Probabilities not summing to 100% mean scenarios are incomplete or a state is missing; fill the gap or put the remainder in 'other'.
  6. Policy surprises as a separate node: hikes/cuts, fiscal expansion/contraction, regulatory change; assign probabilities and triggers.
  7. No. Positive expected return can be offset by a small-probability large loss; watch tail loss, drawdown and risk budget.
  8. Anchor on historical frequency, option-implied distributions, analyst dispersion and macro models, then adjust for current information; pure subjectivity is overconfident.
  9. In stagflation, higher inflation lifts rates and cuts valuations while weaker growth cuts earnings, so both fall; commodities and inflation-linked bonds do relatively better.

Chapter takeaways

  • A scenario tree structures growth, inflation and policy uncertainty; it is not a forecast.
  • Probabilities should be calibrated with history, option-implied distributions and models, then updated.
  • Conditional probabilities map to assets better than unconditional ones.
  • Each scenario needs a transmission chain, winners/losers and tail loss.
  • Scenario losses must fit the IPS risk budget; otherwise adjust positions first.

Working paper: four columns for a checkable judgement

The subject of this article is "INV-038". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "Macro scenarios". Column two states the transmission channel of "Probability weighting". Column three states the institutional boundary of "the institutional boundary". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Probability weighting" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. the institutional boundary is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.
