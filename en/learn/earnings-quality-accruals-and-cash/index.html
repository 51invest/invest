Earnings Quality: Profit, Cash and Accruals

Profit is an accounting measure; cash is an economic one. High accruals and low cash conversion can signal aggressive revenue recognition, working-capital bloat or one-off gains; earnings-quality analysis turns profit back into cash and capital and then tests durability.

Problem

Two companies can report the same 1 billion net profit: one collects 1.2 billion cash, the other only 0.6 billion. Their quality differs. Earnings-quality analysis answers three questions: is profit supported by cash, does growth depend on working-capital expansion, and are one-offs treated as recurring profit[1]?

Inputs

Income statement, balance sheet, cash-flow statement, revenue-recognition policies, receivables ageing, inventory, contract liabilities, capitalisation policies and non-GAAP adjustments. Use at least 3–5 years to see accrual trends; one year is easily distorted by acquisitions or seasonality[2].

Assumptions

First, operating cash flow is harder to manipulate than net income; second, accruals reverse, so high accruals may depress future profit; third, working-capital changes depend on the growth stage; fourth, non-GAAP adjustments must be checked item by item, not accepted wholesale[3].

Steps

Step 1, compute the accrual ratio: Accruals=(NI−CFO)/TAAccruals = (NI - CFO) / TAAccruals=(NI−CFO)/TA. Step 2, compute cash conversion: CFO/NICFO / NICFO/NI. Step 3, decompose working capital: receivables, inventory, payables and contract liabilities. Step 4, check revenue recognition: early recognition or channel stuffing. Step 5, remove non-recurring items and rebuild sustainable profit. Step 6, compare with peers and own history.

Interpreting results

With net income 10, operating cash flow 6 and total assets 100, the accrual ratio is 4% and cash conversion 60%. Positive accruals mean profit exceeds cash, possibly from receivables or inventory; if positive and rising for years, be careful. A cash conversion persistently below 80% suggests weak earnings quality.

Failure conditions

First, the company is expanding fast and working capital naturally rises; second, cash flow is distorted by one-off tax or litigation; third, accounting standards change; fourth, financial, insurance or utility cash-flow definitions differ; fifth, acquisitions make statements incomparable; sixth, management uses non-GAAP adjustments to hide problems.

Reproduction

From the three statements, take net income, operating cash flow, total assets, receivables, inventory and payables, and compute five-year accruals and cash conversion on a consistent basis; reconcile non-GAAP adjustments to the notes; benchmark against peer medians rather than one company.

Reading and review

The gap between profit and cash usually sits in working capital and timing. Net income of 10 against operating cash flow of 6 means 4 of profit did not become cash; if receivables rose by 3, inventory by 2 and payables fell by 1, the gap is explained. Split accruals into reversible and non-reversible: receivables and inventory can reverse, prepayments and depreciation need not. Then strip one-offs: disposals, impairments and litigation gains lift current profit without proving sustainable earnings. A three-year cash-conversion series is more reliable than one year. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Data and institutional check

Before comparing "Accruals and cash conversion" with "Revenue recognition and working capital", check four things: whether units and denominators match; whether nominal and real, adjusted and unadjusted series have been reconciled; whether the sample is bound by one legal and accounting regime; and whether an independent source can reproduce the order of magnitude.

Three counterexamples

  1. Counterexample 1. Think of single-quarter cash flow fluctuations as a proxy for earnings quality: Seasonality, working capital timing, and one-time taxes can all distort CFOs in the short term. Its value is not to reject the whole article but to show where the conclusion starts to need extra evidence.
  2. Counterexample 2. Only look at net profit margin, not cash conversion: under the same profit margin, the higher the accrual, the greater the risk of future revisions. Its value is not to reject the whole article but to show where the conclusion starts to need extra evidence.
  3. Counterexample 3. Adding non-recurring earnings back to "core profits" without accounting for tax shields and sustainability is equivalent to replacing independent judgment with management's caliber. Its value is not to reject the whole article but to show where the conclusion starts to need extra evidence.

All three point to the same thing: the model is a conditional statement, not an unconditional forecast. State the conditions and the reader knows when to use it and when to stop.

Reading checklist

A final accrual check is the cash conversion cycle: receivables days plus inventory days minus payable days. A lengthening cycle can signal weaker bargaining power or slowing demand, even when profit still grows. Compare the cycle with peers and with the company’s own history; a single year can be distorted by seasonality or a large contract.

Further check

Before applying “Earnings Quality: Profit, Cash and Accruals”, test the weakest link between “Accruals and cash conversion” and “Revenue recognition and working capital”. Write down the observable proxy, the direction of the causal link and the condition under which the link reverses. Then ask whether the data are measured at the same frequency and definition as the decision, and whether a cost or lag sits between the signal and the outcome. If the chain cannot be stated without hand-waving, the result is a description, not a usable claim. Run one counterexample and one stress case before extending the conclusion to another market, period or population.

Evidence grades: who can check this claim

For "Accruals and cash conversion", the source can usually support what it is, who publishes it and how it is measured. It cannot automatically support "the same happens in another market". Pull out the third-layer sentences and the reader can see where the risk sits.

For replication, give one person the raw numbers and assumptions and ask them to reach the same order of magnitude. If they cannot, the article is missing a step, not the reader.

Orders of magnitude: rule out arithmetic error first

Before the mechanism, run three checks. First, convert ratios into absolute amounts: 5% of 1,000 and 5% of a trillion are different objects. Second, convert annualised figures into period values: multiplying a quarterly number by four amplifies seasonality and one-offs. Third, convert nominal into real: high inflation can hide a real contraction behind nominal growth.

None of these needs a complex model, only paper and a calculator. They block the common failure of a plausible story with a wrong order of magnitude. Teaching numbers in the article are labelled assumptions; formal citation follows the date, unit and footnote of the source.

Compress the conclusion into three lines

Line one is the conclusion: one sentence with the entity, direction and condition. Line two is the condition: which assumption must hold. Line three is the counterevidence: what data would make you abandon it.

If the three lines cannot be written, the article is still wrapped in background, significance and implications rather than a testable judgement. Hand it to a reader who disagrees and ask them to attack only lines two and three; that is more useful than asking them to repeat the whole text.

This is the house style: checkable facts first, conditional judgement second, and the unknown left on the table rather than covered by a longer explanation.
