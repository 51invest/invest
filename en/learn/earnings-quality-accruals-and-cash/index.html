Earnings Quality: Profit, Cash and Accruals

Profit is an accounting measure; cash is an economic one. High accruals and low cash conversion can signal aggressive revenue recognition, working-capital bloat or one-off gains; earnings-quality analysis turns profit back into cash and capital and then tests durability.

Problem

Two companies can report the same 1 billion net profit: one collects 1.2 billion cash, the other only 0.6 billion. Their quality differs. Earnings-quality analysis answers three questions: is profit supported by cash, does growth depend on working-capital expansion, and are one-offs treated as recurring profit[1]?

Inputs

Income statement, balance sheet, cash-flow statement, revenue-recognition policies, receivables ageing, inventory, contract liabilities, capitalisation policies and non-GAAP adjustments. Use at least 3–5 years to see accrual trends; one year is easily distorted by acquisitions or seasonality[2].

Assumptions

First, operating cash flow is harder to manipulate than net income; second, accruals reverse, so high accruals may depress future profit; third, working-capital changes depend on the growth stage; fourth, non-GAAP adjustments must be checked item by item, not accepted wholesale[3].

Steps

Step 1, compute the accrual ratio: (Accruals = (NI - CFO) / TA). Step 2, compute cash conversion: (CFO / NI). Step 3, decompose working capital: receivables, inventory, payables and contract liabilities. Step 4, check revenue recognition: early recognition or channel stuffing. Step 5, remove non-recurring items and rebuild sustainable profit. Step 6, compare with peers and own history.

Interpreting results

With net income 10, operating cash flow 6 and total assets 100, the accrual ratio is 4% and cash conversion 60%. Positive accruals mean profit exceeds cash, possibly from receivables or inventory; if positive and rising for years, be careful. A cash conversion persistently below 80% suggests weak earnings quality.

Failure conditions

First, the company is expanding fast and working capital naturally rises; second, cash flow is distorted by one-off tax or litigation; third, accounting standards change; fourth, financial, insurance or utility cash-flow definitions differ; fifth, acquisitions make statements incomparable; sixth, management uses non-GAAP adjustments to hide problems.

Reproduction

From the three statements, take net income, operating cash flow, total assets, receivables, inventory and payables, and compute five-year accruals and cash conversion on a consistent basis; reconcile non-GAAP adjustments to the notes; benchmark against peer medians rather than one company.
