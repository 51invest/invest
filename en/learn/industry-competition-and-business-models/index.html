Industry Competition and Business Models

Judge whether profits can persist through industry structure, unit economics and capital returns: start with the profit pool and entry barriers, then compute customer lifetime value and acquisition-cost payback; all example numbers are hypothetical.

LevelSystematic

Problem and output

This analysis asks whether an industry allows excess returns to persist and whether a business model turns revenue into cash and capital returns. Inputs are Form 10-K business and risk disclosures, financial data, industry shares and unit economics. Outputs are an industry map, a business-model table, unit economics, a competitive-advantage judgment, stress tests and falsification conditions. It does not by itself produce a valuation or a recommendation.

Inputs and data requirements

  • Form 10-K Item 1 “Business” requires a description of the company’s business including its main products and services, the subsidiaries it owns and the markets it operates in; competition, regulation, labor issues, special operating costs and seasonal factors are things the section “may also include”, not separately mandated items. Item 1A “Risk Factors” covers the most significant risks, and the guide says companies “generally list the risk factors in order of their importance” [1].
  • Annual report and notes: revenue mix, gross margin, capex, working capital and segments.
  • Industry data: competitors, shares, barriers, prices and capacity, with the market definition and data year stated.
  • Unit economics: customers, ARPU, gross margin, churn, CAC and payback; three to five peers, five years.

Assumptions

A subscription software company has 1,000 customers, $10,000 annual ARPU, 80% gross margin, 10% annual churn and $30,000 CAC. Each customer generates $8,000 annual gross profit; CAC payback 3.75 years, LTV $80,000, LTV/CAC 2.67.

Steps

  1. Define the market and unit; avoid a narrow definition that inflates share.
  2. Map structure: competitors, shares, capacity, price wars, bargaining power, substitutes and barriers; the textbook defines monopolistic competition as “many firms competing against each other, but selling products that are distinctive in some way”, and a monopolistic competitor earning profits “must expect the entry of firms with similar, but differentiated, products” [3]; the statement that “oligopoly has higher entry barriers” is this article's own four-way market-structure classification - section 10.1 contains no discussion of oligopoly (the word occurs 0 times) - so it carries no citation.
  3. Describe the model: who pays, for what, pricing, recurrence, costs and capital intensity.
  4. Calculate unit economics: LTV = ARPU × gross margin / churn (undiscounted, with 1 / churn as the average customer life); CAC payback = CAC / (ARPU × gross margin), in years, without churn or discounting; LTV/CAC = LTV / CAC; ROIC = NOPAT / invested capital.
  5. Test the advantage: compare gross margin, ROIC and cash conversion with peers across the cycle; trace it to cost, differentiation, switching costs, network effects, intangible assets or scale.
  6. Stress: price -10%, churn 15%, CAC +20%, input costs +10%; see what breaks. The example provides no cost base, so the +10% cost shock is only a checklist item, not a numeric demonstration; actual payback also lengthens with churn.
  7. Falsify: two consecutive years of falling share, a 5 percentage-point gross-margin decline or payback above 4 years means redoing the judgment.

Example

A 10% price cut takes ARPU to $9,000 and annual gross profit to $7,200; when churn rises 5 percentage points to 15%, LTV falls to $48,000, LTV/CAC to 1.60 and payback rises to 4.17 years. Revenue down 10% and churn up 5 percentage points are enough to worsen the unit economics. Taking CAC alone, a 20% increase moves it from $30,000 to $36,000: payback becomes 36,000 / 8,000 = 4.50 years and LTV/CAC 80,000 / 36,000 = 2.22, already past the 4-year falsification line; combine the price cut with the higher CAC and LTV/CAC falls further to 48,000 / 36,000 = 1.33 with payback of 36,000 / 7,200 = 5.00 years. Coca-Cola's 2025 Form 10-K shows a different model: its concentrate operations sell beverage concentrates to authorized bottlers, and the filing names PepsiCo a primary competitor [2]. Competition ultimately shows up in price, churn and acquisition cost, not in share alone - this article's judgment. “Our concentrate operations typically generate net operating revenues by selling beverage concentrates, sometimes referred to as “beverage bases,” syrups, including fountain syrups, and certain finished beverages to authorized bottling operations (to which we typically refer as our “bottlers” or our “bottling partners”)”; the filing's competitor sentence reads: “In many of the countries in which we do business, PepsiCo, Inc. is a primary competitor. Other significant competitors include, but are not limited to, Nestlé S.A., Keurig Dr Pepper Inc., Danone S.A., Suntory Beverage & Food Limited, Anheuser-Busch InBev, Kirin Holdings, Heineken N.V., Diageo plc and Red Bull GmbH” [2].

Interpreting the result

Industry structure sets the size and stability of the profit pool; the business model determines how much of it a company captures and how much capital it needs. High share does not guarantee high returns: with price competition, capital intensity or strong bargaining power on either side of the industry, share can be low-return scale. A niche leader with high switching costs and low capital needs can earn a higher ROIC. Because ROIC = after-tax operating margin × capital turnover, asset-light and asset-heavy models cannot be compared on gross margin alone. The final evidence is ROIC above the cost of capital across the cycle, with cash flow keeping up with profit.

Failure conditions

  • the market is defined too narrowly or too broadly, so shares and rivalry are not comparable
  • management adjectives are used in place of share, price and ROIC data
  • gross margins are compared across accounting policies without adjusting for capitalization or leases
  • capex and working capital are ignored, so growth is treated as value
  • a single year is read as a cyclical peak, and private or import competitors are ignored
  • churn is left undefined in the LTV/CAC ratio, time units are mixed, or no falsification condition is set

Verification

  1. Recompute revenue as customers × ARPU and payback from gross profit and CAC.
  2. Compare five-year ROIC and cash conversion across three to five peers after putting them on the same accounting basis.
  3. Change price, churn and CAC one at a time; check whether the conclusion flips.
  4. Check share with two sources and test whether the cycle or regulation changes barriers.

Knowledge-point review

industry structure

Definition: Industry structure covers firm numbers, concentration, entry barriers, bargaining with suppliers and buyers, and substitution.

Mechanism: Five forces, life cycle and regulation shape the profit pool; concentration does not guarantee profit, and competition and capacity discipline matter.

Example: For example, airlines have high entry barriers but are capital-intensive and price-competitive, so profits are volatile.

Boundary: Structure is not static; technology, regulation and globalisation shift boundaries, and concentration alone does not equal profitability.

business model

Definition: A business model explains how a firm creates value, whom it charges, its cost structure and cash cycle.

Mechanism: Revenue model, acquisition, retention, pricing power and capital intensity determine scalability and cash conversion; subscriptions, platforms and projects differ.

Example: For example, software subscriptions invest in acquisition then charge recurring fees, so retention and cash flow drive value; construction recognises revenue by progress.

Boundary: A business model is not a story; unit economics, customer lifetime value, working capital and imitation determine durability, and growth can burn cash.

competitive advantage

Definition: Competitive advantage is a firm's durable ability to beat rivals through lower cost, higher value or stronger lock-in.

Mechanism: Cost advantage comes from scale, technology and supply chains; differentiation from brand, networks and switching costs; it must be defensible and cash-generative.

Example: For example, a cosmetics brand can charge a premium, but heavy advertising may prevent the advantage from becoming profit.

Boundary: Advantage is not permanent; substitution, regulation, habits and imitation erode it, and a moat can be a cycle or accounting illusion.

corporate governance

Definition: Corporate governance allocates rights, oversight and incentives among shareholders, boards, managers and stakeholders.

Mechanism: Boards, audit, disclosure, pay and voting protect capital; controlling holders, dual-class shares and related-party deals are key risks.

Example: For example, an independent audit committee constrains related-party transactions and protects minority shareholders.

Boundary: Governance is not just attendance and bylaws; family control, political ties and informal incentives shape decisions, and formal independence can be captured.

capital allocation and KPIs

Definition: Capital allocation is management's choice among capex, M&A, R&D, dividends, buybacks and debt repayment; KPIs measure operating efficiency.

Mechanism: ROIC, incremental returns, cash flow, unit cost and retention determine allocation quality, and KPIs should match strategy and cost of capital.

Example: For example, a firm using free cash flow to buy back undervalued shares may raise per-share value if returns exceed project investment.

Boundary: More KPIs are not better; accounting metrics can be managed, growth alone ignores capital cost, and allocation is distorted by incentives and empire building.

Exercises

  1. Concept. Why does high market share not guarantee high ROIC?
  2. Calculation. Using the example numbers, what are LTV/CAC and the payback period in the base case? What do they become after a 10% price cut and churn of 15%?
  3. Applicability. Which measures matter for a low-margin retailer versus software?
Suggested answers
  1. Profit pool, capital intensity, price competition and bargaining power may prevent scale from becoming returns; share is not a moat.
  2. Base: LTV $80,000, LTV/CAC 2.67, payback 3.75 years. After: LTV $48,000, LTV/CAC 1.60, payback 4.17 years.
  3. Retail needs inventory turnover, gross margin and working capital; software needs churn, CAC payback, LTV/CAC and retention.
