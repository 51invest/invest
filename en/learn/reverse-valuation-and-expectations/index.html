Reverse Valuation and Expectations

Treat the market price as given and solve for the growth or return it implies, then test whether those expectations are credible; all example numbers are hypothetical.

Problem and output

Reverse valuation treats the market price as known and solves for the growth, margin or discount rate it implies. It answers "what is the market assuming, and is that credible?" The output is a set of implied expectations, falsification conditions and a range, not a target price or a buy/sell recommendation. It turns "is it expensive?" into "what must the company achieve?"

Inputs and data requirements

The current price, diluted shares, net debt, normalized FCF or dividends, an explicit discount rate and terminal growth rate, and a forecast horizon; historical data on industry volume, price, share, margin and reinvestment. Align the price and statements to the same date, and avoid stale, illiquid or single-trade-distorted prices. If the company pays no dividend, use FCF rather than dividends; if the two differ materially, explain why. Record the date, currency and unit for every input.

Assumptions

The example is hypothetical, with all amounts in one illustrative currency: a share price of 40, 100 shares, net debt of 0, so enterprise value (EV) equals the market value of 4,000; base FCF of 200; a 5-year forecast in which FCF grows at a constant g and then at 2.5% in perpetuity; a discount rate of 9%. Only g is solved for; all other inputs are fixed. Cash flows arrive at year-end. The 9% and 2.5% are both nominal; converting to a real basis would require restating FCF and perpetual growth together, or the two are not comparable.

The forward model used here follows OpenStax: each period’s cash flow and the terminal value are discounted at the required return, so terminal value, discount rate and growth jointly determine value [2]. The Gordon growth model and the observation that valuation is highly sensitive to growth and the required return come from the same section and underpin the terminal-growth and sensitivity steps [1].

Steps

  1. Normalize base FCF: remove one-off items and abnormal working-capital swings, and deduct maintenance capex.
  2. Write the forward model: EV = Σ FCF0(1+g)^t/(1+r)^t + TV/(1+r)^5, with TV = FCF5(1+2.5%)/(9%-2.5%).
  3. Set EV equal to 4,000 and solve for g by bisection or a spreadsheet goal-seek; the result is about 8.1%.
  4. Translate g into operating assumptions: revenue growth comes from volume and price; then check whether margin and reinvestment can support it.
  5. Compare with history, the industry and peers: if industry volume grows 3% and price 2%, 8.1% requires roughly 3 extra points of share gain or margin improvement. This step is the article's own judgement, and it holds only if FCF grows with revenue - that is, with the FCF margin and the reinvestment ratio unchanged. Recomputed against this article's model on 3 November 2026, the implied g is 8.09% at r = 9% and EV 4,000 (11.71% at r = 10%, 2.84% at a share price of 32 and 12.53% at 48), versus industry revenue growth of 1.03 x 1.02 - 1 = 5.06%, a gap of 3.03 percentage points - which is where the 3 points come from. Loosen the premise and the gap changes: a 1% relative fall in the FCF margin each year would require 9.18% revenue growth, and 2% each year would require 10.30%.
  6. Sensitize: if r rises from 9% to 10%, implied g is about 11.7%; at a share price of 32 or 48, implied g is about 2.8% or 12.5%.
  7. Write falsification conditions: the conclusion fails if share, gross margin, capex or discount-rate assumptions are disproved.

Reading and review

Reverse valuation does not forecast price; it infers what the current price already believes. Given price, cash flow and discount rate, solve for implied growth or margin, then compare it with long-run industry growth, returns on capital and competition. If implied growth is far above a sustainable level, the price depends on further upgrades; if it is below a conservative estimate, a margin of safety may exist. Use it with scenarios, not as one-point certainty. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Interpreting the result

The 8.1% is the five-year FCF growth implied by the price, not a forecast. It may be reasonable or too optimistic; this article draws no conclusion about the example company. If 8.1% is well above long-run industry growth, the price depends more heavily on unproven share gains. The value of reverse valuation is that it turns a vague "expensive" into a testable question: can the company deliver this volume, price and return? When terminal value is a large share of the total, the answer is sensitive to r and perpetual growth, so report the range rather than one implied rate.

Damodaran’s notes state that intrinsic value rests on expected cash flows, growth potential and risk, with DCF discounting those expectations to a present value, while relative valuation defers to how the market prices similar assets; the reverse solve in this article belongs to the first family [3].

Failure conditions

  • FCF is negative or highly volatile, so the model has no solution or multiple solutions
  • r is below or close to perpetual growth
  • the price is stale, illiquid or manipulated
  • accounting profit is used instead of cash flow, or shares and dilution are ignored
  • several variables are solved at once, turning assumptions into conclusions
  • the market price is treated as intrinsic value, or the implied value is treated as a forecast

Verification

Plug the solved g back into the model and confirm it returns 4,000; cross-check with a DDM or an exit multiple; test the result against a bottom-up revenue, margin and capex model; sensitize r, perpetual growth and the forecast horizon; finally, compare with independent industry data.

Reproduction

Record the price date, shares, net debt, base FCF, r, perpetual growth, forecast horizon and solver. Formulas: EV = Σ FCF_t/(1+r)^t + TV/(1+r)^n; TV = FCF_n(1+g∞)/(r-g∞). Use bisection or a spreadsheet single-variable solve, with the error kept within 0.1 percentage point.

Decision sheet: reverse valuation, implied growth and market expectations on one table

This portfolio sheet starts with constraints, not views. For “Reverse Valuation and Expectations”, ask what the liabilities, horizon, tax base and tradable universe are, and in which account and horizon “reverse valuation” and “implied growth” are compared. This is a method article: write the inputs, steps and failure conditions in full.

First, the scope of reverse valuation: total return or price return, before or after tax, local currency or foreign currency? “treating correlation as causation” usually puts returns from different accounts and horizons into one table. Align currency, fees and rebalancing frequency before ranking.

Next, the relationship between implied growth and market expectations: how much risk budget, correlation, liquidity and trading cost change, and which portfolio line absorbs the effect. If a link is only an assumption, mark it; do not present it as a realised fact.

INV-011: Finally, the refutation: if correlation rises, liquidity falls or after-tax return is eaten by fees, does the advantage of reverse valuation survive? Write the switch condition into the sheet, so you know when to trim, switch or do nothing.

INV-011: Bring liabilities and horizon back. Long liabilities with short-duration assets, or floating liabilities with fixed income, can make an “average return” fail at the wrong moment. The sheet needs a cash-flow maturity ladder, not only expected return and volatility.

INV-011: If only one line can survive, keep the rebalancing rule for market expectations: which signal triggers, how often to check, and the trading-cost cap. Compress the rest, but not this line; it decides whether the allocation can be executed consistently.

Boundary case: A 40-yuan price implies 4% growth; at 3%, what is it worth?

With 100 shares, zero net debt and a market value of 4,000, base free cash flow is 200 and the discount rate is 9%. Using a simple perpetual-growth formula, EV = FCF × (1 + g) ÷ (r - g). A 40-yuan price corresponds to EV of 4,000; solving gives g ≈ 4.0% (200 × 1.04 ÷ (9% - 4%) ≈ 4,160, close to 4,000). At g = 3%, value is about 200 × 1.03 ÷ 6% = 3,433; at g = 5%, about 5,250. The market price is unchanged, but moving the implied growth assumption from 4% to 3% cuts value by about 14%.

The conclusion reverses when free cash flow and the discount rate move together: if slower growth comes with lower capital spending and higher free cash flow, value need not fall; if risk raises the discount rate, the decline is larger. The boundary is that reverse valuation translates price into expectations, not a price forecast. The simple perpetual formula is unsuitable for high-growth or volatile cash-flow companies.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not a valuation conclusion.

Reverse valuation must also test whether the implied assumptions are achievable. If implied growth exceeds the industry's long-run rate, the market needs a reason to pay for it; if implied margin exceeds peer ceilings, the competitive moat needs explanation. After translating price into expectations, the next question is what conditions those expectations require.
