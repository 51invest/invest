Choosing an Investment Benchmark

Problem and output

A benchmark answers two questions: whether the portfolio carries the risk that was planned, and what “good” is measured against. Inputs are objectives and constraints, holdings, and candidate index methodologies. The output is a benchmark statement fixing the covered market, construction rules, weighting, rebalancing schedule, return convention and change rules.

Inputs and data requirements

  • objectives, horizon, liquidity needs and risk capacity (see INV-001)
  • holdings, weights, currency and valuation date
  • the index methodology document and its version date
  • return convention: price index or total return including dividends
  • fee basis and rate, plus the tracking difference of the tracking product
  • comparison frequency and sample period, covering at least one rebalancing cycle
  • data source, license and retrieval date

Assumptions

The example assumes one currency and one calendar year; a 0.60% annual portfolio fee deducted from gross return; an index fund with a 0.20% fee and a -0.15% tracking difference as the proxy; no taxes; example returns on a price basis with dividends not considered; and teaching assumptions rather than forecasts.

Steps

  1. Write the goals before the benchmark. The benchmark should cover the market and risks the portfolio holds, not the index that rose most.
  2. Read the methodology. Record the universe, eligibility rules, weighting method, weight caps and rebalancing frequency.
  3. Find an investable proxy. An index cannot be invested in directly (see MKT-005); funds or derivatives track it, so fees and tracking difference must be included.
  4. Align conventions. Currency, period, frequency, price or total return, and before or after fees must match.
  5. Compute the relative difference. excess = (1 + r_p) / (1 + r_b) - 1, and record the standard deviation of monthly relative returns as tracking error.
  6. Write the conditions for changing the benchmark in advance. Underperformance alone is not one.

Example

Assume a portfolio earns 8.2% gross, so 8.2%-0.60% = 7.6% net; the benchmark index returns 7.5% on a price basis; the proxy fund returns 7.5%-0.20%-0.15% = 7.15% net.

  • versus the proxy fund: 1.076 / 1.0715 - 1 = 0.42%
  • versus the index itself: 1.076 / 1.075 - 1 = 0.09%

The gap is about 0.33 percentage points. If 0.30 percentage points of trading costs were omitted, 7.6% becomes 7.9%, and the comparison becomes 1.079 / 1.0715 - 1 = 0.70%. In this article's judgment, consistent bases matter more than the headline number.

Interpreting the result

A benchmark is a ruler; changing it changes the conclusion. The base definition of indexes and benchmarks is in MKT-005; the SEC states "You cannot invest directly in a market index, but because index funds track a market index they provide an indirect investment option." and that an index fund "An index fund may underperform its index because of fees and expenses, trading costs, and tracking error." [1]. Construction drives exposure (this article's judgment): the Nasdaq-100 is designed to measure the performance of 100 of the largest Nasdaq-listed non-financial companies. The Index employs a modified market capitalization weighting scheme. It runs a December annual reconstitution, which includes a rebalance, plus March, June and September rebalances, and its concentration limits work in two tiers: at company level, "If any company's initial weight exceeds 24%" the weights are cut so no company exceeds 20%, and companies above 4.5% are capped at a 40% aggregate if their sum reaches 48%; at security level, stage 1 caps any security above 15% at 14%, and stage 2 reduces a top-five aggregate of "40% or greater" to 38.5% [2]. FTSE Russell builds its Russell US Indexes modularly by segment and style, rolls sub-indexes up to the Russell 3000, markets them as benchmarks, and moves reconstitution from annual to semi-annual in 2026 [3]. Confirm that constituents, weights and rebalancing rules fit the portfolio before comparing.

Failure conditions

  • a benchmark that does not match the portfolio, such as small-cap against large-cap
  • a price index compared with a portfolio that receives dividends, or the reverse
  • a gross index compared with a net portfolio, omitting trading costs and taxes
  • changing the benchmark after underperformance, or showing only the best of several
  • a sample shorter than one rebalancing cycle, or mixing monthly and annual frequencies
  • ignoring methodology changes, such as the 2026 move to semi-annual Russell US reconstitution
  • reading tracking error as stock-selection skill

Verification

  1. Recompute against a second, closely related benchmark.
  2. Recompute on a total-return basis to see the effect of dividends.
  3. Confirm the sample covers at least one reconstitution and one rebalance.
  4. Have a second person recompute from the same data.

Reproduction

The benchmark statement records the index name and version date, provider, universe, weighting method and caps, rebalancing dates, return convention, proxy product and fee, tracking difference, comparison frequency and change conditions. Relative difference and tracking error use the formulas above; missing months are dropped and logged.

Working paper: a four-column check for "Choosing an Investment Benchmark"

The subject of this article is "Choosing an Investment Benchmark". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "investment benchmark". Column two states the transmission channel of "index methodology". Column three states the institutional boundary of "tracking error". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "index methodology" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. tracking error is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Boundary case: A 60/40 portfolio against a 50/50 benchmark: tracking difference is not skill

A portfolio holds 60% stocks and 40% bonds; the benchmark holds 50% and 50%. If stocks rise 20% and bonds fall 5%, the portfolio returns 0.6 × 20% + 0.4 × (-5%) = 10%, while the benchmark returns 0.5 × 20% + 0.5 × (-5%) = 7.5%, so the portfolio leads by 2.5 points. If the benchmark is changed to 60/40, its return is 10% and the portfolio matches it. If the fund charges 0.5% and the benchmark 0.1%, the net gap narrows by another 0.4 points.

The conclusion reverses when the benchmark is not investable or uses different construction rules: if it holds assets the portfolio cannot own or uses a different tax convention, tracking difference cannot be attributed to skill. The boundary is that a benchmark first defines the risk budget and only then defines good or bad. Replacing the original benchmark with a better-performing index turns benchmark choice into an ex-post story.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not portfolio performance.

Benchmark comparison also depends on currency, tax and rebalancing rules. A local-currency versus foreign-currency gap may come from exchange rates; pre-tax versus after-tax differences may come from tax rates; different rebalancing frequencies change risk exposure. Align these conventions before judging whether active management adds value.

Reading and review

Choosing a benchmark is choosing whom to compare with, what to compare and over what horizon. A broad index measures market beta, a style index measures style exposure and a custom benchmark fits a multi-asset portfolio. Construction, rebalancing, tax treatment and fees all affect the comparison; comparing a total-return portfolio with a price index overstates active management. The benchmark should be investable and replicable, or relative return has no economic meaning. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.
