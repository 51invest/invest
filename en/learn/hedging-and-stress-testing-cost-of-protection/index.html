Hedging and Stress Testing: The Cost of Protection

Hedging does not remove risk; it changes its shape. Futures lock in a price but give up upside; options pay a premium but keep some upside. Stress testing asks whether protection still works in extreme scenarios.

ModuleINV-M12 · 对冲与压力
LevelSystematic
Read2 min · 11 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

Problem

Hedging is not about whether risk exists but which risk you want to keep. An airline locking fuel cost can use futures; an exporter locking FX can use forwards; a portfolio wanting upside but limited downside can buy protective puts. Every tool changes the shape of risk and brings basis, premium or liquidity costs[1].

Inputs

Exposure to hedge, beta or sensitivity, contract size and margin, basis or premium, correlation, liquidity, price shocks under stress. Also the hedge horizon and cash-flow timing; maturity mismatch leaves a gap when rolling[2].

Assumptions

First, correlation between hedge and exposure is stable; second, basis or premium is observable; third, margin and liquidity are sufficient; fourth, stress scenarios cover extreme but feasible jumps; fifth, hedge accounting and tax do not change the economic result[3].

Steps

Step 1, measure exposure: spot value (V), beta (\beta). Step 2, compute hedge ratio: (h = \beta V / F), where (F) is notional per contract. Step 3, choose the tool: futures, forwards, options or natural hedge. Step 4, compute cost: basis, premium, margin and roll. Step 5, stress test: price jumps, correlation rises, liquidity disappears. Step 6, monitor residual risk and basis changes.

Interpreting results

With portfolio value 100, beta 1.2 and futures notional 100, (h = 1.2) contracts; selling 1.2 futures brings market exposure near 0. A protective put with strike 90 and premium 3 caps downside at about 13% (10% price fall plus 3% premium) but keeps upside minus the premium. Hedging is not free insurance.

Failure conditions

First, correlation differs from assumptions; second, basis is unfavourable at roll; third, option premium eats returns; fourth, margin calls force liquidation; fifth, stress scenarios miss liquidity gaps; sixth, the hedge instrument itself carries credit or settlement risk.

Reproduction

Use historical prices and exposure to compute return distributions before and after hedging, reporting basis, premium and margin costs separately; set stress scenarios for price jumps, volatility spikes and correlation reversals; check hedge-ratio stability across windows.

Exercises

  1. Hedge question: portfolio 100, beta 1.2, futures notional 100 per contract. How many contracts to sell? What if beta falls to 0.8?
  2. Cost question: protective put premium 3, strike 90. What is maximum downside, and how does upside change?
  3. Conditions question: list three failure conditions for stress testing and explain how each changes protection.
