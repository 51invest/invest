Hedging and Stress Testing: The Cost of Protection

Hedging does not remove risk; it changes its shape. Futures lock in a price but give up upside; options pay a premium but keep some upside. Stress testing asks whether protection still works in extreme scenarios.

Problem

Hedging is not about whether risk exists but which risk you want to keep. An airline locking fuel cost can use futures; an exporter locking FX can use forwards; a portfolio wanting upside but limited downside can buy protective puts. Every tool changes the shape of risk and brings basis, premium or liquidity costs[1].

Inputs

Exposure to hedge, beta or sensitivity, contract size and margin, basis or premium, correlation, liquidity, price shocks under stress. Also the hedge horizon and cash-flow timing; maturity mismatch leaves a gap when rolling[2].

Assumptions

First, correlation between hedge and exposure is stable; second, basis or premium is observable; third, margin and liquidity are sufficient; fourth, stress scenarios cover extreme but feasible jumps; fifth, hedge accounting and tax do not change the economic result[3].

Steps

Step 1, measure exposure: spot value VVV, beta β\betaβ. Step 2, compute hedge ratio: h=βV/Fh = \beta V / Fh=βV/F, where FFF is notional per contract. Step 3, choose the tool: futures, forwards, options or natural hedge. Step 4, compute cost: basis, premium, margin and roll. Step 5, stress test: price jumps, correlation rises, liquidity disappears. Step 6, monitor residual risk and basis changes.

Interpreting results

With portfolio value 100, beta 1.2 and futures notional 100, h=1.2h = 1.2h=1.2 contracts; selling 1.2 futures brings market exposure near 0. A protective put with strike 90 and premium 3 caps downside at about 13% (10% price fall plus 3% premium) but keeps upside minus the premium. Hedging is not free insurance.

Failure conditions

First, correlation differs from assumptions; second, basis is unfavourable at roll; third, option premium eats returns; fourth, margin calls force liquidation; fifth, stress scenarios miss liquidity gaps; sixth, the hedge instrument itself carries credit or settlement risk.

Reproduction

Use historical prices and exposure to compute return distributions before and after hedging, reporting basis, premium and margin costs separately; set stress scenarios for price jumps, volatility spikes and correlation reversals; check hedge-ratio stability across windows.

Reading and review

Hedging cost has three layers: basis, premium and margin. Futures lock price, but basis moves with transport, inventory and delivery month; options pay a premium for asymmetric protection, and time value decays as volatility falls; margin can jump in stress and force an early close. A portfolio with beta 1.2, spot 100 and futures 100 per contract can sell 1.2 contracts to cut beta near zero, but basis and correlation risk remain. Stress the price, correlation and liquidity together, not only a 10% price fall. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Reading checklist

Hedging also differs between accounting and economics: futures gains and losses may hit current earnings while the spot value is not recognised at the same time. An economically effective hedge can still make reported profit volatile. Design the hedge with cash flow, statements and taxes in view, not beta alone.

Further check

Before applying “Hedging and Stress Testing: The Cost of Protection”, test the weakest link between “Futures hedging and basis” and “Option protection and premium”. Write down the observable proxy, the direction of the causal link and the condition under which the link reverses. Then ask whether the data are measured at the same frequency and definition as the decision, and whether a cost or lag sits between the signal and the outcome. If the chain cannot be stated without hand-waving, the result is a description, not a usable claim. Run one counterexample and one stress case before extending the conclusion to another market, period or population.

One last check

Finally, check who bears the residual risk when the hedge fails: the treasury desk, the operating unit or the shareholder. A hedge that transfers risk inside the firm without disclosure is not risk reduction; it is risk relocation.

Working paper: a four-column check for "Hedging and Stress Testing: The Cost of Protection"

The subject of this article is "Hedging and Stress: The Cost of Protection". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "Futures hedging and basis". Column two states the transmission channel of "Option protection and premium". Column three states the institutional boundary of "Stress scenarios and residual risk". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Option protection and premium" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. Stress scenarios and residual risk is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Review sentence

Write one sentence only: "Under the ____ convention, a change in ____ affects ____ through ____; if ____ appears, the claim fails." The four blanks are the unit, the variable, the channel and the falsifier. If the sentence cannot be written, at least one of the four columns is still empty; if it can be written but cannot be observed, the falsifier is wrong. On every data update, reread this sentence before reading the charts.

Boundary case: Beta of 1.2 versus 0.8: a 0.4 difference in the hedge ratio

Spot exposure is 10 million yuan and each futures contract has a notional value of 1 million. At beta 1.2, the hedge ratio is 1.2 × 10 ÷ 1 = 12 contracts; at beta 0.8, it is 8. If price falls 20%, 12 short futures gain 2.4 million while spot loses 2 million, a net gain of 0.4 million; 8 contracts gain only 1.6 million, a net loss of 0.4 million. If basis weakens by 1%, futures gain 0.1 million less and the 12-contract net gain falls to 0.3 million.

The conclusion reverses when correlation rises or liquidity disappears: in stress, futures and spot may diverge and the beta estimate fails; options preserve upside but a 5% premium drags returns. The boundary is that the hedge ratio is an estimate, not a guarantee. Futures lock prices, options insure, and natural hedges differ in cost and residual risk.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not a hedging plan.
