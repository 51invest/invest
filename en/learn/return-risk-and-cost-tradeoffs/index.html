Return, Risk and Cost Trade-offs

Compare net nominal return, risk and costs on one basis, using scenario ranges instead of a single expected value; all example numbers are hypothetical.

Problem and output

Advertisements often quote a single return, but the outcome depends on how much risk you take, how much you pay in costs, and how much purchasing power inflation erodes. This method compares alternatives on one basis: net return, risk and cost. The output is a comparison table and scenarios, not a verdict on the “best product.”

Inputs and data requirements

  • nominal return and time unit
  • inflation or a price index
  • fees: management, commissions, spreads, custody
  • taxes and payment timing
  • risk: scenario range, historical volatility, maximum drawdown
  • liquidity, horizon and minimum holding
  • data source, sample period and fee basis

All inputs must use the same currency, horizon and tax treatment. Historical returns are not forecasts.

Assumptions

The example assumes a one-year holding period, a starting amount of 100,000 yuan (CNY), 2.5% inflation, a 1.0 percentage point annual fee deducted directly from the nominal return, no taxes, and scenarios rather than predictions. All figures below are in yuan and are teaching assumptions, not the actual performance of any product. Actual fees may be charged on ending or average assets; check the product documents. A 1.0% fee on ending assets gives 100,000 × 1.07 × (1 - 1%) = 105,930, a net nominal return of 5.93% rather than the 6.0% you get by subtracting percentage points.

Steps

  1. State the gross nominal return and time unit.
  2. Subtract fees and taxes to get net nominal return; a fee is a cost, not a market decline.
  3. Convert to real: r_real = (1 + r_net) / (1 + π) - 1, where r_net is net nominal return. For low inflation, r_real ≈ r_net - π, but check the error. The derivation and the error discussion are in the main article Real Returns and Purchasing Power (INV-003); this article only uses the result.
  4. Use at least three scenarios (down, base, up), plus maximum drawdown and liquidity limits.
  5. Compare return per unit of risk, not the headline return. The convention used here is fixed as “same holding period, same return definition: ratio = mean / standard deviation”. This is the article's own quotient and does not subtract a risk-free rate, so it is not a Sharpe ratio. Computed from source 3's Table 12.4 (nominal arithmetic average annual returns and their standard deviations, 1981–2020): large company stocks 12.64 / 16.06 = 0.787, Baa bonds 10.34 / 7.67 = 1.348, US T-bills 3.94 / 3.39 = 1.162[3]. That both bond rows beat the equity row already warns that the quotient is dominated by its denominator, and that table is nominal: it cannot be compared with this article's net real return unless both sides use the same nominal-or-real definition and the same one-year horizon.
  6. Costs are nearly certain; returns are not.

Example

Hypothetical plan A has a gross nominal annual return of 7.0%, a fee of 1.0 percentage point, a net nominal return of 6.0%, and an ending value of 106,000 yuan. With 2.5% inflation:

  • exact real return: 1.06 / 1.025 - 1 = 3.41%
  • approximation: 6.0%-2.5% = 3.5%, an overstatement of about 0.09 percentage point
  • 106,000 yuan at year-end equals 106,000 / 1.025 = 103,414.63 yuan in today’s money

If the market falls 10.0%, net nominal return is -11.0% and the ending value is 89,000 yuan; the real return is 0.89 / 1.025 - 1 = -13.17%, worth 86,829.27 yuan in today’s money. If the market rises 20.0%, the net nominal return is 19.0% and the real return is 1.19 / 1.025 - 1 = 16.10%. By comparison, a deposit paying 3.2% with no fee has a real return of 1.032 / 1.025 - 1 = 0.68%.

Plan A’s base-case real return exceeds the deposit’s, but its one-year range is -13.17% to +16.10%; the deposit’s 0.68% is more certain. That is the trade-off: a higher expected return usually comes from bearing more uncertainty, not from a free lunch.

Interpreting the result

Nominal return says how much money you have; real return says how much purchasing power you gained. Fees lower returns [1]; they are charged in good and bad years while returns are uncertain, so low costs matter more over time. Risk is uncertainty about the return; a larger standard deviation means more risk [2], and risk covers the range of outcomes, their probabilities and liquidity limits, not the chance of losing money alone. A standard deviation weighs gains and losses alike and says nothing about the path of a drawdown or whether you can exit, so this article pairs volatility with scenarios and maximum drawdown rather than measuring risk by volatility alone. Large-company stocks in the United States averaged about 12.64% a year in nominal terms from 1981 to 2020, with a standard deviation of about 16.06% (arithmetic average) [3]. An expected value does not replace a downside check. (Definition note: the header of Table 12.4 reads verbatim Nominal Average Annual Returns 1981–2020 Standard Deviation of Returns 1981–2020, its title is Table 12.4 Arithmetic Average Annual Returns and Standard Deviation by Asset Class, 1981–2020, and the data are credited to Aswath Damodaran Online; the same table lists Baa bonds 10.34% / 7.67%, 10-year T-bonds 8.21% / 9.92%, US T-bills 3.94% / 3.39% and inflation 2.93% / 1.76%. All of these are nominal arithmetic averages and the table makes no real-return adjustment, so the quotients above are this article's own division of two columns in that table.) [3]

Failure conditions

  • comparing gross returns with someone else’s net returns
  • using nominal return as real return
  • comparing only averages, ignoring downside and drawdown
  • missing layered fees, taxes or spreads
  • treating past high returns as a guarantee
  • mismatching horizon and liquidity
  • using leverage or concentration to close a gap

Verification and reproduction

  1. Recalculate every scenario with the exact formula and record approximation error.
  2. Change the fee to a charge on ending assets and compare.
  3. Test at least two inflation rates and three return scenarios.
  4. Check fees, tax rates and data sources so the result can be reproduced.
  5. Record assumptions and the review date.

Knowledge-point review

holding period and compound return

Definition: Holding period is the time from purchase to sale, and compound return links period returns into annualised growth.

Mechanism: Volatility drag makes the arithmetic mean exceed the geometric mean, and a loss requires a larger gain to recover.

Example: For example, +50 percent then −50 percent leaves 0.75, an annualised return near −13.4 percent, not zero.

Boundary: Compound return is not a simple average; cash-flow timing, fees and taxes change it, and short-sample annualisation can exaggerate or hide risk.

real purchasing power

Definition: Real purchasing power is return after inflation, determining whether investment preserves spending capacity.

Mechanism: Real return approximates nominal minus inflation; taxing nominal returns cuts it further, and household baskets can exceed CPI.

Example: For example, a 6 percent nominal return with 4 percent inflation leaves about 2 percent real, less after tax.

Boundary: Real purchasing power is not one number; household baskets, regions and medical costs differ, and CPI can understate experienced inflation.

fees and taxes

Definition: Fees cover management, custody, trading and distribution, while taxes hit interest, dividends, gains and transactions.

Mechanism: Asset-based fees compound against investors, and taxes depend on account, holding period and realization; high turnover raises both.

Example: For example, a 1 percent annual fee can consume roughly a quarter of terminal value over 30 years, and frequent trading adds spreads and taxes.

Boundary: Lower fees are not always better; cheapness can sacrifice service, liquidity and risk control, and tax optimisation can lock assets or add complexity.

currency and benchmark selection

Definition: Currency moves change the home-currency return on foreign assets, and a benchmark is the reference for performance, matching mandate and risk.

Mechanism: Unhedged foreign return is local return plus FX change; benchmarks can be market, peer or liability based but must be consistent and investable.

Example: For example, a dollar asset rising 8 percent with 3 percent yuan appreciation returns about 5 percent in yuan; a yuan bond benchmark needs currency clarification.

Boundary: Currency is not noise; hedging costs, rate differentials and correlation change risk, and a benchmark can be chosen as a low bar to beat.

Data and institutional check: nominal return

Before using "nominal return" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns real return.

Third, the institutional boundary. investment risk is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: nominal return

Learning "nominal return" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "real return": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "investment risk" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of nominal return still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind real return reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "nominal return" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "real return" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Review questions: nominal return

  1. State the numerator, denominator and unit of "nominal return" in one sentence, then explain why it is not the same metric as "real return".
  2. Give an example where a change in "investment risk" reverses the conclusion, and name the rule that changed.
  3. Find a time series and show how seasonal adjustment, base period or revision changes its direction.
  4. Design a transfer test: from firm to household, or bank to fund; which parameters must be re-estimated?
  5. Write a falsifiable claim: which observable signal would make you abandon this article's conclusion?

Short answers are enough, but each must leave a number, date or source location. If you cannot answer, the module is still at the noun level; if you can, you have the minimum condition for transferring the concept to new data.

Give “holding period and compound return”, “real purchasing power”, “fees and taxes” to two readers unfamiliar with the topic and ask each for one observable proxy and one counterexample. If their proxies point to different objects, the definition is not yet operational; if neither can produce a counterexample, the boundary is not understood. Review is not a vote; it tests whether the conclusion can be rebuilt independently.

Exercises

  1. Approximation versus exact. A product earns 8.0% nominal, inflation is 3.0%, and the fee is 1.5 percentage points. What are the net nominal, approximate real and exact real returns?
  2. Trade-off judgment. Plan A has a base-case real return of 3.41% and a worst year of -13.17%; plan B has a real return of 0.68% with little volatility. Which is better?
  3. Meaning of a fee. Why is a 1.0 percentage point fee not simply “1% of return”?
Suggested answers
  1. Net nominal return 6.5%; approximate real return 3.5%; exact return 1.065 / 1.03 - 1 = 3.40%.
  2. There is no unique answer. It depends on horizon, liquidity, risk capacity and goals. A has a higher base case but a larger downside; B is steadier but grows purchasing power slowly.
  3. The fee is charged on assets, so its share of net return depends on the gross return. At 2% gross, 1 percentage point is half the return; over longer horizons it also compounds against the ending value.
  1. In 100 words, state the core metric in "INV-002", including its denominator and unit.
  2. Write "nominal return" and "real return" as observable variables and name the time gap between them.
  3. Find one rule change in "investment risk" and explain how it changes sample coverage.
  4. Run a counterfactual with this article's method: change one variable and hold the rest fixed.
  5. If you switched to an independent source, which order of magnitude would move most, and why?
  6. Write one falsifiable claim with an observable signal, a waiting period and a review metric.
