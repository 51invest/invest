Industry Map: A Diligence Checklist from Value Chain to Capital Returns

Industry research starts not with a story but with a map: who is upstream, who is downstream, where profit pools sit and why capital returns differ. Overlaying the value chain, entry barriers and capital intensity shows whether a company earns from industry structure or a temporary cycle.

Definition and intuition

An industry map places participants, segments, profit pools and capital returns on one canvas. It answers three questions: where money is made, why there, and whether it persists.

Four layers: value chain (upstream, manufacturing, distribution, service), profit pool (revenue, gross margin, ROIC by segment), structure (entry barriers, concentration, substitutes, bargaining power) and cycle (capacity, inventory, prices, policy).

An industry map is not a static photo; profit pools migrate with technology, regulation and trade. Research must see both where money is now and where it is moving[1][2].

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose an equipment industry: upstream components have 10 billion revenue and 8% ROIC; assembly has 30 billion and 12%; distribution services have 20 billion and 20%. The largest profit pool is distribution because it owns customer relationships and after-sales.

If assembly integrates upstream, capital intensity rises and ROIC may fall from 12% to 10%; if e-commerce replaces distribution, distribution ROIC falls to 10% and the pool shifts to platforms.

When comparing ROIC, also compare cost of capital and growth: a 20% ROIC with a 10% cost creates value; an 8% ROIC with a 10% cost destroys it. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Three checklists

Checklist 1: value chain. Customers, suppliers, substitutes, entry barriers and profit pools at each segment; who holds pricing power.

Checklist 2: capital returns. ROIC, capital intensity, depreciation, working capital and growth; whether high ROIC comes from moats or accounting conventions.

Checklist 3: cycle and policy. Capacity utilisation, inventory, prices, subsidies, tariffs and environmental constraints; where the industry sits in the cycle.

Compare the industry map with company financials: is the company's ROIC above the industry, where does the advantage come from and can it persist; do not look only at growth[3][4].

Conditions, boundaries and common mistakes

Boundary 1: an industry map is not company analysis. The map explains structure; company analysis still needs management, execution, financials and governance; good industries contain bad companies.

Boundary 2: ROIC definitions differ. R&D capitalisation, leases, goodwill and working-capital definitions affect ROIC; adjust before comparing.

Boundary 3: profit pools move. Technology, regulation and consumer habits shift pools; a static map misleads.

Mistake 1: the fastest-growing segment is the most profitable. Fast growth with high capital intensity and competition can produce low ROIC; profit pools are about returns, not revenue alone.

Mistake 2: high ROIC always means a moat. It can come from accounting, a cycle peak or leverage; decompose the source and durability.

Deeper note: the ROIC tree, value-chain bargaining and diligence questions

An industry map starts with the value chain, then the profit pool. From raw materials, components, manufacturing, distribution, retail to services, mark value added, capital intensity, concentration and bargaining power at each link. Profit pools usually concentrate in a few links, not across the whole industry.

ROIC splits into three trees: margin (price minus cost), capital turnover (revenue over capital) and leverage (use less). High ROIC can come from high margin (brands, patents), high turnover (staples, retail) or low capital (software, services); sustainability differs by source.

Value-chain bargaining depends on substitutability, switching costs and information. Upstream oligopolies have power over downstream; downstream platforms have power over fragmented suppliers; intermediaries that can be bypassed see margins compressed. Ask who controls scarce resources, bears inventory risk and owns customer relationships.

Capital intensity drives cyclical elasticity. High fixed-cost industries see profits explode on the upside and losses amplify on the downside; asset-light industries are less elastic but more stable. Comparisons must adjust for capital structure, leases and R&D capitalisation.

Diligence questions: why do customers choose it over rivals? Can suppliers bypass it? How much capital and time does capacity expansion need? What is sacrificed in a price war? What returns has management earned on past capital allocation? Each needs verifiable evidence.

Moats must be specified: network effects, switching costs, cost advantage, intangible assets, economies of scale. Moats erode through technology, regulation and consumer behaviour; write how the moat narrows, not just that one exists.

Common misuses: equating industry growth with company growth, high gross margin with high ROIC, market share with a moat, and ignoring capital intensity and cycles. The map's purpose is to locate the profit pool and sustainable returns.

Finally, diligence must reach unit economics and capital returns: for each new unit of capacity, how much is invested, what return, and how long to pay back. If unclear, the growth story is not credible.

Industry-map review checklist

Review an industry map for how it explains profit-pool migration, not for a pile of value-chain labels:

  1. Are value-chain boundaries clear? How upstream, manufacturing, channels and services are separated; when one firm spans segments, profit must sit in the right link.
  2. Is the profit pool measured by revenue or ROIC? Revenue is not profit; read gross margin, capital intensity and returns, ideally through an ROIC tree.
  3. Are entry barriers verifiable? Licences, scale, network effects, switching costs and patents need observable evidence; narrative barriers do not count.
  4. Who holds bargaining power? Upstream concentration, downstream fragmentation, substitutes and switching costs allocate profit.
  5. Is the cycle position marked? Capacity, inventory, price and policy stage; treating a cycle peak as normal overstates returns.
  6. Is capital return sustainable? High ROIC attracts entrants; ask for how long and on what moat.
  7. Does the map update with technology and regulation? Profit pools migrate; the map needs review dates and trigger events.
  8. Are diligence questions answerable? Each claim should map to data or an interview question; an unanswerable map is only a hypothesis.

The value is a verifiable chain from where money is made, to why, to whether it lasts.

Finally, label every claim on the map by evidence grade: verified, to be tested, or pure hypothesis. Diligence should first test the highest profit pools, strongest barriers and most concentrated links; low-evidence claims should not enter valuation directly.

Minimum action: pick the three largest profit pools, write one verifiable barrier and one piece of evidence that would overturn it for each, and start diligence with the falsifier.

Record: date every arrow on the map; do not make new decisions from an old profit pool.

In one line: profit pools migrate; date the map.

Note: put evidence grades in the diligence memo; verbal judgements do not count.

Label: archive the evidence grade and date together.

What would change the conclusion

An industry map depends on profit-pool data and barrier judgements:

  1. Segment data are misallocated. If firms span links and transfer pricing distorts segment profit, the ROIC tree points to the wrong link.
  2. Barriers are eroding. If technology substitution, regulatory opening or new entrants weaken moats, historical pools cannot be extrapolated.
  3. A cycle peak is treated as normal. If utilisation, price and inventory are at peaks, margins mean-revert.
  4. Capital intensity changes. If the business shifts from heavy to asset-light, ROIC is not comparable with history.

Practical implication: an industry map must land on ROIC and evidence grades; when profit pool, barriers and cycle disagree, trust verifiable data first.

Exercises

  1. Draw upstream, manufacturing, distribution and service layers for an industry.

  2. Upstream ROIC 8%, cost of capital 10%: create or destroy value?

  3. Distribution ROIC 20%, cost of capital 10%: what is economic profit?

  4. Why can profit pools shift from distribution to platforms?

  5. What non-moat factors can produce high ROIC?

  6. How do you combine an industry map with company analysis?

  7. True or false: the fastest-growing segment is always the most profitable. Explain.

  8. Why do profit pools usually concentrate in a few value-chain links?

  9. How do the three sources of high ROIC differ in sustainability?

Suggested answers

  1. Upstream materials/components, manufacturing, channel/distribution and service/after-sales; mark value added, capital intensity, concentration and bargaining power at each layer.
  2. ROIC 8% below a 10% cost of capital means negative economic profit and value destruction; faster growth destroys more.
  3. Economic profit = (20%−10%)×invested capital; with 100 million invested, 10 million of economic profit.
  4. Channel profit shifts to platforms because platforms own customer relationships, data and traffic, while channels can be bypassed or substituted.
  5. High ROIC can come from accounting (leases, R&D capitalisation), a cyclical peak, leverage or temporary pricing, not necessarily a moat.
  6. Map the industry to find the profit pool, then analyse the company's position in the high-return link and whether it can defend it.
  7. Not necessarily. The fastest-growing link may be capital-intensive, competitive and low-return; compare ROIC with capital cost.
  8. Scarce resources, customer relationships, information and scale concentrate in a few links; others are substitutable or weakly positioned.
  9. High margin from brands/patents is sustainable but contestable; high turnover from operations is imitable; low capital from business model is exposed to technology and regulation.
