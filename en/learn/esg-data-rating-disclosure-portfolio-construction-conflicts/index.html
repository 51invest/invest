ESG Data Conflicts: Ratings, Disclosure and Portfolio Construction

The same company can be rated A by one ESG provider and C by another because agencies disagree on what matters and how to score. Portfolio construction cannot rely on ratings alone; go back to raw disclosure, industry context and financial transmission.

Definition and intuition

ESG refers to environmental, social and governance non-financial factors. ESG ratings score companies on these dimensions; ESG disclosure is company data under frameworks such as GRI, SASB and TCFD; portfolio construction turns the information into weights and constraints.

The three layers differ: disclosure is raw data, ratings are processed judgement, portfolios are investment decisions. Rating disagreement comes from scope (which issues matter), measurement (which metrics), weights (how to aggregate) and industry adjustments.

A company can differ widely across raters without either being wrong; agencies may disagree on what matters for an industry. Portfolio construction should return to raw disclosure and financial transmission[1][2].

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose two cement companies: A has high emissions but complete disclosure and strong governance; B has lower emissions but missing disclosure and average governance. Agency Alpha weights governance and rates A high; agency Beta weights emissions and rates B high.

A rating-only portfolio may buy A and exclude B; but A faces higher carbon-cost risk, while B's data gap may hide problems. The better approach combines emissions intensity, disclosure quality, carbon-price sensitivity and governance records.

If the carbon price rises from 80 to 120 yuan per tonne, A's unit cost rises 40 yuan and gross margin falls 3 points; B's lower emissions are less affected. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Three checks

Check 1: rating methodology. Which issues, weights and industry adjustments; ratings from different agencies are not directly comparable.

Check 2: raw disclosure. Scope 1, 2 and 3 emissions, water, waste, labour and governance records; whether data are third-party assured.

Check 3: financial transmission. How carbon prices, regulation, litigation, community relations and talent costs affect cash flow; ESG is not a moral label but a valuation input.

Data gaps are larger for small companies and emerging markets; use industry neutrality, disclosure quality and alternative data, not a single rating[3][4].

Conditions, boundaries and common mistakes

Boundary 1: ESG is not morality. It covers financially relevant environmental, social and governance risks, not a value judgement; investor values shape weights.

Boundary 2: ratings are not facts. They are agency judgements with method errors and conflicts; read methodologies and raw data.

Boundary 3: greenwashing is hard to spot. Selective disclosure, vague targets and missing Scope 3 flatter performance; look for assurance, history and verifiable targets.

Mistake 1: a high ESG rating means low risk. It does not imply fair valuation or financial strength; ESG is one risk dimension.

Mistake 2: ESG portfolios always sacrifice returns. It depends on screening, industry exposure and pricing; excluding high-emission sectors can reduce diversification or avoid regulatory risk.

Deeper note: ESG data, rating divergence and portfolio construction

ESG data are not homogeneous. Environmental data (emissions, energy, water) are more measurable; social data (labour, community) rely on disclosure and estimates; governance data (board, pay, audit) are more structured. Providers differ in coverage, methodology and missing values, and stitching them together creates bias.

Rating divergence is normal. S&P, MSCI and Sustainalytics can score the same company very differently because of weights, industry adjustments, controversy treatment and disclosure coverage. Treating a rating as 'truth' misleads; understand the source of divergence.

Disclosure versus substance: more disclosure is not better performance and may be greenwashing; substance means emissions intensity, accident rates, pay gaps, board independence and supply-chain audits. Disclosure is an input, not an outcome.

Portfolio construction has four approaches: negative screening, positive screening, ESG integration and thematic investing. Each carries different exposures; negative screening shrinks the universe and concentrates sectors; integration can sacrifice tracking error; thematic can crowd.

Conflicts of interest: managers charge ESG product fees, providers charge, rating agencies consult, companies choose disclosure; disclose conflicts and voting policy. ESG is not value-neutral; value judgements must be transparent.

Measurement: coverage, data timeliness, rating divergence, portfolio carbon intensity, controversy events and engagement records. A single score is insufficient; use multiple sources and actual impact.

Common misuses: treating ESG ratings as return forecasts, disclosure as performance, exclusion as impact, and ignoring greenwashing and conflicts.

Finally, ESG is information plus values plus execution, not one scorecard; portfolio construction must state goals, constraints and trade-offs.

ESG review checklist

Before adding ESG to a portfolio, separate ratings, data and portfolio construction:

  1. What does the rating measure? Risk exposure, impact, compliance or value alignment; agencies use different questionnaires and weights, so divergence is normal.
  2. Is data coverage complete? Large firms disclose more and small firms less; treating missing values as low scores systematically penalises small caps and emerging markets.
  3. Is the underlying data traceable? Emissions, labour and governance metrics may be self-reported, third-party or regulatory; audit quality of self-reports needs separate assessment.
  4. How does ESG map to financial performance? Lower tail risk, higher cost of capital or growth; an ESG score without a transmission mechanism is only a label.
  5. Are portfolio constraints executable? Exclusion lists, tilts and engagement differ in turnover, tracking error and liquidity cost.
  6. Where are the conflicts of interest? Rating agencies also advise, fund managers also vote; governance and disclosure must address conflicts.
  7. How is greenwashing identified? Separate targets, baselines, offsets and absolute versus intensity metrics; slogans are not enough.
  8. Is fiduciary duty clear? When ESG and return objectives conflict, who wins and how is it disclosed; fiduciaries must not package personal preferences as client preferences.

ESG quality is not a high score but traceable data, an explicable transmission and executable constraints.

Finally, separate facts, ratings and opinions: emissions data are facts, ratings are methods, portfolio tilts are opinions. Mixing them leaves readers unable to tell whether disagreement comes from data, models or values.

Minimum action: split ESG tilts into exclusion, tilt and engagement tables, each with data source, turnover cost and tracking error; review conflicts of interest quarterly.

Record: rating disagreement is information; log the gap between two agencies and why it exists rather than forcing an average.

What would change the conclusion

ESG portfolio conclusions depend on ratings, data and transmission:

  1. Ratings diverge. If two agencies score the same firm very differently, an aggregate hides real risk.
  2. Small-cap data are missing. If missing values become low scores, the portfolio systematically excludes small caps and emerging markets.
  3. The regulatory regime changes. If disclosure rules or carbon prices shift, historical ESG alpha can reverse.
  4. Engagement costs are high. If engagement, voting and reporting cost more than they return, net performance falls.
  5. Fiduciary conflict. If clients do not adopt ESG objectives, tilts may breach fiduciary duty.

Practical implication: ESG portfolios should separate facts, ratings and opinions; logging rating disagreement, missing data and engagement cost shows whether return comes from risk pricing or value expression.

Data note: compare on the same rating date and definition; agencies differ in coverage and missing-data treatment, so scores are not directly interchangeable.

Scope note: date every rating and label coverage and definition; scores from different agencies cannot be mixed across periods.

Further reading: disclosure standards and evidence

The ISSB issued IFRS S1/S2 in 2023 to harmonise global climate and sustainability disclosure; the EU CSRD requires double materiality (financial and impact), with broader scope; SASB provides industry metrics. The standards are not fully compatible, so cross-country comparison needs adjustment.

Empirical research finds an unstable relationship between ESG ratings and stock returns, with large differences across raters; high-ESG portfolios outperform in some periods and lag in others, depending on sector, valuation and rates.

Greenwashing research uses disclosure text, gaps between targets and actual emissions, and third-party audits; regulators are tightening naming and disclosure rules for 'sustainable' funds.

Reading suggestion: read ISSB and CSRD requirements first, then rating divergence and return evidence; separate disclosure, rating and portfolio layers rather than treating disclosure as performance.

Chapter takeaways

  • ESG data are heterogeneous; environmental, social and governance factors differ in measurability.
  • Rating divergence comes from weights, industry adjustments, controversy treatment and disclosure coverage.
  • More disclosure is not better performance; distinguish greenwashing from substance.
  • Negative screening, positive screening, integration and thematic investing carry different exposures.
  • ESG involves values and conflicts; disclose goals and trade-offs.
