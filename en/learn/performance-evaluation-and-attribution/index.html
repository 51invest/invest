Performance Evaluation and Attribution

Compare a hypothetical 8.7% portfolio return with a 6.8% benchmark, deduct a 0.60% fee, and decompose the 1.30 percentage point net excess into allocation, selection and interaction effects while showing why one year cannot prove skill.

ModuleINV-M13 · 绩效与归因
LevelSystematic
Read7 min · 15 sections

Problem and output

Performance evaluation asks whether the result met the objective at the risk taken; attribution asks whether excess return came from allocation, selection or interaction. The outputs are net return, excess return versus a benchmark, risk-adjusted measures, an attribution decomposition and an uncertainty statement, not an overall verdict on a manager.

Inputs and data requirements

Inputs include portfolio and benchmark returns over the same period, beginning weights, fees, cash flows and sample length. Return frequency must match, weights must refer to the same instant, and the benchmark must be investable and style-consistent. The example is a hypothetical one-year, 12-month observation: portfolio stocks/bonds 70/30 versus benchmark 60/40; stock returns 12% portfolio and 10% benchmark; bond returns 1% portfolio and 2% benchmark; annual fee 0.60%; tracking error 4%.

The performance-presentation convention follows CFA Institute GIPS: report portfolio performance using total return time-weighted, aggregate portfolios into composites and make the required disclosures, with fair presentation and full disclosure as the objective [1].

Assumptions

Returns are gross; the 0.60% annual fee is deducted directly from excess return as an approximation; there are no interim cash flows, or time-weighted returns have removed their effect; one currency, no taxes; weights are set at the start; the significance test assumes monthly excess returns are independent and identically distributed; all numbers are teaching assumptions.

Steps

  1. Portfolio return 0.7×12% + 0.3×1% = 8.7%; benchmark 0.6×10% + 0.4×2% = 6.8%; gross excess 1.9 percentage points.
  2. Deduct the fee: net excess is about 1.9%-0.60% = 1.30 percentage points.
  3. Allocation effect Σ(w_p-w_b)×r_b = 0.1×10% + (-0.1)×2% = 0.8 percentage points.
  4. Selection effect Σw_b×(r_p-r_b) = 0.6×2% + 0.4×(-1%) = 0.8 percentage points.
  5. Interaction effect Σ(w_p-w_b)×(r_p-r_b) = 0.1×2% + (-0.1)×(-1%) = 0.3 percentage points; the three sum to 1.9, matching gross excess.
  6. Risk adjustment: information ratio 1.30%/4% = 0.33.
  7. Significance: mean monthly excess 1.30%/12 = 0.108%, monthly tracking error 4%/√12 = 1.15%; their ratio 0.108%/1.15% ≈ 0.09 is the monthly information ratio, and multiplying by √12 gives t ≈ 0.33, so "no skill" cannot be rejected.

The portfolio arithmetic follows OpenStax: a portfolio return is the weighted average of the asset returns, and portfolio volatility depends on the weights, the individual volatilities and the correlations [2].

Interpreting the result

The 1.30 percentage point net excess is indistinguishable from noise over one year; 0.8 points of allocation come from overweighting the higher-returning stock sector, 0.8 points of selection from stocks beating their benchmark by 2 points offset by bonds lagging by 1 point, and 0.3 points from interaction. Attribution is an ex post decomposition, not repeatable proof of skill; shorter samples and larger tracking error weaken the conclusion. The same numbers under a different attribution convention produce a different allocation and selection split, so the convention must be fixed in advance and disclosed. Evaluation should combine net return, risk, style consistency and rule adherence, not a ranking alone.

Failure conditions

  • benchmark mismatched in style, currency or horizon
  • judging net results with gross returns and ignoring fees, taxes and slippage
  • a short or selected sample with survivorship bias
  • using beginning weights when weights changed during the period
  • zero tracking error or a zero denominator
  • hiding the attribution residual or dumping interaction into one component
  • inferring long-term skill from a single year

Verification

Recompute with daily or weekly data; use a style-matched alternative benchmark; check that the three effects sum to excess return; estimate intervals with rolling windows and bootstrapping; compare after-fee and after-tax results; audit cash-flow treatment in both portfolio and benchmark.

Reproduction

R_p=Σw_p r_p; R_b=Σw_b r_b; allocation=Σ(w_p-w_b)r_b; selection=Σw_b(r_p-r_b); interaction=Σ(w_p-w_b)(r_p-r_b). Example: 8.7%-6.8%=1.9%; 1.9%-0.60%=1.30%; 0.8+0.8+0.3=1.9.

Knowledge-point review

benchmarks

Definition: A benchmark is the reference for evaluating portfolio performance and should match mandate, risk, currency and constraints.

Mechanism: It can be a market index, peer group, liability or absolute target; choice affects attribution, incentives and flows.

Example: For example, a China equity fund uses the CSI 300, while a global portfolio uses a local-currency or hedged global index.

Boundary: A benchmark is not an easy low bar; mismatch hides risk and creates fake alpha, and benchmarks can be gamed or stale.

money-weighted and time-weighted returns

Definition: Time-weighted return removes cash-flow timing and measures the strategy, while money-weighted return reflects the investor's actual capital experience.

Mechanism: Time-weighted suits manager comparison, money-weighted reflects contributions and withdrawals; large flows significantly change the latter.

Example: For example, a fund returns 10 percent annually, but an investor buying high and selling low can have a negative money-weighted return.

Boundary: The two are not interchangeable; time-weighted ignores investor timing, and money-weighted mixes investor decisions with manager skill.

risk-adjusted performance

Definition: Risk-adjusted performance compares return with volatility, downside, beta or drawdown using Sharpe, Sortino, information and Treynor ratios.

Mechanism: Metrics should match the risk definition and strategy; Sharpe uses total volatility, Sortino downside, information ratio tracking error.

Example: For example, two funds with equal returns, the lower-volatility one has a higher Sharpe and better risk-adjusted result.

Boundary: Risk adjustment is not one ranking; metrics depend on sample, frequency and tails, leverage can lift Sharpe while adding blowup risk, and returns may omit unpriced risk.

style and performance attribution

Definition: Style describes value, growth, size and other traits, and attribution decomposes return into market, sector, selection, factor and interaction effects.

Mechanism: Attribution separates beta from alpha and allocation from selection; style exposure and sector weights drive performance in different environments.

Example: For example, a portfolio beats its benchmark by 3 percent, with 2 from sector overweight and 1 from selection.

Boundary: Attribution is not exact; models, cross terms and residuals matter, style exposure can hide, and short performance may be style beta rather than skill.

luck and skill

Definition: Luck is random or non-repeatable outcome, while skill is repeatable judgment, execution and risk management.

Mechanism: Separating them needs long samples, benchmarks, process and out-of-sample tests; short outperformance can be beta, style or luck.

Example: For example, a manager beats for two years but holds hot factors, which may not be stock-selection skill.

Boundary: Performance is not proof of skill; survivorship, backtests and short samples exaggerate, luck breeds overconfidence, and process matters more than one result.
