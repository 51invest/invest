Hedging: Purpose and Cost

Use a hypothetical coffee purchase to show how futures and options lock a price, surrender part of the upside and leave basis risk, and how margin, financing, fees and basis change the effective price.

Problem and output

Hedging uses an offsetting or related position to reduce a specific risk. The outputs are the hedge ratio, instrument, tenor, cost and residual basis risk. The aim is to lock an acceptable net exposure and reduce cash-flow volatility, not to remove all uncertainty or raise returns.

Inputs and data requirements

Inputs include the physical exposure, price or duration sensitivity, contract size, expiry, margin rate, financing rate, commissions and basis history; basis must be matched by product, location and quality. The example is hypothetical: buy 100 tonnes of coffee in three months at CNY 2,000 per tonne, with futures at the same price, a 10-tonne contract, 10% margin, 3% annual financing and a CNY 50 commission.

The hedge-versus-speculation distinction follows OpenStax: a hedge is a risk-management tool that reduces exposure, while speculation is a directional bet on the future [1].

Assumptions

Futures and spot prices are highly correlated; basis mean-reverts but is uncertain; the physical purchase quantity is not adjusted during the hedge; hedge accounting and taxes are ignored; margin can be met at any time. All numbers are teaching assumptions, not a trading recommendation.

Steps

  1. Exposure is 100×2,000 = CNY 200,000; contract notional is 10×2,000 = CNY 20,000; contracts are 200,000/20,000 = 10.
  2. Buy 10 futures; margin is CNY 20,000; three-month financing is 20,000×3%×0.25 = CNY 150.
  3. If both futures and spot rise to CNY 2,300: spot costs CNY 230,000, the futures gain is CNY 30,000, and net cost is CNY 200,000.
  4. If both fall to CNY 1,700: spot costs CNY 170,000, the futures loss is CNY 30,000, and net cost is still CNY 200,000.
  5. If futures rise to CNY 2,300 and basis is +CNY 50 per tonne (basis = spot price minus futures price here), spot cost is CNY 235,000; adding the CNY 50 commission and CNY 150 financing (CNY 200 in total), net cost is 235,000-30,000+200 = CNY 205,200, or CNY 2,052 per tonne.
  6. With an option struck at CNY 2,000, a premium of 3% of notional is CNY 6,000 and the maximum effective price is about CNY 2,060 per tonne; the buyer keeps the benefit of a price fall and faces no margin calls.
  7. Record the hedge ratio, expiry and roll, and changes in basis.

The futures mechanics also follow OpenStax: the contract trades the asset in the future at a price locked in today, and the exchange requires margin and marks positions to market daily to reduce counterparty risk [2].

Interpreting the result

Futures lock the price near CNY 2,000 at the cost of giving up the benefit of a fall, plus margin, financing, commissions and basis. A hedge ratio of 1.0 hedges price risk only, not quantity, quality, counterparty or liquidity risk. A basis of +CNY 50 raises the effective purchase price to CNY 2,052: the lock is the futures price plus basis, not an absolute spot price. The hedge ratio should match exposure sensitivity rather than quantity mechanically; options keep the downside benefit but cost more. Compare the cost with the risk reduction achieved.

Failure conditions

  • a wrong exposure or sensitivity estimate
  • over-hedging or under-hedging
  • basis widening without a buffer
  • margin calls forcing liquidation
  • roll costs and reduced liquidity
  • missing the opportunity cost of capital tied up in margin
  • counterparty default on an OTC contract [3]
  • accounting and tax errors
  • treating the hedge as a directional speculation
  • hedging risk that diversification could remove

Verification

Compare hedged and unhedged profit and loss; backtest basis; run ±15% price scenarios; calculate hedge effectiveness; stress margin; check liquidity, roll spreads, and the match of expiry, currency and quality between instrument and exposure.

Reproduction

contracts = exposure/contract notional; net cost = spot cost - futures P&L + fees; effective price = net cost/quantity. Example: 10 = 200,000/20,000; 205,200/100 = 2,052.

Knowledge-point review

risk exposures

Definition: Risk exposures are sensitivities to market, rates, credit, FX, commodities, liquidity and factors.

Mechanism: They are measured by beta, duration, credit spreads, VaR and factor regressions; explicit and hidden exposures together drive losses.

Example: For example, a portfolio with beta 1.2 should fall about 12 percent when the market falls 10, plus sector and FX exposure.

Boundary: Exposure is not one number; nonlinearity, correlation and tails deviate losses, and models can miss hidden risk.

instrument selection

Definition: Risk-management instruments include cash, futures, options, swaps, ETFs and structured products for hedging, insurance or diversification.

Mechanism: Choice depends on risk type, horizon, liquidity, cost and basis; futures are linear, options convex and swaps customised.

Example: For example, index futures hedge market beta, options hedge tails and FX forwards hedge currency.

Boundary: More complex tools are not better; basis, counterparty, margin and terms cause hedge failure, and illiquid tools cannot be adjusted under stress.

hedge error

Definition: Hedge error is residual risk or deviation from the intended hedge, arising from basis, correlation, maturity and modelling mistakes.

Mechanism: Hedge ratio, instrument choice and dynamic adjustment determine error; partial hedges may retain views or add new risk.

Example: For example, hedging a stock with a sector ETF leaves tracking error and idiosyncratic risk.

Boundary: Hedging is not risk elimination; basis widening, correlation breaks and counterparty default cause losses, and over-hedging adds cost and liquidity risk.

risk scenarios

Definition: Risk scenarios are assumption sets for rates, growth, inflation, credit, FX or extreme events used to estimate losses and capital needs.

Mechanism: They can be historical, hypothetical or stress tests; correlation, feedback and liquidity shift under pressure, and tails must be covered.

Example: For example, a stress test combines rates up 200 basis points, home prices down 20 percent and unemployment up 5 points.

Boundary: Scenarios are not forecasts; assumptions are subjective, tails can be worse, models may understate feedback and liquidity, and incomplete coverage misses new risks.

liquidity and tail risk

Definition: Liquidity risk is the inability to sell or fund in time, and tail risk is extreme but low-probability loss.

Mechanism: Liquidity deteriorates under stress, tails raise correlation and force deleveraging, and normal models such as VaR understate tails.

Example: For example, bond fund redemptions surge in a crisis, forcing discounted sales and losses beyond normal volatility.

Boundary: Liquidity is not constant; normal depth can vanish under stress, tail models can understate due to short history, and insurance and hedges carry basis and counterparty risk.

Data and institutional check: hedging

Before using "hedging" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns basis risk.

Third, the institutional boundary. futures is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

These four checks do not prove the conclusion, but they block the common failures: dividing two numbers with different definitions, treating a short swing as a long trend, and relabelling an institutional difference as a behavioural one. All teaching numbers in the article are assumptions; for formal citation, follow the date, unit and footnotes in the original source.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: hedging

Learning "hedging" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "basis risk": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "futures" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Boundary conditions should be observable thresholds, not adjectives. First, the sample threshold: does the data cover a full cycle, include stress periods, and include failures that exited the market? Second, the unit threshold: levels, growth rates, ratios and indices are not interchangeable, and the denominator must be stated. Third, the institutional threshold: are the effective date, covered entities and exemptions the same? Fourth, the behavioural threshold: when the metric changes, do agents actually adjust decisions, or does only the accounting number move? If any one fails, the conclusion is a scenario, not a baseline.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of hedging still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind basis risk reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "hedging" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "basis risk" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Decision table: risk exposures, instrument selection, hedge error

Put the three knowledge points into one decision in the order object, mechanism and boundary: first confirm what is being observed, then whether the causal chain holds, and finally whether the conclusion can be extended. If the definition in step one and the proxy in step two use different definitions, later calculations are precise about the wrong object. If step three omits the boundary, one successful sample is mistaken for a general law.
