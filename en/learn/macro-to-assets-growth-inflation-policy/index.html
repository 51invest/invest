From Macro to Assets: Pricing Growth, Inflation and Policy

Macro research is not forecasting one GDP number; it links growth, inflation, policy rates and risk premia into an asset-pricing chain. Real rates, earnings expectations and discount rates move separately, and asset prices rearrange; the same macro data mean different things for different assets.

Problem

Given a set of macro data, how do we judge what it means for equities, bonds and cash separately? A common error is to read “good growth” as “stocks up” or “high inflation” as “bonds down”. This article splits macro variables into three channels: earnings, discount rates and risk premia, then discusses their weights across assets[1].

Inputs

Four inputs are needed: nominal growth (or the output gap), inflation (CPI or PPI), policy and long-end yields, and risk premia or credit spreads. Data must carry frequency, seasonal adjustment and revision history; the first macro release and the final revision can differ a lot, and revised data must not be passed off as real-time information[2].

Assumptions

First, asset prices react to expected changes, not realised data; second, growth and inflation shocks can be identified separately; third, policy-rate changes transmit through the yield curve and discount rates; fourth, risk premia are not constant and move with uncertainty and liquidity. If these assumptions fail, the macro-to-asset mapping distorts[3].

Steps

Step 1, decompose the real rate: r≈i−πr \approx i - \pir≈i−π. Step 2, classify the shock: demand expansion, supply contraction or policy shift. Step 3, map to earnings expectations: growth helps earnings, but higher costs can offset. Step 4, map to discount rates: higher rates depress long-duration valuations. Step 5, map to risk premia: uncertainty widens credit spreads and equity risk premia. Step 6, compare asset duration and cash-flow stability.

Interpreting results

The same “rate cut” is bullish for bonds through a lower discount rate, possibly bearish for banks through net-interest margin compression, and more bullish for long-duration growth stocks. Ask: is the change real or inflation expectations? Level or curve shape? Temporary shock or rule change? Only after splitting channels can direction and size be judged.

Failure conditions

First, data revisions or definition changes; second, growth and inflation co-move too closely to separate; third, policy is replaced by administrative controls or quantity tools; fourth, risk premia are driven by a liquidity crisis; fifth, asset cash flows are insensitive to macro; sixth, the market has already priced it. Under these conditions, single-variable macro signals are unreliable.

Reproduction

Use one dataset to compute real rates, earnings growth and credit spreads separately, then regress the three asset returns on the channels; check signs, lags and out-of-sample stability. Record data versions and release dates; do not use only final revisions.

Reading and review

Before macro data reach asset prices, separate a change in real rates from a change in inflation expectations. A higher nominal rate can come from either; the first compresses all long-duration assets, the second affects stocks and bonds differently. Then split earnings from risk premia: growth beats expectations help revenue, but costs may keep margins flat; higher uncertainty widens credit spreads and equity risk premia. Only after separating the three channels can one explain why the same data move different assets in opposite directions. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Reading checklist

A practical warning: the same macro print can move assets in opposite directions because the channels have different weights. Before trading the headline, write the expected path for real rates, earnings and risk premia separately, then ask which channel dominates the asset’s duration. If you cannot name the channel, the signal is not yet an investment case.

Further check

Before applying “From Macro to Assets: Pricing Growth, Inflation and Policy”, test the weakest link between “Decomposing growth and inflation” and “Real rates and discount rates”. Write down the observable proxy, the direction of the causal link and the condition under which the link reverses. Then ask whether the data are measured at the same frequency and definition as the decision, and whether a cost or lag sits between the signal and the outcome. If the chain cannot be stated without hand-waving, the result is a description, not a usable claim. Run one counterexample and one stress case before extending the conclusion to another market, period or population.

For “From Macro to Assets: Pricing Growth, Inflation and Policy”, ask what the liabilities, horizon, tax base and tradable universe are, and in which account and horizon “Decomposing growth and inflation” and “Real rates and discount rates” are compared. First, the scope of Decomposing growth and inflation: total return or price return, before or after tax, local currency or foreign currency? “treating correlation as causation” usually puts returns from different accounts and horizons into one table. Align currency, fees and rebalancing frequency before ranking.

Next, the relationship between Real rates and discount rates and Cross-asset transmission and risk premia: how much risk budget, correlation, liquidity and trading cost change, and which portfolio line absorbs the effect. If a link is only an assumption, mark it; do not present it as a realised fact.

Boundary case: Growth 4%, inflation 3%, policy rate 2%: a negative real rate

With a 2% nominal policy rate and 3% inflation, the real rate is about -1%. If growth is 4% and earnings expectations rise 5%, but risk premium pushes the discount rate up one point, asset prices can move in opposite directions. If inflation rises to 5% and the policy rate to 4%, the real rate is still -1%, but the nominal discount rate is higher and long-duration assets face more valuation pressure. If growth falls to 1% and earnings expectations are cut 3%, numerator and denominator worsen together.

The conclusion reverses depending on whether the shock is supply or demand: demand expansion lifts earnings, while supply contraction lowers earnings and raises inflation, so the same “growth plus inflation” mix has opposite asset implications. The boundary is that macro-to-asset mapping is not single-variable. Real rates, earnings expectations and risk premium are three separate chains that must be combined.
