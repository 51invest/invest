Trend, Price and Volume

Use moving averages, closes and volume to test whether a trend exists, while stating lag, false breakouts and out-of-sample checks; the example is hypothetical data.

Problem and output

The inputs are a fixed-frequency, adjusted series of closes and volume, plus moving-average and comparison windows. The output is trend direction, whether volume confirms it, an invalidation condition and the uncertainty. The method does not forecast tops or bottoms; it turns "a trend exists and volume supports it" into testable conditions.

Inputs and data requirements

Daily or weekly bars adjusted for splits and dividends; volume in a single unit, with a note on whether after-hours and dark-pool trades are included; this article requires at least 60 bars so that the average and comparison windows fit. Halts, limit moves and auction prints distort price and volume and should be flagged separately. The example uses 10 hypothetical trading days.

The SMA convention follows the Fidelity indicator pages: the average price over a set period, read as trend direction, smoother at longer windows but with greater lag, with price crossing the average read as a change of trend [1].

Assumptions

Closes (days 1 to 10): 20, 21, 22, 21, 23, 24, 23, 25, 26, 25; volume (millions of shares): 10, 11, 12, 10, 14, 15, 12, 16, 18, 14. Trend uses a 5-day SMA and its slope; confirmation uses 5-day average volume. Prices are adjusted and volume covers all venues. The 10-day example only demonstrates the arithmetic; a real test must meet the 60-bar minimum above.

Steps

  1. Compute SMA5: 21.4 on day 5 and 24.6 on day 10, so the average is rising.
  2. Compute 5-day average volume: 11.4 on day 5 and 15.0 on day 10.
  3. Classify direction: the day-10 close of 25 is above SMA 24.6, which is above the day-5 value of 21.4.
  4. Split volume by direction (an up day closes above the prior close): 6 up days average 14.3 million shares and 3 down days 12.0 million, a ratio of 1.19.
  5. Write the rule: a close above a rising average with up-day volume dominance keeps the trend intact; a close below the average on above-average volume invalidates it.
  6. Record the lag: a 5-day average fully reflects a turn only after old prices roll out of the window; longer windows smooth more and lag more.

Average volume is defined on the same Fidelity pages as total volume over the period divided by the number of bars, with a price move on high volume treated as more meaningful than the same move on low volume [2].

Interpreting the result

The example rises 25% over ten days, the average rises 3.2 (21.4 to 24.6), and the volume ratio is 1.19, a mild "price up, volume up" confirmation. But day 10 closes at 25, below the prior 26, on 14 million shares versus a 15.0 million average, so momentum is already slowing. A moving average is an average, not a law of support, and a ratio of 1.19 is not statistically significant. The trend describes the sample, not the future. Research on 58 liquid futures instruments finds return persistence over one to twelve months that partly reverses later [3], which makes trend worth testing but not a law. The same rule gives “false breakout” a testable meaning: if day 11 closes at 23.5 on 18 million shares, above the 15.6 million five-day average, the rule written in step 5 flags invalidation and the earlier close-above-a-rising-average breakout was a false breakout; the judgement uses the rule fixed in advance, not a window or threshold changed afterwards.

Failure conditions

  • prices are unadjusted, or volume units change across the sample
  • halts, limit moves or auction prints create fake volume
  • illiquidity lets a single trade move the price
  • the same window defines and tests the trend, which is circular
  • a crossover is treated as a cause or a guarantee of return
  • costs, slippage and taxes are ignored

Verification

Recompute direction and the volume ratio with 10- and 20-day windows; repeat on an out-of-sample period; measure the hit rate, average payoff and turnover cost after each signal; compare with a random benchmark. If the conclusion flips with the window, report the instability instead of choosing the best-looking window.

Reproduction

SMA_n = (1/n) Σ C_t; average volume = total volume / number of bars; volume ratio = average up-day volume / average down-day volume. Keep the raw data, adjustment method, window parameters and every signal date.

Knowledge-point review

candlestick charts

Definition: Candlesticks record open, high, low and close for a period, with shapes reflecting buyer-seller pressure.

Mechanism: Body, shadows, colour and volume matter; a single candle is weak and needs trend, location and volume context.

Example: For example, a long lower shadow at support may show buying, but confirmation is needed.

Boundary: Candlesticks are not prediction tools; patterns are subjective, sample and hindsight bias mislead, and single signals are unreliable in noisy markets.

trend

Definition: A trend is a sustained price direction over time, up, down or sideways, defined by the sequence of highs and lows.

Mechanism: Trends are driven by flows, fundamentals, policy and sentiment; trend following works in one-way markets and whipsaws in ranges.

Example: For example, rising highs and higher lows mark an uptrend, while a break below a prior low may weaken it.

Boundary: Trends do not necessarily persist; turns are hard to identify live, lagging confirmation misses or chases, and leverage amplifies reversal losses.

support and resistance

Definition: Support is a zone where buying emerges on declines, and resistance where selling emerges on rallies.

Mechanism: They come from prior highs and lows, volume clusters, round numbers and Fibonacci levels; breakouts and false breaks need volume confirmation.

Example: For example, a stock repeatedly supported at 50 yuan may turn that zone into resistance after a break.

Boundary: Support and resistance are not precise lines; zones, liquidity and news invalidate them, hindsight drawing overfits, and false breakouts are common.

moving averages

Definition: A moving average smooths past prices to identify trend and dynamic support or resistance.

Mechanism: Short averages are more sensitive and long ones more stable; crossovers, alignment and deviation are common uses, but averages lag.

Example: For example, the 50-day crossing above the 200-day is seen as a long-term strengthening signal.

Boundary: Moving averages are not predictions; lag, parameter choice and range whipsaws mislead, they do not explain fundamentals, and crossover trading costs are high.

momentum indicators

Definition: Momentum measures the speed and magnitude of price change using RSI, MACD and rate of change.

Mechanism: It identifies trend strength and overbought or oversold conditions; divergence, flattening and parameters affect signals, and overbought can persist in strong trends.

Example: For example, RSI above 70 is often called overbought, yet prices can keep rising in a strong trend.

Boundary: Momentum is not a reversal guarantee; overbought and oversold can persist, indicators derive from price and correlate, and ranges produce false signals.

volume

Definition: Volume is the quantity traded in a period, reflecting participation, liquidity and flows.

Mechanism: Rising price on rising volume often confirms a trend, while rising price on falling volume warns; volume breakouts, quiet pullbacks and divergences are common readings.

Example: For example, a breakout above a prior high on higher volume is more credible, while a low-volume breakout often fails.

Boundary: Volume does not guarantee direction; high-frequency trading, hedging and index changes distort it, volume can be distribution, and single-day readings are noisy.

market breadth and common indicators

Definition: Market breadth measures how widely gains are shared across stocks, sectors and volume, using advance-decline lines, new highs and lows, and put-call ratios.

Mechanism: Breadth confirms the health of an index trend; narrow rallies led by a few heavyweights often signal internal fragility.

Example: For example, the index makes a new high while fewer stocks advance, showing weaker participation.

Boundary: Breadth is not precise timing; construction, heavyweights and rotation affect it, divergences can persist, and single indicators fail.

Data and institutional check: trend

Before using "trend" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns moving average.

Third, the institutional boundary. volume is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: trend

Learning "trend" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "moving average": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "volume" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of trend still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind moving average reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "trend" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "moving average" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Exercises

  1. Concept. Why is a moving-average signal lagged by construction?
  2. Calculation. How far above its 5-day average does the day-10 close sit, in percent?
  3. Judgement. If day 11 closes at 23.5 on 18 million shares, what does the rule say?
Suggested answers
  1. It uses the past n prices, and each new price enters with weight 1/n, so a turn takes time.
  2. (25-24.6)/24.6 ≈ 1.6%.
  3. SMA5 becomes (23+25+26+25+23.5)/5 = 24.5; the close of 23.5 is below it and volume of 18 million exceeds the 15.6 million five-day average, so the rule flags invalidation.
