Earnings-Quality Analysis

Judge whether reported profit is sustainable, cash-backed and comparable: reconcile net income to operating cash flow, then explain the gap through accruals, revenue quality, non-GAAP adjustments and notes; all example numbers are hypothetical.

LevelSystematic
Read6 min · 14 sections

Problem and output

Earnings-quality analysis asks whether profit is sustainable, cash-backed and comparable with peers and history. Inputs are three to five years of statements, notes, MD&A and non-GAAP reconciliations. Outputs are a checklist covering accruals, cash conversion, revenue quality, capitalization, one-offs and accounting changes, plus falsification conditions. It does not replace audits.

Inputs and data requirements

  • Three to five years of statements, notes, MD&A and the audit opinion [3].
  • A full reconciliation of each non-GAAP measure to its closest GAAP measure [2].
  • Peer data; policies for revenue, inventory, depreciation, capitalization and leases.
  • Adjustments: stock compensation, restructuring, impairments, litigation and tax.

Assumptions

All example amounts are in the same hypothetical currency unit (no specific currency is intended). Example: revenue 1,000 (+10% year over year, about 909 a year earlier), net income 100, operating cash flow 80, capex 50; receivables 200 versus 160 a year earlier, so DSO rises from about 64 to 73 days; non-GAAP net income 125 excludes 15 of stock-based compensation and 10 of restructuring, although restructuring has recurred for three years.

Steps

  1. Reconcile net income to operating cash flow: list depreciation, stock-based compensation and working-capital changes; compute OCF/net income = 80/100 = 0.80, free cash flow = 80 - 50 = 30, FCF/net income = 0.30 and accrual ratio = (100 - 80) / 1,250 = 1.6% (average total assets are assumed: 1,200 opening, 1,300 closing). The statements are related but not equivalent; non-cash items explain part of the gap [1].
  2. Check revenue quality: recognition, returns, receivables and DSO, deferred revenue and channel inventory. Revenue grew 10% while receivables grew 25% (200/160 - 1) and DSO rose from about 64 to 73 days; this needs explanation.
  3. Check expenses: are R&D, software and customer-acquisition costs expensed or capitalized; have depreciation lives or reserves been relaxed?
  4. Check one-offs: do restructuring, impairments and litigation recur? If so, treat them as operating costs.
  5. Check non-GAAP: require full reconciliation. Adding back stock-based compensation and recurring restructuring does not make adjusted profit of 125 cleaner than 100 [2].
  6. Check the balance sheet and notes: goodwill, leases, off-balance-sheet items, covenants, pensions and accounting changes.
  7. Compare across time and peers: three to five years, consistent accounting and common-size ratios.
  8. Falsify: OCF/net income below 0.8 for two years, DSO up another 10 days, restructuring in a fourth year, an auditor change or restatement. The 0.8, 10-day and 4-year thresholds are examples, not universal standards.

Interpreting the result

Net income is an accounting construct, and cash flow is not pristine: working-capital timing, supplier finance, factoring and capex classification all move cash. In the example, revenue grows 10%, cash conversion is 0.80, free cash flow is 30% of net income and receivables grow 2.5 times as fast as revenue. Together these warrant investigation, not a fraud claim. Non-GAAP measures can add information, but excluding stock-based compensation and recurring restructuring overstates sustainable profit; the SEC requires reconciliation and equal or greater prominence for GAAP [2]. Separate recurring from timing differences.

Failure conditions

  • using one year or quarter, or stale, pre-restatement data
  • treating operating cash flow as clean cash, ignoring working capital, factoring and supplier finance
  • ignoring capex and capitalization, overstating free cash flow
  • comparing companies with different accounting, such as LIFO versus FIFO, leases or capitalization
  • accepting non-GAAP without reconciliation, or double-counting adjustments
  • calling accounting differences fraud

Verification

  1. Rebuild operating cash flow from balance-sheet changes and tie it to the cash flow statement.
  2. Add non-GAAP adjustments back to GAAP; check tax and share counts.
  3. Compare three to five years with peers on a consistent basis; test free cash flow, operating cash flow and net income side by side.
  4. Read the audit opinion and notes for restatements and going-concern language.

Reproduction

The checklist records year, revenue, net income, operating cash flow, capex, free cash flow, receivables, DSO, accrual ratio, non-GAAP adjustments and accounting changes. Formulas: accrual ratio = (net income - operating cash flow) / average total assets; DSO = ending receivables / revenue × 365. Attach sources and definitions.

Knowledge-point review

earnings

Definition: Earnings are accounting profit after revenue and costs, reflecting operating results but shaped by recognition, expenses and one-offs.

Mechanism: Revenue growth, gross margin, expense ratio and tax determine profit; sustainable earnings come from core operations and convert into cash.

Example: For example, 20 percent revenue growth from price cuts and easy credit lowers margins and raises receivables, weakening earnings quality.

Boundary: Earnings are not cash; accounting policy, channel stuffing, subsidies and asset sales flatter profit, and quarterly figures have base effects.

cash flow

Definition: Cash flow is actual cash in and out; operating cash flow reflects core operations, and free cash flow after capex funds dividends, debt and buybacks.

Mechanism: Profit-cash gaps come from receivables, inventory, depreciation, amortisation and one-offs; cash conversion is a key quality measure.

Example: For example, net profit is 100 million but operating cash flow only 20 million as receivables and inventory surge.

Boundary: More cash flow is not always better; underinvestment, delayed payments and prepayments lift it short term but hurt long-term capacity, and quarters fluctuate.

assets

Definition: Assets are resources controlled by a firm, including current, fixed, intangible, goodwill and financial assets, measured at cost or fair value.

Mechanism: Asset structure reflects business model and capital intensity; turnover, impairment, off-balance items and quality determine real value.

Example: For example, asset-light software has little fixed assets but more goodwill and capitalised R&D, while manufacturing is capital-heavy.

Boundary: Assets are not value guarantees; goodwill, receivables and inventory can be overstated, off-balance liabilities and impairment change equity, and book value is not realisable value.

leverage

Definition: Leverage is debt relative to equity or cash flow, magnifying gains and losses and adding fixed obligations.

Mechanism: Net debt, interest cover, maturity and covenants determine risk; floating rates and short debt funding long assets amplify refinancing pressure.

Example: For example, a firm expands with cheap debt, but falling demand leaves interest and principal due, eroding equity.

Boundary: Leverage is not a free accelerator; high leverage triggers covenants and distressed sales under stress, and off-balance liabilities and leases hide it.

accounting estimates

Definition: Accounting estimates are management judgments on useful lives, bad debts, inventory write-downs, impairment, warranties and revenue recognition.

Mechanism: Estimates affect profit and asset values and should rest on history and reasonable assumptions; aggressive or conservative choices distort comparability and future adjustments.

Example: For example, extending useful lives cuts current depreciation and lifts profit without changing physical wear.

Boundary: Estimates are not objective facts; incentives, industry practice and audit quality matter, changes can hide deteriorating trends, and footnotes and cash flow are essential.

financial anomalies and analysis framework

Definition: Financial anomalies are signals diverging from economic reality, such as profit-cash gaps, abnormal receivables or inventory, related-party deals and frequent restatements.

Mechanism: An analysis framework starts from the business model and cross-checks growth, margins, cash, assets, leverage and governance to assess sustainability and risk.

Example: For example, rising profit with negative operating cash flow and receivables growing faster than revenue is a classic warning cluster.

Boundary: Anomalies are not convictions; expansion, M&A and seasonality can explain them, single metrics mislead, and multi-period, peer and footnote work is needed.

Exercises

  1. Concept. Why can net income rise while operating cash flow falls?
  2. Calculation. With the example numbers, what are OCF/net income, FCF/net income and DSO?
  3. Applicability. How does capitalizing software affect a comparison with peers?
Suggested answers
  1. Rising receivables or inventory, earlier revenue recognition, non-cash gains or payables changes can separate profit from cash.
  2. 0.80; 0.30; DSO 73 days, versus about 64 days a year earlier (160/909 × 365).
  3. Capitalization defers expense and raises current profit and assets, reducing current expense and cash-flow pressure; adjust capitalization, amortization and free cash flow first.
