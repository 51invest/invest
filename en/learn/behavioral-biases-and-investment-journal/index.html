Behavioral Biases and an Investment Journal

Use a hypothetical journal of 30 decisions to record ex ante reasons, invalidation conditions and bias tags, then review adherence, holding-period gaps and outcome gaps instead of judging decision quality by profit alone.

ModuleINV-M14 · 行为与纪律
LevelSystematic
Read6 min · 14 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

Problem and output

Behavioral biases are systematic errors of judgement or preference; an investment journal fixes the information, reasons and expectations at the moment of a decision so they can be checked later. The outputs are a searchable record, bias tags and process measures such as adherence rate, holding-period gap and breach share, not a psychological diagnosis, and not a guarantee of returns.

Inputs and data requirements

Inputs include trade records, decision time, price, position size, ex ante rationale, expected return and horizon, invalidation condition, confidence score, information sources, emotional state and rule checks. The example is a hypothetical 12-month journal of 30 closed decisions: 21 complied with pre-set rules (70% adherence), while 9 breached the single-position limit or added to a position ad hoc; winners were held 21 days on average and losers 56 days; rule-compliant trades averaged +1.8%, breaches -4.2%.

Assumptions

Rationales are self-reported and may be incomplete; keeping a journal changes behaviour; the sample only demonstrates the measurement convention; biases are not assumed to disappear; all numbers are teaching assumptions. Of the bias labels used here, the disposition effect and loss aversion are supported by the sources listed, while anchoring, confirmation, herding and fear of missing out in the pre-trade checklist are only common labels without individual sources.

Steps

  1. Before trading, record date, asset, side, quantity, decision price, rationale, expected return and horizon, invalidation condition, confidence 1–5, and a bias checklist (anchoring, confirmation, loss aversion, herding, fear of missing out).
  2. Run a pre-trade rule check: single-position limit, sector limit, cash buffer, cooling-off period; if failed, write down the exception.
  3. After execution, add fill price, slippage and costs.
  4. After closing, review at a fixed interval: outcome, whether the process followed the rules, what was knowable at the time, and bias tags; separate "good luck with a bad process" from "good process with a bad outcome".
  5. Aggregate: adherence 21/30 = 70%; holding gap 56-21 = 35 days; outcome gap 1.8%-(-4.2%) = 6.0 percentage points; breach share 9/30 = 30%.
  6. Turn frequent biases into rules: for example, a 24-hour cooling-off period for news-driven trades and a written invalidation condition for adding to losers.

Interpreting the result

The 35-day holding gap is consistent with the disposition effect: winners sold too early and losers held too long; the 6.0 percentage point outcome gap is descriptive, not causal, because breaches may cluster in more volatile assets. The journal's value is preserving what was thought at the time and reducing hindsight bias, the tendency of outcome knowledge to make events look more predictable in advance than they were; that definition and its research background are covered in the Historical Analogies and Hindsight Bias article (HIS-002) and are not repeated here. The journal cannot prove a rule works unless the sample is adequate, the rule is fixed in advance and it is tested out of sample.

The Nobel lecture separates the normative from the descriptive model and sets out loss aversion, reference dependence and framing effects in prospect theory [2].

Failure conditions

  • writing rationales afterwards and rewriting memory to fit outcomes
  • too many fields to sustain
  • recording only profit and loss, not reasons and invalidation conditions
  • crediting success to skill and failure to luck
  • using 30 observations to prove a bias or a rule
  • storing sensitive account information without protection
  • logging trades without a scheduled review
  • changing the evaluation standard during review

Verification

Reconcile dates, prices and quantities with broker statements; have a second person audit 10 entries against the same coding sheet; compare entries with the rules set at the start of the year; recompute adherence and holding gaps each quarter; score decision quality separately from outcome; test a rule prospectively on a small scale.

Reproduction

adherence=compliant trades/total trades; holding gap=average days held for losers-average days held for winners; outcome gap=average return of compliant trades-average return of breaches. Example: 21/30=70%; 56-21=35 days; 1.8%-(-4.2%)=6.0 percentage points.

Knowledge-point review

overconfidence

Definition: Overconfidence overestimates the accuracy of one's judgment, knowledge or control, leading to overtrading, concentration and underestimated risk.

Mechanism: It comes from self-attribution, information overload and culture; in investing it raises costs, reduces diversification and ignores contrary evidence.

Example: For example, after several wins an investor adds leverage and concentrates in one stock, underrating drawdown odds.

Boundary: Confidence is not competence; overconfidence is hard to self-detect, success can be luck, feedback lags, and correction needs process and records rather than willpower.

loss aversion

Definition: Loss aversion means losses hurt more than equal gains please, causing reluctance to realize losses, premature profit-taking and risk-preference reversals.

Mechanism: It depends on reference points, framing and emotion; in investing it holds losers, chases break-even and avoids reasonable risk.

Example: For example, after a stock falls below purchase price an investor refuses to sell, hoping to break even and deepening the loss.

Boundary: Loss aversion is not simple cowardice; it drives disposition effects and distorted risk decisions, reference points shift, and over-stopping can sell at lows.

herding

Definition: Herding is following the crowd, driven by social proof, information asymmetry, career pressure and fear of missing out.

Mechanism: It amplifies flows, trends and bubbles and accelerates selling in crises; institutions and individuals both herd.

Example: For example, social-media buzz on an asset induces FOMO buying and short-term mispricing.

Boundary: Herding is not always wrong; crowds can inform, but homogeneity and echo chambers hide risk, and followers often enter at peak crowding.

narratives

Definition: Narratives are stories, causality and identities explaining prices and markets, shaping expectations, attention and flows.

Mechanism: They spread by simplifying complexity, offering belonging and emotion; strong narratives can diverge from fundamentals for long.

Example: For example, “this time is different” or “new economy” stories make investors ignore profit and valuation.

Boundary: Narratives are not evidence; stories select data, ignore counterexamples and self-reinforce, reverse sharply when broken, and can be interrupted by policy or reality.

checklists

Definition: A checklist fixes key steps, risks and questions into a repeatable list, reducing omissions and emotional interference.

Mechanism: Investment checklists cover thesis, valuation, risk, liquidity, conflicts and exit; simplicity, actionability and updates matter.

Example: For example, before buying: does cash flow support it, what is the worst case, what size, and what is the exit.

Boundary: Checklists are not magic; long ones become ritual, stale items miss new risks, ticking can replace thinking, and training and review are needed.

journaling and review

Definition: A journal records investment reasons, assumptions, emotions and outcomes, and review compares expectations with results to extract better rules.

Mechanism: Journals include timing, information, sizing and exit conditions; review separates process quality from outcome and avoids hindsight.

Example: For example, record “buying on cash-flow improvement” and review after three months whether cash flow delivered, not just price.

Boundary: Journaling is not a ritual; selective records, outcome focus and defensive attribution defeat learning, and review needs honesty and outside feedback.

Exercises

  1. Calculation. What are the adherence rate and breach share?
  2. Interpretation. What bias might the 35-day holding gap reflect?
  3. Judgement. Does the 6.0 percentage point outcome gap prove that rule compliance earns more?
Suggested answers
  1. 70% and 30%.
  2. The disposition effect: selling winners too early and holding losers too long; differences in asset risk may also contribute [1].
  3. No; the sample is small and non-random, and asset and market conditions are uncontrolled.
