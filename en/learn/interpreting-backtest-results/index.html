Interpreting Backtest Results

Use a hypothetical 60-month backtest to separate statistical significance, trading costs, multiple trials and out-of-sample evidence, so a historical fit is not mistaken for a future return.

Problem and output

A backtest applies a fixed rule to historical data and reports returns, drawdowns, turnover and statistics. The question is how much of the record comes from the rule, how much from sample selection, cost assumptions and luck, and whether it merits an out-of-sample test rather than being treated as a return promise.

Inputs and data requirements

Use a point-in-time universe, adjusted prices, dividends, and delisting and suspension records. Register the rule, parameter grid and number of trials before looking at results. Charge costs on traded value and turnover, and match the benchmark by period, currency and exposure. The example is a hypothetical 60-month series of gross monthly returns: mean 0.90%, standard deviation 3.20%, risk-free rate 0.20% per month, one-way cost 0.10% per month, and 20 parameter trials.

Assumptions

Returns are adjusted; the strategy rebalances monthly; the raw series is gross of costs; only the best of 20 trials is reported; the regime is unchanged; the benchmark has similar exposure. All numbers are teaching assumptions, not a strategy recommendation.

The Sharpe convention follows OpenStax: an average return without risk is meaningless, the Sharpe ratio is the portfolio risk premium divided by the standard deviation of returns, and both Sharpe and Treynor are relative measures that need a benchmark or peer comparison [3].

Steps

  1. Fix the rule, sample, costs and benchmark before computing anything.
  2. Test the mean: t = 0.90/(3.20/√60) = 2.18.
  3. Annualized Sharpe: (0.90-0.20)/3.20×√12 = 0.76.
  4. Charge 0.10% per month: mean 0.80%, t = 1.94.
  5. Apply a simple multiple-testing correction for 20 trials: the two-sided 5% threshold is about 3.02, so 2.18 fails.
  6. Split the sample: the first 36 months have mean 0.70%, standard deviation 3.00% and t=1.40; the last 24 months have mean 1.20%, standard deviation 3.50% and t=1.68. Neither half is significant.
  7. Compare with the benchmark and factor exposures to see whether the return is just market, size or industry beta. If changing the start date changes the conclusion, call the result fragile.

Presenting only the best of 20 trials is the textbook case of backtest overfitting and selection bias: Bailey and López de Prado explain in the Journal of Portfolio Management that a backtest optimizer searches for the parameter set with the best historical record, that reporting only positive results with an uncontrolled number of trials produces overly optimistic performance expectations, and that the Deflated Sharpe Ratio corrects for multiple-testing selection bias and non-normal returns [2].

Interpreting the result

0.90% a month is 10.8% simple annualized and about 11.35% compounded. After costs, 0.80% a month is about 10.03% compounded. The numbers look respectable, but t=2.18 is unremarkable after 20 trials, falls below 2 after costs, and is lower in both halves. Statistical significance is an in-sample signal-to-noise ratio; economic significance asks whether the return survives fees, capacity and risk; implementability asks about borrow, liquidity and trading constraints. A backtest generates a hypothesis, not proof of future returns: it narrows the candidate list but provides no confidence guarantee.

There is also a methodological reason a backtest can only raise a hypothesis: Bailey et al. report in the Journal of Computational Finance that standard anti-overfitting techniques such as hold-out tend to be unreliable in investment backtesting, and propose combinatorially symmetric cross-validation (CSCV) to estimate the probability of backtest overfitting (PBO) [1].

Failure conditions

  • look-ahead, survivorship or restated data
  • changing parameters, breakpoints or the sample after seeing results
  • reporting only the best trial and hiding failures
  • ignoring costs, market impact, turnover and capacity
  • a sample that is too short or spans a regime change
  • a mismatched benchmark or exposure
  • explaining returns with contemporaneous characteristics

Verification

Run out-of-sample and walk-forward tests; vary parameters, groupings and weights; charge costs, turnover and capacity limits; compare factor exposures and benchmarks; report every trial, including failures; replicate with an independent data source.

Reproduction

t = mean/(sd/√n); annualized Sharpe = (mean - risk-free rate)/sd × √12; multiple-testing threshold z = Φ⁻¹(1-0.05/(2×20)) ≈ 3.02. Example: 2.18 = 0.90/(3.20/√60); 0.76 = 0.70/3.20 × 3.464. Record the data version, universe, rebalancing dates, costs and number of trials.

Reading checklist

A final check is the cost of the strategy in the real account: spreads, borrow, taxes and capital calls. A backtest that ignores them is a hypothesis about gross returns, not a plan for net returns.

Working paper: four columns for a checkable judgement

The subject of this article is "INV-016". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "backtest". Column two states the transmission channel of "overfitting". Column three states the institutional boundary of "multiple testing". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "overfitting" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. multiple testing is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Review sentence

Write one sentence only: "Under the ____ convention, a change in ____ affects ____ through ____; if ____ appears, the claim fails." The four blanks are the unit, the variable, the channel and the falsifier. If the sentence cannot be written, at least one of the four columns is still empty; if it can be written but cannot be observed, the falsifier is wrong. On every data update, reread this sentence before reading the charts.

Boundary case: Sixty months and twenty parameter sets: how much of the in-sample Sharpe is real?

Monthly excess return has a mean of 0.70% and a standard deviation of 3.20% over 60 months, giving a Sharpe of about 0.70 ÷ 3.20 × √60 = 1.69. After a one-way cost of 0.10%, the mean is 0.60% and the Sharpe about 1.45. If twenty parameter sets were tried, a rough independent-test calculation raises the expected maximum in-sample Sharpe. If out-of-sample data cover only 12 months, the standard error is about 1 ÷ √12 = 0.29, so a 1.45 Sharpe is not stable.

The conclusion reverses when costs are low, capacity is large and out-of-sample results agree: with low turnover and a 0.02% cost, net Sharpe is close to gross; only continued out-of-sample performance makes the strategy investable. The boundary is that a backtest is a historical fit, not future return. Multiple trials, costs and out-of-sample evidence are three separate gates.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not strategy performance.

A backtest should also report trading frequency, capacity and extreme months. A high Sharpe may come from a few months or from a microcap strategy that cannot absorb capital; without drawdown, turnover and capacity, readers cannot judge replicability. Showing distribution and path together is more informative than one annualised number.

Reading and review

A backtest first needs the question “how many choices were made inside the sample?” Parameters, universe, entry rules and stop thresholds were all tried, so the final curve is a survivor of multiple testing. Read it through out-of-sample performance, net return after turnover and cost, capacity and drawdown tolerance. If it works only in certain years, identify the market structure of those years rather than treating it as a permanent law. Backtesting generates hypotheses; it does not prove the future. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.
