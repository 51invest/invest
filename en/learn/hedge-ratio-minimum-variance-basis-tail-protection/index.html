Hedge Ratio: Minimum Variance, Basis Risk and Tail Protection

A hedge ratio is not '100% or nothing'; it is how much to hedge, with which instrument and how much basis risk to bear. The minimum-variance ratio cuts volatility but may sacrifice return; tail protection needs non-linear tools, not simply more shorts.

Definition and intuition

The hedge ratio (h) is the derivative position relative to the hedged asset. The minimum-variance ratio is h* = Cov(asset, instrument) / Var(instrument), minimising portfolio variance.

If asset and instrument move one-for-one, h*=1; if the instrument is more volatile, h*<1; if they are negatively correlated, h* can be negative (reverse hedge).

Basis risk is the risk that the price gap between asset and instrument changes: futures versus spot, delivery location, credit quality. Tail risk is the risk that linear hedges fail in extreme events; options, tail funds and dynamic hedging suit tails better[1][2].

Formal expression and worked example

Suppose the asset's monthly volatility is 8%, the instrument's 10% and correlation 0.8. h* = 0.8×8%/10% = 0.64, hedging 64% of exposure.

At 100% hedge, basis risk remains: if the instrument rises 10% and the asset 8%, the short loses 10% while the asset gains 8%, a net loss of 2%. At 64%, the net result is closer to zero but basis risk remains.

In a gap, a linear short cannot protect the upside tail; a put option limits downside but costs premium. Choose the ratio by objective: h* to cut volatility, options or tail strategies to protect tails. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Three trade-offs

Trade-off 1: variance versus return. The minimum-variance ratio cuts volatility and may cut expected return; if the asset has positive alpha, over-hedging removes it.

Trade-off 2: linear versus non-linear. Futures, forwards and swaps are linear, cheap but limited tail protection; options are non-linear, protect tails but cost premium.

Trade-off 3: static versus dynamic. A static ratio is set from estimates; a dynamic ratio adjusts with correlation, trades more and carries model risk.

Hedging does not remove risk; it swaps one risk for another: basis, counterparty, liquidity and model risk. Define what you are hedging before choosing tools[3][4].

Conditions, boundaries and common mistakes

Boundary 1: correlations are unstable. In crises, asset-instrument correlation changes and historical h* fails; stress test it.

Boundary 2: instrument liquidity. Options and tail tools can lose liquidity under stress, with wider premia and spreads.

Boundary 3: accounting and margin. Margin, collateral and accounting for hedges affect cash flow; do not look only at economic hedge effectiveness.

Mistake 1: 100% hedging is safest. Full hedging still carries basis and instrument risk; over-hedging can reduce returns.

Mistake 2: hedging is buying insurance. Linear hedges cut volatility but not tails; insurance needs options or non-linear tools.

Deeper note: optimal hedge ratio, basis and tail protection

The hedge ratio is not 1:1. The minimum-variance ratio is the covariance of spot and futures returns divided by the variance of futures returns: h*=Cov(ΔS,ΔF)/Var(ΔF). If they move together perfectly, h*=1; if futures are more volatile, h*<1; if correlation is low, h* is near 0. A 1:1 hedge may under- or over-hedge.

Basis risk is the residual risk. Basis equals spot minus futures; changes in basis create gains or losses even when the price direction is right. Basis depends on storage, transport, interest rates, convenience yield and contract expiry; location, quality and timing differ.

Dynamic hedging is path-dependent. Futures require margin, and adverse moves trigger calls; insufficient cash can force liquidation at the worst point. Plan hedge ratio, margin and liquidity together, not just theoretical variance.

Tail protection conflicts with minimum variance. A minimum-variance hedge cuts everyday volatility but may over-hedge the tail; options preserve upside and pay premium, good for tail protection but costly. The goal decides: lower volatility or floor losses.

Cross hedging uses a related but different asset, with more basis risk; correlations can fail under stress and historical correlations cannot be extrapolated.

Evaluation: post-hedge volatility, maximum drawdown, basis P&L, margin usage and roll cost. Watching only post-hedge return ignores risk and cost.

Common misuses: treating hedging as risk elimination, using historical correlation instead of live basis, ignoring margin and rolls, and over-hedging so the directional exposure flips.

Finally, hedging trades a certain cost for an uncertain loss; it is not free insurance. Ratio, instrument and tenor must match the risk source.

Hedging review checklist

Review a hedge for whether it reduces the intended risk rather than swapping one risk for another:

  1. What is the hedge objective? Lower volatility, lock a price, protect the tail or raise Sharpe; objectives dictate tools and ratios.
  2. Is the minimum-variance ratio stable? Cov/Var estimated on a historical window drifts as basis and correlation change; report estimation error.
  3. How large is basis risk? Futures versus spot, delivery location, credit grade and maturity mismatches create basis; it jumps in stress.
  4. Is tail protection linear? Short futures fail in an extreme rally; options, dynamic hedging and tail funds have different costs.
  5. Are hedging costs included? Margin, roll, option premium and liquidity discounts erode returns; ignoring them overstates protection.
  6. Can you fund liquidity and margin calls? Margin rises in extremes and can force liquidation; stress-test the funding chain.
  7. Does the ratio follow the view? Tactical deviations from h* are allowed if the reason and drawdown threshold are written down.
  8. Are accounting and tax treatments consistent? Instrument classification, P&L recognition and period matching affect reported results.

A good hedge is not 'did you buy insurance' but which risk is covered, at what cost, and whether it still works in extremes.

Finally, the hedge plan should state when to stop or adjust: basis beyond a threshold, correlation reversal, insufficient margin or a changed objective. A hedge without exit rules can become a new risk source in stress.

Minimum action: write a one-pager for each hedge: target risk, instrument, ratio, basis threshold, margin cap and exit condition; stress-test at three times historical volatility.

Record: archive basis and margin data daily so the layer of hedge failure can be identified afterwards.

In one line: hedge exit rules matter as much as entry rules.

Note: archive hedge ratio and basis together; P&L alone cannot support a review.

Label: update stress tests quarterly and include a doubling of margin.

What would change the conclusion

Hedge-ratio conclusions depend on stable basis and correlation:

  1. Basis is non-stationary. If spot-futures structure changes, historical h* drifts and the static ratio fails.
  2. Option volatility is mispriced. If implied volatility is below true tail risk, insurance cost is understated.
  3. Margin is procyclical. If margin rises in stress, hedgers may be forced to liquidate and amplify risk.
  4. Correlation regimes switch. If asset-tool correlation reverses in a crisis, linear hedges fail.

Practical implication: a hedge plan must state target risk, ratio, basis threshold, margin cap and exit condition; without exit rules, a hedge becomes a new risk source in stress.

Data note: compare hedges on total return and all-in cost; price P&L alone misses margin, roll and option premium.

Scope note: compare hedges on the same accounting and tax basis; instrument classification affects P&L recognition and period matching.

Final note: accounting and tax bases change hedge P&L recognition; compare strategies on the same basis or Sharpe ratios are not comparable.

Check: put hedge P&L on the same accounting and tax basis before comparing strategies.

Reading supplement

Hedge ratio depends on the objective. A minimum-variance ratio stabilises portfolio volatility but may sacrifice tail protection; basis and roll cost change the effective ratio; if the goal is extreme-loss protection, options fit better than linear futures. State the objective function, horizon and acceptable residual risk before choosing the ratio.

Suggested answers

  1. h* = Cov(ΔS,ΔF)/Var(ΔF).
  2. h* = 0.8×8%/10% = 0.64 using correlation times the volatility ratio.
  3. Tool up 10%, asset up 8%, a 100% hedge gives about −2% before basis and costs.
  4. Delivery location, quality, timing, storage, transport, rates and convenience yield; any futures-spot difference can shift.
  5. Linear hedges suit everyday volatility; options and non-linear tools suit tail protection while preserving upside.
  6. The model assumes stable volatility and correlation, which change; path, margin and liquidity add risk.
  7. No. A 100% hedge can over-hedge and ignores basis, margin and roll cost; the optimal ratio is usually below 1.
  8. Covariance divided by futures variance; when futures are more volatile or correlation is low, h* is below 1.
  9. Basis changes create P&L even when direction is right; supply-demand, storage and contract expiry drive it.

Chapter takeaways

  • The minimum-variance hedge ratio is covariance over futures variance, usually below 1.
  • Basis risk comes from location, quality, timing and supply-demand; the right direction can still lose.
  • Dynamic hedging needs margin and liquidity buffers to avoid forced liquidation at the worst point.
  • Minimum variance and tail protection are different goals; options cost more but preserve upside.
  • Evaluate post-hedge volatility, drawdown, basis and roll cost, not return alone.
