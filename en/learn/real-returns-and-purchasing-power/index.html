Problem and output

Nominal return grows the account balance; real return grows what you can buy. The general nominal-versus-real distinction is defined in the main article Nominal and Real Values (ECO-012) and is not restated here; this article applies it to investment returns. The question it answers is: given a nominal return, inflation, fees and taxes, how do you calculate the change in purchasing power, and what can the result not tell you? The output is a set of reproducible formulas, one example and a list of limitations.

Inputs and data requirements

  • nominal return and compounding frequency
  • inflation: a price index for the same period and economy, stating whether it is year-over-year, an annual average or cumulative
  • fees: charged on assets, transactions or returns
  • tax rate, tax base and payment timing
  • holding period
  • data source and index definition

Nominal return, inflation and fees must cover the same period. The definition of a price index, its base period and the year-on-year, annual-average or cumulative conventions belong to the main article Price Indices and Inflation (ECO-013) and are not repeated here; this article keeps only one conclusion from it: a price index is built on an average consumption basket [3], not on any individual’s.

Assumptions

The example assumes a starting amount of 100,000 yuan (CNY), a one-year holding period, an 8.0% gross nominal return, a 0.5% annual fee charged on the pre-fee ending assets (108,000 yuan), 20% tax on the nominal gain, and 3.0% inflation; it ignores extra tax on inflation adjustments, deferred accounts and local taxes. Amounts below are in yuan. The return, inflation, tax and fee figures are all teaching assumptions, not data published by any product or any country; actual tax and fee rules vary by jurisdiction and account. The Fisher relation uses expected inflation [1]; an ex post calculation uses realized inflation.

Steps

  1. Pre-fee ending value: 100,000 × 1.08 = 108,000 yuan.
  2. Deduct the fee: 108,000 × 0.5% = 540; the after-fee value is 107,460 yuan.
  3. Taxable nominal gain: 107,460 - 100,000 = 7,460 yuan; tax 7,460 × 20% = 1,492 yuan; after-tax value 105,968 yuan.
  4. Net nominal return: 105,968 / 100,000 - 1 = 5.968%.
  5. Exact Fisher relation: (1 + r_net) = (1 + r_real)(1 + π), so r_real = (1 + r_net) / (1 + π) - 1. The identity, the approximation r_real ≈ r_net - π and the cross term the approximation drops are defined in the main article Interest-Rate Types and Conventions (FIN-012); this article uses those results rather than redefining them. Here 1.05968 / 1.03 - 1 = 2.88% [1].
  6. Check with the approximation: 5.968%-3.0% = 2.97%, about 0.09 percentage point above the exact value.
  7. Purchasing power: 105,968 / 1.03 = 102,881.55 yuan in today’s money.
  8. Tax as a share of the real gain: the real gain is 102,881.55 - 100,000 = 2,881.55 yuan, so 1,492 / 2,881.55 = 51.8%. The nominal tax rate is only 20%, but this denominator is the after-fee, after-tax increase in purchasing power; the same tax is 20.0% of the pre-tax nominal gain of 7,460 yuan and 25.0% of the after-tax nominal gain of 5,968 yuan, so the denominator has to be named.

Without fees and taxes, 1.08 / 1.03 - 1 = 4.85%; the approximation is 5.0%, an overstatement of about 0.15 percentage point. Fees, taxes and inflation together reduce an 8.0% gross nominal return to a 2.88% real return.

For multiple years with constant inflation, a future nominal amount V_t is worth V_t / (1 + π)^t in today’s purchasing power. The general deflation-by-index formula (nominal value ÷ (index ÷ 100)) is set out in the main article Nominal and Real Values (ECO-012); when the index base period is the same as the period you convert back to, the index ratio equals the product of the (1 + π) terms, so the two formulas give the same result. At 3.0% inflation, 100,000 yuan today needs 100,000 × 1.03^10 = 134,391.64 yuan in 10 years to preserve purchasing power; if only 100,000 yuan remains after 10 years, it is worth 100,000 / 1.03^10 = 74,409.39 yuan in today’s money.

Interpreting the result

Real return answers how much purchasing power grew; nominal return answers how much the account balance grew. When taxes are levied on nominal gains, tax paid as a share of the real gain can exceed the nominal tax rate; with high inflation, an investor can earn a nominal gain and still lose purchasing power. The order of fees and taxes in the calculation matters and must be stated. U.S. TIPS adjust principal by CPI and pay interest on the adjusted principal [2], but taxes, index definition and holding period still change the final purchasing power.

The calculation also has limits: it measures purchasing power in terms of the index you chose, not your own cost of living. The gap between an average basket and individual consumption, along with substitution, index weights and coverage, is treated in the main article Price Indices and Inflation (ECO-013) and is not developed here.

Failure conditions

  • subtracting inflation from nominal return before deducting fees and taxes
  • using CPI or HICP as a personal basket without explaining the difference
  • treating an annual inflation rate as cumulative inflation
  • using the approximation with high inflation or high returns
  • ignoring that the tax base is the nominal gain, or ignoring payment timing
  • comparing nominal returns across currencies or horizons
  • treating historical real returns as a guarantee

Verification and reproduction

  1. Recalculate the example with the exact formula and record approximation error.
  2. Shift inflation by 1 percentage point and check the direction and size of the result.
  3. Vary the tax rate, fee basis and holding period in a sensitivity analysis.
  4. Check the index definition, base period and source; confirm the applicable tax rules.
  5. Keep formulas, inputs and dates so another person can reproduce the result.

Working paper: a four-column check for "Real Returns and Purchasing Power"

The subject of this article is "Real Returns and Purchasing Power". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "real return". Column two states the transmission channel of "purchasing power". Column three states the institutional boundary of "inflation". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "purchasing power" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Boundary case: With 6% nominal, 4% inflation and a 1% fee, what real return remains?

At a 6% nominal return and 2.5% inflation, the Fisher real return is 1.06 ÷ 1.025 - 1 = 3.41%. After a 1% fee and a 20% tax on the nominal return, net nominal is about 6% × 0.8 − 1% = 3.8%, and the after-tax real return is about 1.038 ÷ 1.025 − 1 = 1.27%. If inflation rises to 4%, the same 6% nominal return and 1% fee leave about 0.96% real before tax and about -0.19% after tax. The account still shows a 6% gain, but purchasing power barely rises.

The conclusion reverses when tax is levied on real rather than nominal returns: with an inflation-indexed tax base, the after-tax real return is higher; if fees are charged on assets, they drag more when nominal returns are low. The boundary is that real return is a purchasing-power measure, not an account balance. Comparing nominal returns across inflation regimes treats inflation as return.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not investment performance.

Exercises

  1. Fisher relation. Nominal return 6.0%, inflation 2.0%. What are the approximate and exact real returns?
  2. Purchasing power. You hold 100,000 yuan today and inflation runs at 3.0% for 10 years. How much is needed to preserve purchasing power? If only 100,000 yuan remains, what is it worth in today’s money?
  3. Tax and inflation. Nominal return 5.0%, inflation 4.0%, tax rate 25%. What is the after-tax real return, and why can it be negative?
Suggested answers
  1. Approximate 6.0%-2.0% = 4.0%; exact 1.06 / 1.02 - 1 = 3.92%.
  2. 100,000 × 1.03^10 = 134,391.64 yuan; 100,000 yuan is worth 100,000 / 1.03^10 = 74,409.39 yuan in today’s money.
  3. After-tax nominal return 5.0% × (1-25%) = 3.75%; exact real return 1.0375 / 1.04 - 1 = -0.24%. Tax is levied on the nominal gain while inflation exceeds the after-tax nominal return, so purchasing power falls.

  3. Find one rule change in "inflation" and explain how it changes sample coverage.
