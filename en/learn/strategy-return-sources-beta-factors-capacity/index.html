Where Strategy Returns Come From: Beta, Factors and Capacity

Any strategy return can be split into market beta, identifiable factors, genuine alpha and luck. Decomposing the sources shows whether it is replicable and whether capacity and fees will eat it; high return does not equal high skill.

Definition

Return attribution is the process of splitting a strategy’s return into explainable sources. The basic three layers are market beta, style or factor exposures, and residual alpha[1]. Beta measures sensitivity to market moves; factors are systematic exposures such as size, value, profitability, investment and momentum; alpha is what remains unexplained after those exposures.

Intuition

Think of a strategy as a restaurant: beta is foot traffic on the street, factors are local taste, and alpha is the kitchen’s own skill. When traffic is good, every restaurant earns money, which does not prove the chef is skilled; only after removing street and taste effects does skill remain. Capacity is seating: too many guests and service breaks down; hedge-fund capacity and disclosure issues belong here too[3].

Formal expression

Run a multi-factor regression on strategy excess returns:

$$ R_p - R_f = \alpha + \beta (R_m - R_f) + \sum_k \beta_k F_k + \varepsilon $$

where (R_p) is strategy return, (R_f) the risk-free rate, (F_k) factor returns and (\varepsilon) the residual. A significantly positive (\alpha) that is stable across samples and regimes may be skill; an insignificant (\alpha) with high factor exposure is more like “repackaged beta”[2].

When it applies

First, factor definitions and data are consistent; second, the sample covers different market regimes; third, trading costs and fees are deducted; fourth, capacity constraints are included; fifth, the benchmark is reasonable; sixth, multiple testing is corrected. Without one, alpha may be data mining or risk exposure.

Example

Suppose market excess return is 6%, strategy beta 1.2, value exposure 0.3 with factor return 4%, alpha 2%, and risk-free 2%. Expected total return is about (2% + 1.2 \times 6% + 0.3 \times 4% + 2% = 11.2%). Of that, 7.2 points come from market beta, 1.2 from value, and 2 from alpha.

Common mistakes

First, “high return means high alpha”: it may be high beta or small-cap exposure. Second, “a good backtest works live”: capacity, turnover and crowding change returns. Third, “more factors are more accurate”: overfitting and collinearity create fake alpha. Fourth, “alpha is stable”: once copied, it decays.

Deeper note: how capacity, turnover and fees eat alpha

The final layer of attribution is not 'how much alpha' but 'how much survives capacity and fees'. Capacity is the assets a strategy can manage without materially worsening returns; it depends on market liquidity, turnover, holdings concentration and market impact.

A rough capacity estimate: a stock trades 500 million yuan a day, the strategy takes 10% of volume and turns the portfolio 200% a year, so the position supports 500×10%/200% = 25 million yuan; with 40 similar stocks, capacity is about 1 billion. Beyond that, impact and slippage rise quickly.

Market impact is non-linear in trade size: small trades pay the spread, large trades move prices. If the strategy trades 1% of the portfolio a day, impact may be 10 basis points; at 10% a day, it may exceed 100 basis points. High-turnover strategies usually have lower capacity because they pay impact many times a year.

Fees include management, performance, custody, audit and trading costs. With a 2% management fee and 20% performance fee, a 10% gross return leaves investors about 10%−2%−0.2×(10%−2%)=6.4%; at a 6% gross return, about 3.2%, more than half eaten by fees. Fee drag on alpha is non-linear: the lower the return, the higher the share.

Alpha decay comes from competition and crowding. Once a factor is discovered, inflows compress the premium; slow decay with small capacity and low turnover, fast decay with large capacity and high visibility. Attribution should report gross alpha, net alpha, capacity utilisation and fee ratio; otherwise 'high alpha' may be a small-scale illusion[2][3].

Sample bias makes alpha look higher. Backtests often drop delisted stocks, keep only survivors, or report only successful strategies; survivorship bias and backfill bias erase losers' negative returns, so the surviving average is too high. If a fund database lists only operating products, the distribution of historical performance is truncated. Before attributing, ask whether the sample contains accounts, products and strategies that disappeared. Lock the sample definition ex ante; never adjust it just because dropping observations improves the result.

Factor definitions drift. The construction of value, quality and momentum (book-to-market, gross margin, 12-month momentum) differs across data providers; changing the definition changes exposures and alpha. State the factor source, construction method and update frequency; do not treat 'the factor' as a fixed entity.

Attribution frequency must match the appraisal horizon. Daily attribution is noisy; monthly or quarterly is more stable, but lower frequency means fewer observations and weaker significance. Judging a quarterly strategy on three years gives only 12 observations, easily noise. Report frequency, sample length and confidence intervals together[1][2][3].

Attribution must separate gross and net. The same strategy may show 4% gross alpha but only 1% net after management fees, trading costs and impact; investors receive net returns, not the gross number in the attribution table. Fix the reporting convention and update it consistently so results stay comparable across periods. If the convention changes, historical attribution is not directly comparable; recompute all periods on the new basis and disclose the differences.

Exercises

  1. Attribution question: market excess 6%, beta 1.2, alpha 2%, risk-free 2%. What is expected total return, and how much comes from beta?
  2. Capacity question: a strategy has capacity 1 billion and scales to 5 billion, raising turnover cost. How should returns change?
  3. Conditions question: list three conditions for judging whether alpha is real and what evidence each needs.
  4. Fee question: gross return 8%, management fee 2%, performance fee 20%. What is the approximate net return?
  5. Capacity question: daily turnover 400 million, participation 10%, annual turnover 250%. How much can one stock support, and what is capacity with 40 stocks?
