Macro Scenario Analysis

Problem and output

Macro scenario analysis turns a view on growth, inflation and policy into a few internally consistent, reproducible conditional paths, then asks how portfolio or company cash flows and valuations change. Outputs are a scenario table, exposure map, return range, triggers and review date. It is not a forecast or a trading instruction.

Inputs and data requirements

  • Horizon and decision: for example, a 12-month allocation review.
  • Macro variables: real GDP, CPI, unemployment, the policy rate, the 10-year yield, credit spreads, exchange rates and commodity prices, with frequency, units and retrieval date.
  • Starting point: latest actuals, publisher and vintage; label forecasts by source.
  • Exposures: split by region, currency, industry, duration and floating-rate debt; for a company, match revenue and cost currencies.

Assumptions

All figures are teaching assumptions, not forecasts, and are not tied to actual data for any date: a 12-month horizon; 60% global equities and 40% bonds; baseline real GDP +2.5%, CPI +2.5% year over year, policy rate 4.0%, 10-year yield 4.2% and portfolio return +6%; recession real GDP -1.0%, CPI +1.5% year over year, policy rate 2.5%, 10-year yield 3.0% and portfolio return -18%; inflation upside real GDP +0.5%, CPI +5.0% year over year, policy rate 6.0%, 10-year yield 5.2% and portfolio return -10%. This example assigns no probabilities; the expected value appears only as a worked demonstration in the steps. Against this site's published snapshot (dated 2026-10-29): US_INFLATION_POLICY_RATE reads 3.749% per annum for the effective federal funds rate in 2026-09, and US_TREASURY_YIELD_CURVE averages 4.989% for September 2026 on the 10-year Treasury with a latest reading of 4.98%. The baseline's 4.0% sits 0.25 points above the 3.749% reading, while its 4.2% sits about 0.79 points below the September daily average of 4.989% (0.78 points below the latest 4.98% reading); they demonstrate scenario mapping only and must not be read as current market conditions — anchoring the scenario to real starting values means substituting the snapshot readings and rewriting the whole example.

Steps

  1. State the decision and horizon.
  2. Build the baseline from latest actuals and public projections; record publisher, date and assumptions [1][2].
  3. Build two or three alternatives and check consistency: nominal GDP growth should be close to real GDP growth plus the GDP deflator; the policy-rate path should not contradict the growth and inflation story; credit spreads should match recession depth.
  4. Map exposures by region, currency, rate and credit sensitivity; document elasticities.
  5. Calculate first-round impacts, then policy responses, defaults and liquidity; report a range, not a point.
  6. Assign probabilities only with evidence; explain subjectivity and compute expected value under Σp = 1.
  7. Set triggers: CPI 1 percentage point above baseline, a 50 basis point policy move or a 200 basis point spread widening.

Formula: E(r) = Σ p_i × r_i, with Σ p_i = 1. If the probabilities are 50%, 30% and 20% for returns of +6%, -18% and -10%, then E(r) = 0.5×6% + 0.3×(-18%) + 0.2×(-10%) = -4.4%, the weighted value under those subjective probabilities.

Interpreting the result

The value lies in exposure and fragility, not a precise point. The baseline shows what happens if assumptions hold; alternatives show what would flip the result. The Federal Reserve calls its stress-test scenarios hypothetical, not economic forecasts; the IMF calls projection conditions working hypotheses [1][2]. The -18% to +6% range is therefore closer to the method's purpose than -4.4%; a substantial repricing of probabilities can flip the sign. Also ask whether correlations rise and liquidity disappears in stress.

Failure conditions

  • only GDP changes while inflation, policy rates and credit spreads contradict one another
  • the baseline has no retrieval date or vintage, so it cannot be recomputed
  • scenario probabilities are presented as objective frequencies, creating false precision
  • only first-round effects are calculated, ignoring policy responses, defaults and liquidity
  • exposures are split by country of registration rather than by revenue and cost currency, double-counting them
  • only one downside scenario is built and stagflation is missing
  • the scenario output is treated as a forecast or a trade recommendation

Verification

  1. Recompute nominal GDP from real GDP and inflation.
  2. Check the policy-rate path against the growth and inflation narrative.
  3. Compare the direction and size of impacts with historical or out-of-sample episodes.
  4. Vary the elasticities, the probabilities and the starting point one at a time and check whether the conclusion still holds.
  5. Ask another person to reproduce the portfolio return from the input table alone.

Reproduction

The scenario table should list scenario name, horizon, optional probability, GDP, CPI, policy rate, 10-year yield, credit spread, equity and bond returns, portfolio return, assumptions, sources and retrieval date, plus an exposure table and formulas. If no probabilities are assigned, state that no expected value is calculated.

Knowledge-point review

growth and inflation

Definition: Growth affects earnings, jobs and risk appetite, while inflation affects costs, rates and valuation; their combination defines the macro regime.

Mechanism: Strong growth with mild inflation usually helps risk assets, while weak growth with high inflation squeezes margins and valuations and limits policy.

Example: For example, firm demand with moderate wage growth can lift profits, while an energy shock raises costs at the same time.

Boundary: Growth and inflation are not single numbers; leading indicators, base effects, regions and distribution differ, and markets may already price them.

policy and credit

Definition: Policy works through rates, fiscal, regulatory and industrial tools, while credit channels funds to the real economy via loans, bonds and shadow banking.

Mechanism: Easing lowers funding costs and expands credit but can build leverage and asset prices; tightening compresses demand and refinancing.

Example: For example, rate cuts and credit expansion boost property and investment, then tighter regulation makes leveraged firms struggle to refinance.

Boundary: Policy is not one-way stimulus; lags, weak transmission and credit divergence make effects uneven, and credibility and debt limit space.

liquidity

Definition: Liquidity is the ability to sell an asset quickly without a large discount, and also the abundance of funding and market depth.

Mechanism: Market liquidity affects spreads and impact costs, funding liquidity affects refinancing and leverage, and both often deteriorate together under stress.

Example: For example, small-cap shares trade thinly and face wider spreads and deep discounts in a crisis.

Boundary: Liquidity is not constant; central-bank easing can improve funding without rescuing a stock, and the illusion of liquidity vanishes at turning points.

economic and market cycles

Definition: Cycles are expansions and contractions of the economy, earnings, credit and valuation around trend, with different lengths and drivers.

Mechanism: They transmit through inventories, capex, credit and sentiment; assets differ in sensitivity to early, mid and late cycle.

Example: For example, early-cycle rate cuts and easier credit help property and small caps, while late-cycle inflation pressures long-duration assets.

Boundary: Cycles are not regular pendulums; length, policy and structure differ each time, mechanical historical mapping misleads, and markets anticipate or overshoot.

scenarios and market reaction

Definition: Scenario analysis defines plausible combinations of growth, inflation, policy and risk, tracing cash flow and valuation rather than one point forecast.

Mechanism: Scenarios need probabilities, triggers and how much is priced; the same data can produce different reactions under different growth-inflation mixes.

Example: For example, above-expected inflation with weak growth may hurt both stocks and bonds as stagflation risk rises.

Boundary: Scenarios are not forecasts; probabilities are subjective, tails and feedback change paths, and reactions depend on positioning, liquidity and narrative.

Data and institutional check: macro scenarios

Before using "macro scenarios" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns stress testing.

Third, the institutional boundary. scenario assumptions is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: macro scenarios

Learning "macro scenarios" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "stress testing": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "scenario assumptions" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of macro scenarios still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind stress testing reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "macro scenarios" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "stress testing" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Exercises

  1. Concept. Why is a stress-test scenario not a forecast?
  2. Calculation. With probabilities of 50%, 30% and 20% for returns of +6%, -18% and -10%, what is the expected return?
  3. Applicability. A company earns 40% of revenue in Europe and pays costs in dollars. Besides U.S. GDP, which variable matters?
Suggested answers
  1. It is a conditional path under stated assumptions, used to test fragility, and makes no claim about true probabilities.
  2. 0.5×6% + 0.3×(-18%) + 0.2×(-10%) = -4.4%, the weighted value under those subjective probabilities.
  3. The euro/dollar exchange rate and European demand; the rate affects revenue translation and relative competitiveness.
  1. In 100 words, state the core metric in "INV-006", including its denominator and unit.
  2. Write "macro scenarios" and "stress testing" as observable variables and name the time gap between them.
  3. Find one rule change in "scenario assumptions" and explain how it changes sample coverage.
  4. Run a counterfactual with this article's method: change one variable and hold the rest fixed.
  5. If you switched to an independent source, which order of magnitude would move most, and why?
  6. Write one falsifiable claim with an observable signal, a waiting period and a review metric.
