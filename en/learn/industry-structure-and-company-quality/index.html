Industry and Company: Moats and Return on Capital

Industry research asks where the money comes from and why it persists; company research asks whether a firm can turn industry profit into its own return on capital. A moat is not a brand slogan but a structure that stops competition from eroding returns: network, switching cost, scale, patents and location.

Problem

The first step in industry research is not ranking companies but locating the profit pool: in the same value chain, upstream resources, midstream manufacturing and downstream brands can capture very different profits. Company research then asks whether the firm can sustain returns above its cost of capital. Together they answer whether a mediocre firm in a good industry or a good firm in a mediocre industry is more worth studying[1].

Inputs

Industry size, concentration, entry barriers, supplier and customer bargaining power, substitutes, company revenue mix, gross margin, capex, working capital and ROIC. Public sources include 10-K business descriptions, customer and competition sections, industry reports and regulatory filings; company narratives must be cross-checked against actual numbers[2].

Assumptions

First, the industry profit pool is stable over a relevant horizon; second, ROIC represents operating quality but must exclude one-offs and accounting distortions; third, WACC reflects the opportunity cost of comparable risk; fourth, a moat eventually shows up in reinvestment returns, not only in current margins[3].

Steps

Step 1, map the value chain and profit pool: revenue, cost, capital intensity and competition at each stage. Step 2, use the five forces: entry barriers, supplier and customer bargaining, substitutes, rivalry. Step 3, compute ROIC: ROIC=NOPAT/ICROIC = NOPAT / ICROIC=NOPAT/IC, where IC is invested capital. Step 4, compare with WACC: only a positive spread is economic profit. Step 5, check reinvestment: does new capital still earn high returns? Step 6, verify the moat source and duration.

Interpreting results

With ROIC 12% and WACC 8%, the spread is 4 percentage points, meaning about 4 of economic profit per 100 of invested capital. But if high ROIC comes from temporary pricing or low capital intensity rather than a moat, competition will erode it. Check 5–10 year ROIC stability and the marginal return on new capital.

Failure conditions

First, changing industry definitions make profit pools incomparable; second, accounting treatment of R&D, leases or acquisitions differs; third, cyclical peaks inflate ROIC; fourth, the moat is regulatory protection that can change; fifth, leverage boosts ROE rather than ROIC; sixth, reinvestment returns are diluted by acquisitions.

Reproduction

From the 10-K, take revenue, operating profit, tax, invested capital and capex, and compute five-year ROIC on a consistent basis; benchmark gross margin and capital turnover against peers; decompose ROIC into margin, turnover and leverage to see what is changing.

Reading and review

A moat must explain why competition has not eroded returns, not why the company is admirable. ROIC of 12% against an 8% WACC means about 4 of economic profit per 100 of invested capital, but if the high return comes from a cycle peak or temporary shortage, the spread will mean-revert. Judge the moat on four things: whether switching costs are real, whether scale advantage grows with volume, whether the network creates positive feedback, and whether regulatory protection is reversible. Reinvestment return is the final test: if new capital earns below WACC, the existing moat may already be eroding. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Reading checklist

A final moat test is customer behaviour under price increases. A true moat lets the firm raise price without losing much volume; a temporary advantage loses customers quickly. Pair that test with the reinvestment spread and the trend in market share, and distinguish a structural moat from a cycle peak.

Further check

Before applying “Industry and Company: Moats and Return on Capital”, test the weakest link between “Industry structure and profit pools” and “ROIC and cost of capital”. Write down the observable proxy, the direction of the causal link and the condition under which the link reverses. Then ask whether the data are measured at the same frequency and definition as the decision, and whether a cost or lag sits between the signal and the outcome. If the chain cannot be stated without hand-waving, the result is a description, not a usable claim. Run one counterexample and one stress case before extending the conclusion to another market, period or population.

Working paper: four columns for a checkable judgement

The subject of this article is "Industries and Companies: Moats and Capital Returns". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "Industry structure and profit pools". Column two states the transmission channel of "ROIC and cost of capital". Column three states the institutional boundary of "Moats and reinvestment". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "ROIC and cost of capital" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. Moats and reinvestment is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Exercises

  1. Return question: NOPAT 12, invested capital 100, WACC 8%. What are ROIC and the spread?
  2. Structure question: a brand company has high gross margin but low capital turnover; a manufacturer has low margin but high turnover. How do you compare ROIC?
  3. Conditions question: list three conditions for a durable moat and what evidence each needs.
  4. Explain the causal chain between “Industry structure and profit pools” and “ROIC and cost of capital” with the article's framework, and name one observable indicator.
