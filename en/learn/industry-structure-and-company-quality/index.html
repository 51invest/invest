Industry and Company: Moats and Return on Capital

Industry research asks where the money comes from and why it persists; company research asks whether a firm can turn industry profit into its own return on capital. A moat is not a brand slogan but a structure that stops competition from eroding returns: network, switching cost, scale, patents and location.

Problem

The first step in industry research is not ranking companies but locating the profit pool: in the same value chain, upstream resources, midstream manufacturing and downstream brands can capture very different profits. Company research then asks whether the firm can sustain returns above its cost of capital. Together they answer whether a mediocre firm in a good industry or a good firm in a mediocre industry is more worth studying[1].

Inputs

Industry size, concentration, entry barriers, supplier and customer bargaining power, substitutes, company revenue mix, gross margin, capex, working capital and ROIC. Public sources include 10-K business descriptions, customer and competition sections, industry reports and regulatory filings; company narratives must be cross-checked against actual numbers[2].

Assumptions

First, the industry profit pool is stable over a relevant horizon; second, ROIC represents operating quality but must exclude one-offs and accounting distortions; third, WACC reflects the opportunity cost of comparable risk; fourth, a moat eventually shows up in reinvestment returns, not only in current margins[3].

Steps

Step 1, map the value chain and profit pool: revenue, cost, capital intensity and competition at each stage. Step 2, use the five forces: entry barriers, supplier and customer bargaining, substitutes, rivalry. Step 3, compute ROIC: (ROIC = NOPAT / IC), where IC is invested capital. Step 4, compare with WACC: only a positive spread is economic profit. Step 5, check reinvestment: does new capital still earn high returns? Step 6, verify the moat source and duration.

Interpreting results

With ROIC 12% and WACC 8%, the spread is 4 percentage points, meaning about 4 of economic profit per 100 of invested capital. But if high ROIC comes from temporary pricing or low capital intensity rather than a moat, competition will erode it. Check 5–10 year ROIC stability and the marginal return on new capital.

Failure conditions

First, changing industry definitions make profit pools incomparable; second, accounting treatment of R&D, leases or acquisitions differs; third, cyclical peaks inflate ROIC; fourth, the moat is regulatory protection that can change; fifth, leverage boosts ROE rather than ROIC; sixth, reinvestment returns are diluted by acquisitions.

Reproduction

From the 10-K, take revenue, operating profit, tax, invested capital and capex, and compute five-year ROIC on a consistent basis; benchmark gross margin and capital turnover against peers; decompose ROIC into margin, turnover and leverage to see what is changing.

Exercises

  1. Return question: NOPAT 12, invested capital 100, WACC 8%. What are ROIC and the spread?
  2. Structure question: a brand company has high gross margin but low capital turnover; a manufacturer has low margin but high turnover. How do you compare ROIC?
  3. Conditions question: list three conditions for a durable moat and what evidence each needs.
