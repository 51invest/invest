Investment Governance: Policy, Delegation and Rebalancing

An investment policy statement is not a document for regulators; it is a governance loop that writes objectives, risk budget, decision rights, rebalancing triggers and review duties into executable rules.

Problem and scope

Many portfolios fail not because the assets were wrong, but because nobody wrote down who may change the rules. A committee liquidates in a drawdown; a manager adds leverage beyond authority; the rebalancing rule says "review periodically" but no one defines the trigger. An IPS that lists only a return target is a wish list, not a policy.

This article divides investment governance into five links: objectives and constraints, strategic allocation, delegation, rebalancing, and review and amendment. It does not recommend a particular allocation. It makes every allocation answerable: who set it, who executes it, how much drift must be reported, when the rule may change, and who signs the change. A hand-calculable drift example turns a rebalancing band into an action.

Eight clauses of an IPS

First, objectives. A return target should state the benchmark, the horizon and the risk taken: for example, "beat a 60/40 benchmark by one percentage point over a rolling five to seven years, with a maximum drawdown of 15%," not "maximise return." Second, risk budget. Allocate risk by volatility, VaR, stress loss or drawdown budget, not only by capital weight. Third, horizon and liquidity. State which liabilities mature within a year and which can tolerate a ten-year lock; cap illiquid assets. Fourth, constraints. Legal, regulatory, tax, ESG, sector concentration, single-issuer, leverage and derivatives limits. Fifth, strategic allocation. Target weights, ranges, benchmarks and rebalancing rules. Sixth, authority. Who may approve a new asset class, change a range, execute a trade, and what are the single-trade and cumulative limits. Seventh, exceptions and escalation. Who is told when a limit is breached, how quickly a decision is made, and what happens in a conflict or model failure. Eighth, review and amendment. How often, on what evidence, who signs, and how versions are retained.

These clauses are not formalism. Miss one and a stress moment becomes an improvised vote. The cost of improvisation usually exceeds the cost of the original drift.

Roles and authority

A minimum viable structure has five roles: the asset owner (individual, family, foundation or company) sets objectives and risk tolerance; the board or investment committee approves policy and new asset classes and oversees execution; the chief investment officer translates policy into allocation and process; the investment team or trading desk executes; external managers, custodian and auditor provide specialised execution and independent checks.

Authority should be a four-dimensional table of amount, asset, horizon and exception, not a sentence saying "the CIO may adjust allocation." For example: the CIO may adjust within three percentage points of target and up to two percent of the portfolio per trade; a new asset class, leverage above 1.2 times or a single issuer above 10% requires committee approval; a five-point drift is reported within 24 hours and decided within five business days.

Roles also need conflict separation. Manager fees, related-party transactions, soft commissions, personal trading and gifts must be in the governance document. Otherwise authority becomes a rent-seeking space.

Three rebalancing rules

First, calendar rebalancing: return to target on a fixed quarterly or annual date. It is simple and auditable, but drift can be large between dates. Second, threshold rebalancing: trigger when any asset deviates beyond a band. It controls risk but may trade too often in a choppy market. Third, calendar plus threshold: check quarterly and trade only if the band is breached. Most institutions use the hybrid.

A band is not arbitrary. It depends on trading cost, taxes, volatility and risk tolerance. If an asset has 20% annual volatility and a 0.5% round-trip cost, too narrow a band causes frequent trading whose cost eats the rebalancing benefit; too wide a band lets the risk budget fail. A common starting point estimates a no-trade region from cost/volatility, then calibrates with stress tests.

Rebalancing also distinguishes drift from active deviation. Drift is the passive change from market moves; active deviation is a deliberate overweight or underweight. Drift should revert by rule; active deviation needs research records and approval. Dressing an active deviation as drift is a common governance failure.

A numerical example

Start with a CNY 10 million portfolio at 60% equity, 30% bonds and 10% cash. In year one equity rises 15%, bonds 2% and cash is unchanged. Ending values are equity 690, bonds 306 and cash 100, total 1,096; weights are 62.96%, 27.92% and 9.12%. Equity is 2.96 points above target, bonds 2.08 below and cash 0.88 below. With a ±5 point band, no trade is triggered; with ±2, equity is outside and rebalancing is required.

Rebalancing to target: equity should be 1,096×60% = 657.6, so sell 32.4; bonds should be 328.8, so buy 22.8; cash should be 109.6, so buy 9.6. Weights return to 60/30/10. At a 0.3% round-trip cost, the direct cost on 64.8 of trades is about 1,944, or 0.018% of the 10.96 million portfolio. That is the concrete price of the band.

In year two, equity rises another 30%, bonds 3% and cash is unchanged. Equity grows from 657.6 to 854.9, bonds to 338.7 and cash stays 109.6, total 1,303.2; equity weight is 65.6%, 5.6 points above target. Even a ±5 band is breached. Without a trade, the equity risk budget drifts from 60% to 65.6%, raising maximum drawdown and VaR. Rebalancing is not a forecast of a fall; it restores the stated risk budget.

A short governance failure case

Consider a foundation with a 60/30/10 policy and a ±5 point band. In a crisis, equity falls 25%, bonds rise 3% and cash is unchanged. Starting from 600/300/100 and total 1,000, equity becomes 450, bonds 309 and cash 100, total 859. Equity weight is 52.4%, 7.6 points below target; bonds are 36.0%, 6 points above. The band is breached on both sides.

The CIO wants to buy equity immediately, arguing that the policy's strategic target is 60%. The chair wants to wait because "the market may fall further." The policy says nothing about who decides in a crisis. The CIO buys 100 of equity without committee approval, using the "rebalancing" exemption. Two months later equity falls another 10%, the portfolio loses more than the policy's drawdown budget, and the auditor asks why the trade exceeded the single-trade limit.

The failure is not the market call; it is the missing rule. A good policy would have said: a band breach triggers a rebalancing memo within 24 hours; the CIO may trade up to 2% of the portfolio per day; a larger trade needs the chair and one committee member; in a declared crisis, the default is to follow the band unless the committee votes to suspend it, and any suspension expires in 30 days. With those rules, the same market path produces a documented decision instead of an improvisation.

The case also shows why the band is not the whole policy. The band tells you when to act; authority tells you who may act; the exception clause tells you how to depart; the review tells you what was learned. Missing any one turns a risk-control rule into a blame game.

A useful governance scorecard has five columns: rule exists, owner named, limit quantified, exception recorded, and review completed. A policy that scores yes on the first three but no on the last two is a document, not a control. The scorecard should be reviewed annually by an independent party, not by the CIO whose authority it measures.

Delegation is not abdication. The asset owner remains accountable for the policy even when execution is delegated. That accountability needs a reporting line that reaches the owner directly, not only through the CIO, and a whistleblowing channel for staff who see a breach. A governance structure that depends on the person with authority to report themselves is not independent. The test is simple: can a junior staff member escalate a breach without losing their job? That is the difference between a governed portfolio and a personality cult.

Data conventions

Convention one: portfolio return must distinguish time-weighted from money-weighted. Time-weighted measures management; money-weighted measures investor experience. The wrong convention turns rebalancing and cash flow into a false performance story.

Convention two: rebalancing reports drift in points, band and trading cost together. A 64.8 trade at 0.3% round-trip is 1,944, about 1.9 bp of a 10,000 portfolio. Drift without cost overstates the benefit.

Convention three: authority and exceptions need amount, asset, horizon, approver and record. Without all five, the policy cannot be audited; committee delegation does not replace individual responsibility, and exceptions cannot bypass records.

Convention four: the policy needs a version, effective date and revision log. Every change to benchmark, band or authority must state who proposed, who approved, when it took effect and which accounts were affected; without a version, the policy cannot be audited or compared over time.

Boundaries and counterexamples

First, rebalancing is not a return enhancer. In a trending market, frequent rebalancing cuts the winner; in a mean-reverting market, it improves risk-adjusted return. The decision depends on the risk budget and cost, not on a feeling.

Second, tax and trading cost change the optimal band. Capital-gains tax in a taxable account can make rebalancing far more expensive than commissions. Use new cash flow, dividends and redemptions for "natural rebalancing" before selling winners.

Third, illiquid assets cannot be rebalanced daily. Real estate, private equity and infrastructure have lagged valuations; book weights can be misleading. The rule needs valuation frequency, cash flow and secondary-market discounts, not daily marks.

Fourth, governance documents can be bypassed. If exceptions are not recorded, signed and reviewed, the authority table is decoration. Every exception should leave a three-line record: who approved, why, and what happened.

Fifth, committees overreact in crises. The 2008 and 2020 episodes show that ad hoc liquidation in stress often misses the rebound. The policy should state that the default action in a crisis is to follow the existing rule unless a pre-specified extreme condition is met, not to hold an ad hoc vote.

Sixth, benchmarks can be gamed. If the benchmark can be changed whenever allocation changes, performance always "meets" it. Fix the benchmark in the policy; changes require independent approval and disclosure.

Additional boundary one: a policy is not a return forecast. It writes benchmark, horizon, risk budget and authority into executable rules; 'long-term outperformance' cannot replace a risk constraint. 60/30/10 is a starting point, not a promise.

Additional boundary two: rebalancing rules change outcomes. Starting 600/300/100, equity up 15% and bonds up 2% gives 629.6/279.2/91.2: equity 62.96%, drift 2.96 points, inside a ±5 point band. A further 30% equity gain takes equity to 65.6% and triggers.

Additional boundary three: trading cost belongs in the rule. A 64.8 trade at 0.3% round-trip costs 1,944, about 1.9 bp of a 10,000 portfolio. Trading every 2-point drift can eat the rebalancing benefit.

Additional boundary four: governance is not a committee replacing individuals. The authority table needs amount, asset, horizon and exception; the exception process needs trigger, deadline, approver and record. An unrecorded 'temporary decision' voids the policy.

One-page checklist

  • Write objectives as benchmark + horizon + risk, not "maximise return."

  • State the risk-budget measure and cap: volatility, drawdown or stress loss.

  • State target weights, ranges, benchmarks and rebalancing rules.

  • Write authority as amount, asset, horizon and exception, with single-trade and cumulative limits.

  • Write calendar, threshold or hybrid rebalancing, plus cost and tax treatment.

  • Write exception triggers, reporting deadlines, approver and audit trail.

  • Review drift, trading cost, exceptions and benchmark changes quarterly.

  • Label every allocation as a teaching assumption, not investment advice.

  • Keep a version, effective date and revision log so every benchmark, band and authority change is traceable.

Common misreads

Misread one: more frequent rebalancing is better. Every trade costs money: a 64.8 trade at 0.3% round-trip is 1,944. Churning inside a 2-point drift hands the rebalancing benefit to trading costs.

Misread two: a signed policy completes governance. Without an authority table, exception process and records, the policy is only a PDF; governance is who can decide what amount, asset and horizon.

Misread three: the committee can change the benchmark at will. Without a benchmark-change rule, performance always 'meets target'; change needs triggers, a cooling period, independent review and disclosure, not a decision by the evaluated party alone.

Misread four: calendar rebalancing is enough. Calendar rules are simple but can miss a risk window when prices move sharply; threshold rules control drift but may trade more in a choppy market. A hybrid rule must state who can pause it, for how long and on what resume condition.

Exercises

  1. Write an objective as benchmark, horizon and risk, not 'maximise return'.
  2. Start 600/300/100; equity rises 15% and bonds 2%; compute ending weights and drift.
  3. With a ±5 point band, is rebalancing triggered in year one? Explain.
  4. Equity rises another 30% in year two; compute weight, drift and trigger.
  5. Trades total 64.8 with a 0.3% round-trip cost; compute cost and its share of the portfolio.
  6. Design an authority table with amount, asset, horizon and exception.
  7. Write an exception process with trigger, reporting deadline, approver and record.
  8. Compare calendar, threshold and hybrid rebalancing.
  9. How does tax change the optimal band? Give a taxable-account example.
  10. How should real estate and private equity be rebalanced? State valuation frequency and cash-flow rules.
  11. What default action should the policy pre-set to prevent crisis overreaction?
  12. Write a benchmark-change rule that prevents permanent 'outperformance'.
