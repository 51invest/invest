Common Technical Indicators: Calculation and Limits

Recompute SMA, EMA, MACD and the stochastic oscillator from the same hypothetical prices to show parameter effects, lag and conflicting signals.

Problem and output

The inputs are an OHLCV series and indicator parameters; the outputs are indicator values, signals and the conditions under which those signals fail. The aim is to reproduce the arithmetic by hand and judge whether different indicators add independent information, not to find a winning combination.

Inputs and data requirements

Adjusted close, high, low and volume at a fixed frequency, long enough for the parameters; the default MACD setting of 12/26/9 needs at least 34 bars (26 to form the first MACD value, then 9 MACD values to form the signal line, with the first one overlapping). Volume is not an input to the four indicators here, but it can test whether a signal comes with abnormal turnover. The example uses 10 hypothetical trading days: closes 20, 21, 22, 21, 23, 24, 23, 25, 26, 25; highs 20.5, 21.5, 22.5, 21.5, 23.5, 24.5, 23.5, 25.5, 26.5, 25.5; lows 19.5, 20.5, 21.5, 20.5, 22.5, 23.5, 22.5, 24.5, 25.5, 24.5.

Assumptions

Closes are in trading-day order; SMA and EMA use closes; the stochastic window is 5 days; for hand calculation the MACD example uses 3/6/3 rather than the 12/26/9 default; values are rounded to two decimals; EMAs use the first SMA as seed. The EMA weights recent prices more heavily, so it responds to new price changes faster than an SMA of the same length; an SMA's average lag is about (n-1)/2 bars.

The SMA convention used here — the average price over a set period, read as a direction of trend, smoother at longer windows but with larger lag — follows the Fidelity indicator pages [1].

Steps

  1. SMA5: 21.4 on day 5 and 24.6 on day 10.
  2. EMA5: seed with the day-5 SMA of 21.4, α = 2/(5+1) = 0.3333, and iterate to 24.48 on day 10.
  3. MACD (3/6/3): EMA3 = 25.00, EMA6 = 24.17, MACD line = 0.83; the signal line, a 3-day EMA of MACD seeded at the day-6 value of 1.1667, is 0.96.
  4. Stochastic: %K = (25-22.5)/(26.5-22.5)×100 = 62.5; %D is the average of the last three %K values (90.0, 87.5, 62.5), equal to 80.0.
  5. Record the signals: price is above a rising average, but MACD is below its signal line and %K is below %D.

The default 12/26/9 setting (a 12-period EMA minus a 26-period EMA, with a 9-period EMA of MACD as the signal line) and the warning that MACD whipsaws back and forth inside a trading range come from the Fidelity MACD page [2].

Scope note: the stochastic oscillator in this article is computed on the fast convention — %K is not smoothed, and %D is the simple three-day average of %K. The registered source page describes the slow stochastic, which smooths %K once more, so the two must not be conflated. Separately, the statements that "the average lag of an SMA is about (n−1)/2 bars" and that "12/26/9 needs at least 26+9−1=34 bars" are arithmetic consequences of the indicator definitions, like the rest of the formulas here: a methodological derivation, not wording from the registered source.

Interpreting the result

One data set produces mixed signals: the trend is up while momentum is weakening. In the example EMA5 sits below SMA5 because the latest price fell from 26 to 25 and the EMA reacts faster; if prices keep rising, the relationship reverses. Indicators are transformations of price, not new information; MACD and the moving average share the same closes, and the stochastic shares the same highs and lows. 62.5 and 80.0 are positions, not probabilities. The 3/6/3 settings and the 5-day window are chosen for hand calculation; 12/26/9 or 14 days would give different values. When signals conflict, check the window and the data before adding more indicators.

The stochastic oscillator measures where the close sits inside the high-low range of a set number of periods, runs from 0 to 100, is conventionally read against 80 and 20 as overbought and oversold, and is described as most effective in wide ranges or slow trends — the Fidelity stochastic page [3].

Failure conditions

  • data are unadjusted, or too few bars make early values incomparable
  • parameters are optimised repeatedly until the chart looks good
  • different software defaults are treated as the same indicator
  • whipsaws in ranging markets, where MACD crosses back and forth
  • 80 and 20 are treated as objective thresholds
  • indicator values are fed into other indicators without checking definitions and windows
  • costs, slippage and taxes are ignored
  • overlapping windows are used for significance tests, which overstates the sample

Verification

Change windows and parameters and check whether signals survive; measure hit rate and payoff out of sample; compare with a simple benchmark; report the full parameter grid, not only the best result; report signal counts, hit rates and maximum drawdown for both default and alternative settings.

Reproduction

SMA_n = ΣC/n; EMA_t = EMA_{t-1} + α(C_t - EMA_{t-1}), with α = 2/(n+1); MACD = EMA_12 - EMA_26, signal = 9-day EMA of MACD; %K = (C - L_n)/(H_n - L_n) × 100, %D = SMA_3(%K). One window and adjustment must run through the whole chart.

Working paper: a four-column check for "Common Technical Indicators: Calculation and Limits"

The subject of this article is "Common Technical Indicators: Calculation and Limits". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "moving averages". Column two states the transmission channel of "MACD". Column three states the institutional boundary of "stochastic oscillator". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "MACD" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. stochastic oscillator is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Boundary case: The same prices can make SMA and EMA disagree

Closing prices are 100, 102, 101, 104 and 103. The five-day SMA is (100 + 102 + 101 + 104 + 103) ÷ 5 = 102.0. If the sixth price is 106, the SMA becomes (102 + 101 + 104 + 103 + 106) ÷ 5 = 103.2, and price 106 is above the average, a bullish signal. A three-day EMA seeded with the first SMA of 102 uses α = 2 ÷ (3 + 1) = 0.5. The fourth EMA is 0.5 × 104 + 0.5 × 102 = 103.0; the fifth is 0.5 × 103 + 0.5 × 103 = 103.0; the sixth is 0.5 × 106 + 0.5 × 103 = 104.5, also below price. A ten-day window would be smoother but more lagged.

The conclusion reverses when parameters and market regime change: EMA is faster in a trend and noisier in a range; the same rule can flip across windows. The boundary is that technical indicators are price transformations, not causal forecasts. Parameters, lag and conflicting signals must be reported together.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not market data.

Beyond parameter choice, the calculation time and signal confirmation matter. The same average can differ before and after the close; if the rule trades at the next open, slippage and gaps change the result. Reading the average alongside volume and volatility shows whether a signal confirms a trend or amplifies noise; one line alone cannot support a buy or sell conclusion.

Finally, put the indicator back into its institutional and cost constraints: trading rules, price limits and tick sizes all change executability, and a signal without those conditions means little.

Reading and review

Technical indicators transform price; they do not forecast it. A moving average smooths noise but lags; MACD captures momentum through the gap between fast and slow averages but whipsaws in ranges; stochastic oscillators place the close inside a high-low range and change signals with small parameter shifts. Before use, ask under what market structure it works, whether it survives trading cost and whether parameters are stable out of sample. Treat indicators as filters, not signal generators, to control overfitting. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.
