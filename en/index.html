“How does a judgement become an executable portfolio?”

Goals, constraints, valuation, portfolios and risk processes for testable investment decisions.

16 modules 53 articles about 8 hr 21 min total

Station brief

Start from a concrete scene, then set out the questions, methods and boundaries of this station.

Two people like the same company. One will not touch the money for three years; the other needs it for a house deposit next year. Their correct actions may be opposite. Investing starts with goals and constraints, not with a ticker.

Write the goal as an amount, a date and a purpose. Write the constraints as liquidity, maximum tolerable drawdown, tax and legal limits. A return target comes after those conditions; without them it is only a wish.

Valuation gives a price range, the portfolio decides position size, and risk budgeting decides how much loss the plan can survive. Do all three together. Finish only one and the next layer can overturn the conclusion.

A backtest describes how a rule behaved in the past; it does not prove future returns. Out-of-sample tests, trading costs, survivorship and multiple trials are all part of the evidence. Miss one and the backtest becomes a story.

Behaviour has a cost too. Chasing, holding on, overtrading and mistaking luck for skill all eat into returns. A decision log, pre-set failure conditions and regular review are more useful than a forecast for the next quarter.

After this station, you should be able to write an investment policy statement: goals, constraints, benchmark, eligible assets, risk budget and review rules.

After this station, you should be able to answer

  • Why should constraints come before return targets?
  • Why is a cheap valuation not the same as low risk?
  • How can you tell that a strategy has stopped working?

Four common misreadings

These claims come up again and again in reader mail, comments and working discussions. Naming them is more useful than repeating definitions.

“Investing means picking good companies.”

Picking a company is one step in the middle. How much you buy, at what price, how the rest of the portfolio moves, and what you do when you are wrong all change the outcome.

“A low valuation means low risk.”

A low multiple can come from a cyclical peak, governance problems, leverage or accounting quality. Cheap is a starting point; risk depends on whether cash flow arrives and the balance sheet survives.

“A high backtest return makes the strategy usable.”

A backtest has to clear costs, out-of-sample data, survivorship and multiple testing. The better it fits the past, the more you should suspect the parameters were chosen to fit that past.

“Diversification means you cannot lose.”

Diversification reduces idiosyncratic risk; it does not remove the market and recession risk shared by all assets. In a crisis, correlations rise and the benefit weakens.

Two ETFs both called “global technology”

Three or four articles from this station, applied to one decision. Every number is a teaching assumption for practising the order of judgement, not an estimate of a real market.

Two funds share almost the same name. One tracks 50 large caps, with the top ten at 60% of assets; the other tracks 200 stocks including mid-caps, with the top ten at 35%. Over three years the first returned more and the second was less volatile.

The fee gap is 0.15 percentage points. On 100,000 yuan at 7% a year, ten years of that gap is about 1,500 yuan; tracking error, bid-ask spread and turnover costs can be larger, especially when small constituents are illiquid.

Account and tax matter too. In a taxable account, dividend reinvestment is taxed differently; in a retirement account, switching is cheaper and the menu is wider. The same fund can produce different after-tax results in different accounts.

Break “same name” into five items: index construction, fees, tracking error, concentration, and tax/account. Score each, then choose the one that fits your goal and holding period; do not let the word “technology” stand in for the holdings.

Key terms

These words recur throughout the station. Learn the sense used here before moving into the articles.

risk capacity 风险承受能力

The financial ability to absorb loss without derailing the plan, set by income, liabilities and horizon; distinct from willingness.

INV-001 · Writing Investment Goals and Constraints
real return 实际收益

Purchasing-power growth after inflation; a positive nominal return does not guarantee a real gain.

INV-003 · Real Returns and Purchasing Power
benchmark 基准

The reference used to judge a portfolio; it must match the goal, eligible universe and risk profile. An index itself is not directly investable.

INV-004 · Choosing an Investment Benchmark
diversification 分散化

Reduces idiosyncratic risk without removing common risk; correlations rise in crises and the benefit weakens.

INV-017 · Diversification and Common Risk
risk budget 风险预算

Allocates the portfolio’s tolerable risk across assets or strategies by risk contribution rather than capital weight.

INV-018 · Asset Allocation and Risk Budgeting
drawdown 回撤

The fall from a peak to a later trough; state the window and whether fees and distributions are included.
